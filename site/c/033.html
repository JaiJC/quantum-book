Infinite and finite square wells

A vial of tiny crystals, all made of the same material, glows red, orange, green or blue depending only on how big the crystals are. Nothing about their chemistry differs; only their size. The reason is the simplest problem in quantum mechanics, a particle trapped in a box, and it won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

On the plate: the allowed states of a particle in a box. There are only certain energies, and between them, none.

The zero-point energy

There is no state with n=0n = 0n=0: that would be ϕ=0\phi = 0ϕ=0, no particle at all. The lowest energy is E1>0E_1 > 0E1​>0. A confined particle can never sit still, because confining it to LLL forces a momentum spread of order ℏ/L\hbar/Lℏ/L (Chapter 30).

Atoms and molecules are a few tenths of a nanometre to a few nanometres across, which is why their energies are electron-volts and their light is visible or near it.

Walls that are not infinite

Real wells have finite depth. Set the depth to 10 and the width to 1.0 (half-width, in units with ℏ=m=1\hbar = m = 1ℏ=m=1). The plate solves for the bound states exactly.

Colours from size

A quantum dot is a crystal of semiconductor a few nanometres across. An electron and the hole it leaves behind are both confined, and the energy of the light they emit when they recombine is the bulk band gap plus their confinement energies, which grow as 1/R21/R^21/R2. Smaller dots, bluer light. Louis Brus worked out this model in 1983, Alexei Ekimov had seen the effect in coloured glass, and Moungi Bawendi learned in 1993 to make the dots with precise sizes. They shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; quantum dots now set the colours of some television screens and are being developed as bright labels for imaging tissue. Established

NoteThe plate uses Brus’s simple formula, with effective masses for the electron and hole and a correction for their attraction. It gets the trend and the rough colours right; accurate predictions for small dots need more detailed models.

A box made of a molecule

In the late 1940s Hans Kuhn treated the electrons of a long dye molecule as particles in a box the length of its chain. Fill the levels two electrons at a time; the colour is the jump from the highest filled level to the first empty one, ΔE=(N+1) h2/8mL2\Delta E = (N + 1)\,h^2/8mL^2ΔE=(N+1)h2/8mL2 for NNN electrons. Try 10 electrons, with the chain about 0.14 nm longer per electron.

Longer chains, redder absorption. Ten electrons over 1.4 nm predicts about 590 nm, and real dyes of this family absorb in that region. A biomedical engineer meets this in every fluorescent label: the cyanine dyes used in imaging, such as Cy3 and Cy5, differ by a two-carbon extension of the chain, and their colours shift accordingly.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why is there no n = 0 state in a box?tap
It would be ψ = 0 everywhere, meaning no particle. Confinement forces a minimum kinetic energy.
Why do smaller quantum dots emit bluer light?tap
Confinement energy grows as 1/R², raising the energy of the emitted photon.
Can a particle be found inside the wall of a finite well?tap
Yes. The wavefunction decays exponentially into the wall rather than stopping, so there is a nonzero probability there.
