Spin-1/2 and Stern–Gerlach

In Frankfurt in February 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms between the poles of a specially shaped magnet. Each atom is a tiny magnet, and classically its orientation should be random, so the beam should have smeared into a continuous band. On the glass plate where the atoms landed they found two separate marks. Not a smear. Two.

On the plate: the experiment. Silver atoms enter from the left; the magnet sorts each one up or down.

Two spots

The deposit was so thin that it was invisible until Stern breathed on it. He smoked cheap cigars, and the sulphur in his breath turned the silver into black silver sulphide, making the two marks appear. That, at least, is how Stern told it, and Bretislav Friedrich and Dudley Herschbach later reproduced the effect.

Toggle the expectation: or . At the time, the split was read as Bohr’s orbits lining up. The right explanation came in 1925: the electron itself carries an intrinsic angular momentum, spin, with s=12s = \tfrac12s=21​, and the outer electron of silver has no orbital angular momentum to confuse things. Spin-½ has two states, so there are two spots. Established

Spinors and the Pauli matrices

A spin-½ state is a two-component vector, a spinor: ∣ψ⟩=α∣↑⟩+β∣↓⟩|\psi\rangle = \alpha|{\uparrow}\rangle + \beta|{\downarrow}\rangle∣ψ⟩=α∣↑⟩+β∣↓⟩. Its spin operators are S=ℏ2σ\mathbf S = \tfrac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigmaS=2ℏ​σ, with the Pauli matrices of Chapter 8:

A magnet tilted at angle θ\thetaθ from zzz measures n^⋅σ\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigman^⋅σ, whose “up” state is cos⁡θ2∣↑⟩+sin⁡θ2∣↓⟩\cos\tfrac\theta2|{\uparrow}\rangle + \sin\tfrac\theta2|{\downarrow}\ranglecos2θ​∣↑⟩+sin2θ​∣↓⟩. Prepare atoms spin-up along zzz, then send them through a second magnet tilted by 60°. The fraction going up is cos⁡2(θ/2)\cos^2(\theta/2)cos2(θ/2): all of them at 0°, half at 90°, none at 180°.

Two z magnets in a row: everything that passed the first passes the second. Insert an x magnet between them and half of what comes out of the last one is spin-down along z again, although every one of those atoms was spin-up along z before. Measuring SxS_xSx​ erased the SzS_zSz​ information, because the two do not commute. This three-magnet experiment is the shortest demonstration there is that quantum measurement changes the state.

The Bloch sphere

Any spin-½ state, up to an overall phase, can be written cos⁡θ2∣↑⟩+eiϕsin⁡θ2∣↓⟩\cos\tfrac\theta2|{\uparrow}\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2|{\downarrow}\ranglecos2θ​∣↑⟩+eiϕsin2θ​∣↓⟩, so it is a point on a sphere with polar angle θ\thetaθ and azimuth ϕ\phiϕ. Set θ=\theta =θ= 60° and ϕ=\phi =ϕ= 45°.

The arrow points along the direction in which a magnet would find spin-up with certainty, and ⟨σ⟩\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle⟨σ⟩ is exactly that arrow. Opposite points on the sphere are orthogonal states: up and down are 180° apart here, though they are perpendicular in Hilbert space. That halving of angles is the same one you met in Chapter 39. Part Three uses this sphere for every qubit.

Precession, and how MRI works

In a magnetic field B\mathbf BB along zzz, a magnetic moment μ=γS\boldsymbol\mu = \gamma\mathbf Sμ=γS has energy H=−γBSzH = -\gamma B S_zH=−γBSz​. The up and down parts pick up opposite phases, so the Bloch arrow turns around zzz at the Larmor frequency ω=γB\omega = \gamma Bω=γB, without changing its tilt. Ehrenfest’s theorem (Chapter 31) says the average spin obeys the classical gyroscope equation.

For protons, γ/2π=42.577\gamma/2\pi = 42.577γ/2π=42.577 MHz per tesla. Set the scanner field to 3.0 T. An MRI scanner listens for exactly this radio-frequency signal from the hydrogen in water and fat, and uses field gradients to tell which part of the body it came from. Established

Rabi oscillations

To flip a spin, add a small field rotating at frequency ω\omegaω near the Larmor frequency ω0\omega_0ω0​. Seen from a frame rotating with it, the spin precesses about a tilted axis, and the probability of finding it flipped oscillates:

P↓(t)=Ω2Ω2+Δ2 sin⁡2 ⁣(Ω2+Δ22 t)P_\downarrow(t) = \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2 + \Delta^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}}{2}\,t\right)P↓​(t)=Ω2+Δ2Ω2​sin2(2Ω2+Δ2​​t)

Here Ω\OmegaΩ is the strength of the drive and Δ=ω−ω0\Delta = \omega - \omega_0Δ=ω−ω0​ the detuning. Set Δ/Ω=\Delta/\Omega =Δ/Ω= 0.00. On resonance the spin flips completely; off resonance it flips partly and faster. Isidor Rabi used this in 1938 to measure nuclear magnetic moments, and won the 1944 Nobel Prize. MRI pulses, atomic clocks and every gate on a qubit are Rabi rotations.
