Grover's search, amplitude amplification, and its optimality

To find the one marked item among a million, checking items one by one takes half a million looks on average. Lov Grover showed in 1996 that a quantum computer needs about 800. That is a real, provable speedup, and it applies to almost any search problem. It is also only quadratic, not exponential, and on realistic error-corrected hardware, where every quantum step is slow, the crossover point where it actually wins can be very far away.

On the plate: Grover’s algorithm as a rotation in a plane.

Two reflections make a rotation

Start in the uniform superposition ∣s⟩|s\rangle∣s⟩ over NNN items, with one marked item ∣w⟩|w\rangle∣w⟩. Everything happens in the plane spanned by ∣w⟩|w\rangle∣w⟩ and the unmarked states. The oracle flips the sign of ∣w⟩|w\rangle∣w⟩: a reflection. The diffusion step, 2∣s⟩⟨s∣−12|s\rangle\langle s| - \mathbb 12∣s⟩⟨s∣−1, reflects about ∣s⟩|s\rangle∣s⟩. How many rounds

After kkk rounds the success probability is sin⁡2((2k+1)θ)\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)sin2((2k+1)θ), so stop at about π4N\frac\pi4\sqrt N4π​N​. Set log⁡2N=\log_2N =log2​N= 6 and run 6 rounds. Going past the optimum over-rotates and the probability falls again: unlike classical search, more work can make things worse. For N=220N = 2^{20}N=220, 804 rounds give success probability 0.9999998. If the number of marked items is unknown, randomly chosen iteration counts (Boyer, Brassard, Høyer and Tapp, 1998) still find one in O(N/M)O(\sqrt{N/M})O(N/M​) rounds.

No better is possible

Charles Bennett, Ethan Bernstein, Gilles Brassard and Umesh Vazirani proved in 1997, by a “hybrid argument” that tracks how much each query can move the state, that any quantum algorithm needs at least about N\sqrt NN​ queries for unstructured search. Christof Zalka showed in 1999 that Grover’s algorithm is exactly optimal. So quantum computers cannot solve NP-complete problems by brute-force search in polynomial time. The general version, amplitude amplification, boosts the success probability ppp of any quantum subroutine to near 1 in O(1/p)O(1/\sqrt p)O(1/p​) repetitions instead of O(1/p)O(1/p)O(1/p). Established

When does it pay?

A quadratic speedup has to overcome a large constant-factor slowdown. An error-corrected logical operation, including all the error-correction work, takes microseconds or more, against nanoseconds for a classical one, and the oracle itself may need thousands of logical gates. Ryan Babbush and colleagues estimated in 2021 that, for plausible early fault-tolerant hardware, quadratic speedups for optimization problems would need running times of days to break even, and that problems with only quadratic advantage are unlikely to be the first useful applications. Larger-than-quadratic speedups are what matter. Debated, since it depends on future hardware speeds.

Three questions to carry with you.

What happens if you run too many Grover rounds?tap
The state rotates past the target and the success probability drops.
Can Grover’s algorithm solve NP-complete problems efficiently?tap
No; the speedup is only quadratic, and that is provably optimal for unstructured search.
Why may a quadratic speedup not help in practice?tap
Error-corrected quantum steps are far slower than classical ones, so the break-even point is very large.
