Quantum effects in ion channels

A potassium channel in a nerve cell membrane passes about a hundred million potassium ions a second, close to the rate at which ions can diffuse to it, yet it rejects sodium, a smaller ion with the same charge, by a factor of a thousand. Roderick MacKinnon’s 1998 crystal structure of the bacterial channel KcsA, which earned the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, showed how: a narrow selectivity filter lined with oxygen atoms that mimic the water shell of a potassium ion. Some physicists have proposed that quantum coherence helps ions move through it. Most biophysicists doubt it.

On the plate: ions hopping through the filter, and the timescales that matter.

The selectivity filter

The filter is about 12 Å long with four binding sites, each formed by a ring of carbonyl oxygens. A dehydrated K⁺ fits snugly; Na⁺ is too small to be coordinated well, so stripping its water costs more than the filter repays. Molecular dynamics and crystallography show ions moving in single file, alternating with water molecules or, in more recent analyses, as directly adjacent ions: the knock-on mechanism, in which an ion entering one end pushes the row along and one exits the other. Classical simulations with accurate force fields reproduce conduction rates and selectivity reasonably well. Established

Knock-on, classically

Watch the classical knock-on cycle on the plate: ions (amber) and water (white) shuffle through four sites. Each step takes tens of picoseconds; a full transit about 10 nanoseconds, consistent with 10810^8108 ions per second. Now compare a key quantum scale: the thermal de Broglie wavelength of a potassium ion at body temperature, λ=h/2πmkBT\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}λ=h/2πmkB​T​, about 0.016 nm, some twenty times smaller than the 0.3 nm spacing of binding sites. Temperature: 310 K. A wave that short behaves essentially as a classical particle. Established

The quantum proposals and the objections

Alipasha Vaziri and Martin Plenio suggested in 2010 that coherent ion transport might contribute to selectivity and speed, and Johann Summhammer and colleagues proposed in 2012 that quantum interference among ion positions could matter. The objections are quantitative. The ions are heavy and hot, so their wavelengths are tiny; they are surrounded by fluctuating carbonyl groups and water that would decohere any superposition of positions within femtoseconds, while hops take picoseconds. And classical models already explain the data. A positive test would need a measurable isotope or temperature effect inconsistent with classical rate theory. None has been reported. Speculative

The structure and selectivity of the potassium channel filter, the knock-on mechanism, the ion’s thermal wavelength against the site spacing, the coherent-transport proposals, and why they face strong timescale objections.
