Shannon information theory and coding

A human genome is about 3.1 billion letters, each A, C, G or T: at two bits per letter, about 780 megabytes. Specialized compressors squeeze it to well under two bits per letter, because the sequence is not random: some letters and patterns are more common than others. How far can compression go? In 1948 Claude Shannon, at Bell Labs, answered that question for any source of data with a single number, its entropy, and in the same paper found the maximum rate at which information can cross a noisy channel. Quantum information theory is built on his foundations.

On the plate: the measured entropy of a sample of text, a sample of DNA, and random letters.

The compression limit

Shannon’s source coding theorem: a long message of nnn symbols can be compressed to about nHnHnH bits and no fewer without loss. The reason is typical sequences: of all ∣X∣n|X|^n∣X∣n possible messages, almost all the probability falls on about 2nH2^{nH}2nH of them, each with probability near 2−nH2^{-nH}2−nH. Number those and you need only nHnHnH bits. A Huffman code, giving frequent symbols short codewords and rare ones long, comes within one bit per symbol of the limit. The same idea, quantized, is Schumacher compression (Chapter 105), where qubits replace bits.

Mutual information and capacity

How much does knowing YYY tell you about XXX? The mutual information I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y)I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y)I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y). A noisy channel turns inputs into uncertain outputs, and its capacity is the largest mutual information any input distribution can achieve. For the binary symmetric channel, which flips each bit with probability ppp,

C=1−H(p)C = 1 - H(p)C=1−H(p)

Set the flip probability to 0.11. Shannon’s noisy channel coding theorem says that at any rate below CCC, codes exist that make errors as rare as you like; above it, none do. Remarkably, a channel that flips 11% of bits can still carry half a bit per use, reliably. It took until the 1990s, with turbo and LDPC codes, to get close to his limit in practice; your phone does so now. Established

Why this matters for what comes

Quantum information inherits Shannon’s questions and changes the answers. A qubit can hold a continuum of states but yields at most one bit when read (Chapter 96). Quantum channels can carry classical information, quantum information, or private information, each with its own capacity, and entanglement can double some of them (Chapter 103). Quantum entropy (Chapter 105) reduces to Shannon’s for classical states. And in Part Four, the entropy of DNA sequences and of neural spike trains will reappear in biology.
