The crisis in classical physics

By classical physics, every atom in your body should have collapsed about a hundred-billionth of a second after it formed. In April 1900 Lord Kelvin told the Royal Institution that the theory of heat and light was beautiful and nearly complete, apart from two small “clouds” on the horizon. Within five years those clouds had become relativity and quantum mechanics.

Part Zero gave you the classical toolkit. This chapter gathers, in one place, the five places where it fails, and fails badly. Each failure is a clue, and each clue will be solved in the chapters that follow.

Failure one: the glowing oven

Equipartition gives every mode of light in a hot box the same average energy kBTk_BTkB​T, and there are ever more modes at short wavelengths. The predicted brightness rises without limit towards the ultraviolet, the Rayleigh–Jeans law. Real ovens and stars glow with a peak and then fade. Chapter 22 shows how Max Planck fixed it in 1900 by letting energy come only in lumps hfhfhf. Established

Failure two: light that knocks out electrons

Shine light on a clean metal and electrons fly out. A wave carries energy in proportion to its brightness, so brighter light should throw electrons out faster. It does not. Brighter light throws out more electrons, at the same speeds; only changing the colour changes their speed, and below a certain frequency nothing comes out at all, however bright the light. Chapter 23 takes this apart. Established

Failure three: barcodes of light

Heat a gas or pass a spark through it and it glows, but not in a smooth rainbow. It emits a few sharp colours, a different barcode for each element. Hydrogen’s visible lines sit at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm. A classical electron orbiting a nucleus could radiate at any frequency, smeared continuously as it spirals. Nothing in classical physics picks out particular colours. Chapter 24 decodes hydrogen’s barcode. Established

Failure five: cold solids

Equipartition predicts that every solid needs the same heat per mole to warm by one degree, 3R≈24.93R \approx 24.93R≈24.9 J/(mol K): the Dulong–Petit law. Copper and lead obey it at room temperature. Diamond needs only about 6.1. And as any solid is cooled towards absolute zero, its heat capacity drops towards zero too. Classical physics has no way for a vibrating atom to refuse heat. Einstein’s quantized oscillators of Chapter 19 do. Established

What classical physics got right

It would be wrong to throw the old physics away. Conservation of energy and momentum survive untouched. Maxwell’s equations survive. Hamilton’s equations survive as the shape of quantum dynamics. What fails is the assumption that energy can be divided as finely as you like, and that a particle has a definite path. Quantum mechanics replaces those two assumptions and keeps nearly everything else.
