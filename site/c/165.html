Hydrogen tunneling and kinetic isotope effects

Swap a hydrogen atom for deuterium, which has the same chemistry but twice the mass, and a reaction that breaks that bond slows down. The ratio of rates, the kinetic isotope effect (KIE), is one of chemistry’s sharpest tools. Classical theory, corrected for zero-point energy, caps it at about 6–7 at room temperature. Soybean lipoxygenase, an enzyme that oxidizes fatty acids, shows about 80, almost independent of temperature. Engineered mutants reach several hundred. Only tunnelling explains numbers like these.

On the plate: a KIE calculator.

The zero-point limit

A C–H bond vibrates at about 2,900 cm⁻¹; even at absolute zero it keeps half a quantum of vibrational energy, its zero-point energy (Chapter 35). The heavier C–D bond vibrates at a frequency lower by the square root of the reduced-mass ratio, about 2,130 cm⁻¹, so it sits lower in its well. If the bond is fully broken at the transition state, the zero-point difference, about 386 cm⁻¹, adds to deuterium’s barrier:

KIEmax≈exp⁡(hc Δν~ZPEkBT)≈6.4 at 298 K\text{KIE}_\text{max}\approx\exp\Big(\frac{hc\,\Delta\tilde\nu_\text{ZPE}}{k_BT}\Big)\approx 6.4\ \text{at 298 K}KIEmax​≈exp(kB​ThcΔν~ZPE​​)≈6.4 at 298 K

This semiclassical picture also predicts that ln KIE is proportional to 1/T1/T1/T, and that the ratio of Arrhenius prefactors AH/ADA_H/A_DAH​/AD​ is close to 1, and it fixes the Swain–Schaad relation between H, D and T effects: ln⁡(kH/kT)/ln⁡(kD/kT)≈3.3\ln(k_H/k_T)/\ln(k_D/k_T)\approx3.3ln(kH​/kT​)/ln(kD​/kT​)≈3.3. Established

Tunnelling signatures

Set the C–H frequency 2900 cm⁻¹ and a tunnelling contribution 0.00 (0 classical over-the-barrier, 1 pure tunnelling from a well-tuned geometry). The plate shows KIE against temperature. Over-the-barrier, the KIE falls as temperature rises; deep tunnelling from a preorganized geometry gives a large KIE that barely changes with temperature, because the hydrogen’s transfer is not thermally activated; only the protein’s gating motion is. Three experimental signatures point to tunnelling: KIEs well above 7, Arrhenius prefactor ratios far from 1, and temperature-independent KIEs. Soybean lipoxygenase shows all three, with KIE ≈ 80 at room temperature, and mutants that loosen the active site raise it to about 660, as Klinman’s group reported. Established

How to read the numbers

A large KIE alone is not proof: tunnelling also contributes in modest amounts to many reactions with ordinary KIEs. The strongest evidence is a combination: magnitude, temperature dependence, prefactor ratios and Swain–Schaad deviations, interpreted with models that include tunnelling explicitly. R. P. Bell’s The Tunnel Effect in Chemistry remains the classic treatment. The broader lesson for quantum biology: tunnelling is a robust, measurable quantum effect with a clear biochemical role, the best-established non-trivial quantum effect in biology. Established

What you now have

Zero-point energies and the semiclassical KIE limit, Arrhenius prefactor ratios, Swain–Schaad relations, the temperature-independent KIE as a tunnelling signature, and soybean lipoxygenase as the benchmark case.

Why would a tunnelling KIE be temperature-independent?tap
The hydrogen transfer itself is not thermally activated; only the protein’s gating motion is.
