Photosynthesis: light harvesting

A leaf is a light-harvesting machine of astonishing efficiency. Hundreds of chlorophyll molecules act as antennas; when one absorbs a photon, the excitation hops from pigment to pigment until it reaches a reaction centre, where it separates charge. Under low light, nearly every absorbed photon makes it, with a quantum efficiency close to 100%. The machinery that achieves this is quantum chemistry: excited states, dipole couplings, excitons. It is beautiful, well understood, and entirely established. The controversy in the next chapter is about something narrower.

On the plate: energy transfer between two pigments as their distance changes.

Antennas

Purple bacteria use ring-shaped complexes called LH2, whose structure Gerry McDermott and colleagues solved in 1995: two concentric rings of bacteriochlorophylls, nine absorbing at 800 nm (B800) and eighteen tightly packed ones absorbing at 850 nm (B850), held in place by proteins. Green sulfur bacteria use the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex, seven bacteriochlorophylls that funnel energy from a large antenna to the reaction centre. Plants use LHCII trimers. In all of them, the protein scaffold sets each pigment’s position, orientation and energy, tuning a downhill energy landscape toward the reaction centre. Robert Blankenship’s Molecular Mechanisms of Photosynthesis covers them in detail. Established

Förster transfer

Theodor Förster showed in 1948 that an excited donor can pass its energy to an acceptor through the interaction of their transition dipoles, without emitting a photon. The rate falls with the sixth power of distance:

Excitons

When pigments are packed so closely that their coupling JJJ is comparable to or larger than the differences in their energies Δ\DeltaΔ and the disorder from the protein, the excitation no longer hops: it is shared. Two coupled pigments form two exciton states, split by Δ2+4J2\sqrt{\Delta^2 + 4J^2}Δ2+4J2​, each a superposition of excitation on both. Set J=J =J= 100 cm⁻¹ and Δ=\Delta =Δ= 200 cm⁻¹. In LH2’s B850 ring, couplings of a few hundred cm⁻¹ delocalize excitons over several pigments, shifting absorption to the red and speeding transfer. This is ordinary, “trivial” quantum mechanics in Lambert’s sense, and it is essential. Whether long-lived coherent dynamics among these states also matters is the next chapter’s question. Established

Antenna complexes (LH2, FMO, LHCII), Förster transfer and its sixth-power law, FRET as a ruler, exciton states and delocalization, and why near-unity efficiency follows from a tuned energy landscape.
