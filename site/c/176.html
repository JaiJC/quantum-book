Quantum sensing in biology: NV centers in diamond

The previous chapters asked whether biology uses quantum mechanics. This one turns the question around: quantum devices are becoming tools for biology. A nitrogen-vacancy centre in diamond, a single defect a few atoms across, works at room temperature, survives inside living cells, and measures magnetic fields, temperature and electric fields at the nanometre scale. In 2013 researchers used a diamond chip to map the magnetic fields of individual magnetotactic bacteria. These are quantum technologies applied to biology, not quantum effects in biology, and they are firmly established.

Imaging a magnetic bacterium

Magnetotactic bacteria build chains of magnetite nanocrystals, magnetosomes, and swim along Earth’s field. David Le Sage, Ronald Walsworth and colleagues placed bacteria on a diamond with a thin layer of NV centres and imaged the field from each cell in 2013. Thermometry and neurons

The zero-field splitting DDD also depends on temperature, falling about 74 kHz per kelvin, because the lattice expands. Georg Kucsko, Mikhail Lukin and colleagues put nanodiamonds inside human cells in 2013 and measured local temperature to millikelvin precision, even creating temperature gradients with laser-heated gold nanoparticles. In 2016 John Barry, Walsworth and colleagues detected the magnetic signal of single action potentials from a marine worm’s and a squid’s giant axons with an NV ensemble. Mammalian neurons are smaller and their fields weaker, so imaging individual human neuron activity this way remains a goal, not an achievement. For a biomedical engineer, NV magnetometry, optically pumped magnetometers for wearable brain imaging (MEG), and NV-based detection of magnetic labels in diagnostics are the most direct entry points into quantum technology. Established
