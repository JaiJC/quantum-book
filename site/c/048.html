Scattering theory

In 1909 Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired alpha particles at gold foil a few hundred atoms thick. Most went straight through. About one in eight thousand bounced back. Rutherford said it was as if a shell fired at tissue paper had come back and hit you, and from the pattern of the bounces he deduced the atomic nucleus. Everything we know about the inside of the proton, the quarks, the Higgs boson, came the same way: throw things at something and watch how they scatter.

On the plate: alpha particles passing a gold nucleus. The close ones swing round; a very few come back.

When a target vanishes

If the s-wave phase shift is exactly π\piπ, sin⁡2δ0=0\sin^2\delta_0 = 0sin2δ0​=0 and the target becomes nearly invisible. In 1921 Carl Ramsauer and, independently, John Townsend found that electrons of about 1 eV pass through argon and xenon gas almost without scattering, although at lower and higher energies the atoms scatter strongly. No classical picture allows a target to disappear at one energy. The plate plots the s-wave cross section against energy for your well; at certain depths, a deep dip appears. Established

Resonances, and how many neutrinos there are

When a phase shift rises quickly through π/2\pi/2π/2, the cross section peaks. Near a resonance of energy E0E_0E0​ and width Γ\GammaΓ,

the Breit–Wigner shape, and the state lives a time ℏ/Γ\hbar/\Gammaℏ/Γ. The most famous example is the Z boson, measured at CERN’s LEP collider: mass 91.19 GeV, width 2.495 GeV. The width counts every way the Z can decay, including into neutrinos, which leave no trace. Each extra kind of light neutrino would widen it by 0.17 GeV and lower the peak. The optical theorem

Scattering removes particles from the forward beam, and the only way to remove them is by destructive interference between the incoming wave and the scattered wave straight ahead. So the total cross section is fixed by the forward amplitude alone:

σtot=4πk Im f(0)\sigma_{\text{tot}} = \frac{4\pi}{k}\,\mathrm{Im}\,f(0)σtot​=k4π​Imf(0)

A striking consequence: a hard sphere of radius aaa has total cross section 4πa24\pi a^24πa2 at low energy, four times its shadow, and it falls only towards 2πa22\pi a^22πa2, twice its shadow, at high energy. Half of that is diffraction into a narrow forward cone, the shadow itself made of scattered waves. The theorem holds in every theory consistent with conservation of probability, and particle physicists use it to check their data.

What you now have

Cross sections, the scattering amplitude, the Born approximation and Rutherford’s formula, partial waves and phase shifts, the Ramsauer–Townsend effect, Breit–Wigner resonances and the Z boson’s neutrino count, and the optical theorem.
