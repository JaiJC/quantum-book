Magnetoreception in birds (cryptochrome)

European robins migrate at night across thousands of kilometres, and in 1972 Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed they use Earth’s magnetic field to do it. Their compass is strange: it ignores the field’s polarity, responding only to the angle of the field lines to the horizontal; it needs light; and in 2014 researchers at the University of Oldenburg found their robins lost the compass inside the campus, until the huts were shielded from the faint electromagnetic noise of the city. Each of these oddities is exactly what a radical-pair compass predicts. Whether birds really use one is the most important open question in quantum biology.

On the plate: an orientation cage.

The behavioural evidence

Caged migratory birds, restless at migration time, hop in their preferred direction, recorded by scratch marks on paper funnels. The Wiltschkos found that reversing the vertical component of the field, which flips the inclination but not the polarity, reverses their choice: an inclination compass, which cannot tell north from south but can tell poleward from equatorward. A radical-pair compass (Chapter 168) has exactly this symmetry, because the singlet yield depends on the field’s axis, not its sign. The compass also needs short-wavelength light, blue to green, and fails under yellow or red. In 2004 Thorsten Ritz and colleagues showed that weak radio-frequency fields near the electron Larmor frequency disrupt orientation, which no iron-mineral magnet would care about. Established as behaviour.

Cryptochrome

Cryptochromes are blue-light receptor proteins in the retina, containing a flavin (FAD) cofactor. Light excites the flavin, and an electron hops along a chain of four tryptophan residues, creating a flavin–tryptophan radical pair. In 2021 Jingjing Xu, Peter Hore, Mouritsen and colleagues expressed robin cryptochrome 4 and showed in the test tube that its radical pairs are magnetically sensitive, more so than the corresponding protein from non-migratory chickens and pigeons. What is still missing is proof in living birds: that cryptochrome 4 radical pairs form in the right cells, with the right orientation, with long enough spin coherence, and that their signals reach the brain. Hore and Mouritsen’s 2016 review sets out what remains to be shown. Debated, leaning supported.

What you now have

The inclination compass, its light dependence, radio-frequency disruption and its significance, the Oldenburg electrosmog experiment, cryptochrome 4’s flavin–tryptophan radical pairs, and what a proof in live birds would need.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why can’t the bird compass tell north from south?tap
It senses the field’s axis and inclination, not its polarity, as a radical pair would.
Why is RF disruption telling?tap
Weak MHz fields affect electron-spin dynamics but would not disturb a magnetic-mineral sensor.
What has not yet been shown?tap
That cryptochrome radical pairs act as the compass inside a living bird.
