Multivariable calculus and vector fields

A spring bubbles up somewhere in the middle of a pond, but you cannot see the bottom. Can you tell how much water the spring is producing just by standing at the edge of a circle around it and measuring what flows out across the circle?

Yes, and the reason is one of the two great theorems of this chapter. Physics is full of quantities that have a size and a direction at every point: the velocity of water, the electric field, the flow of heat. To reason about them we need calculus that works in more than one dimension.

Divergence: how much comes out

Draw a tiny box anywhere in the flow and ask how much more water leaves it than enters it, per unit area. That number is the divergence. For a field F=(Fx,Fy)\mathbf F = (F_x, F_y)F=(Fx​,Fy​) it is

∇⋅F=∂Fx∂x+∂Fy∂y\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}∇⋅F=∂x∂Fx​​+∂y∂Fy​​

The whirlpool and the river have zero divergence everywhere: whatever flows into a box flows out. The spring has all of its divergence concentrated at the single point where the water appears.

Watching the boundary

Now answer the opening question. Around the spring, draw a circle of radius 1.0 and add up the flow crossing it. The flow weakens as 1/r1/r1/r, but the circle grows as 2πr2\pi r2πr, so the total, the flux, comes out at exactly 2π2\pi2π for every circle.

That is the divergence theorem: the flux out through a closed boundary equals the total divergence inside it.

Stokes’ theorem

Walk once around a loop in the whirlpool and add up how much the flow pushes you along. That total is the circulation. Stokes’ theorem says it equals the total curl inside the loop:

∮∂SF⋅dr=∫S(∇×F)⋅dA\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_S (\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf A∮∂S​F⋅dr=∫S​(∇×F)⋅dA

Gradient: the way uphill

A third operation turns a single number at each point, such as temperature or height, into a field of arrows. The gradient ∇f=(∂f/∂x,∂f/∂y)\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial x, \partial f/\partial y)∇f=(∂f/∂x,∂f/∂y) points in the direction where fff rises fastest, and its length is how fast. What you now have

Gradient, divergence and curl, and two theorems that trade what happens inside a region for what happens on its edge. Maxwell’s equations are written in exactly this language.
