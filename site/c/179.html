Origin of life and quantum arguments

Life’s molecules are handed. Proteins are built from left-handed amino acids, DNA and RNA from right-handed sugars, in every organism on Earth. Ordinary chemistry makes both hands in equal amounts. How did life choose one? Many proposals invoke ordinary chemistry and chance; some invoke quantum effects, from tunnelling in prebiotic reactions to the way electrons’ spins interact with chiral molecules. All are speculative. This chapter asks which ideas are testable.

On the plate: a toy model in which a tiny bias decides which hand wins.

Schrödinger’s question

In What Is Life? (1944), Schrödinger argued that heredity must be carried by an “aperiodic crystal”, a molecule stable enough to store information against thermal noise, and that its stability is a quantum effect: chemical bonds hold because energy levels are discrete. Watson and Crick’s DNA, nine years later, fit his description. This is quantum mechanics in Lambert’s trivial sense, the basis of all chemistry. Questions about the origin of life ask something harder: whether quantum effects selected particular outcomes during prebiotic chemistry. Established for Schrödinger’s point; the rest is open.

Amplifying a small bias

Charles Frank showed in 1953 that a reaction in which each hand catalyses its own formation and inhibits the other amplifies any initial imbalance until one hand takes over; Kenso Soai found a real reaction that does this in 1995. In such systems, an extremely small systematic bias can decide the winner across many independent “pools”. Set the bias to 0.020 (relative to random fluctuations of 0.05) and . With no bias, about half end left-handed; a bias of 0.005 tips about 70% of pools, and 0.02, less than half the size of the early fluctuations, makes nearly all choose the favoured hand. The question is where such a bias could come from. Chiral-induced spin selectivity

In 1999 Ron Naaman and colleagues found that electrons passing through films of chiral molecules come out spin-polarized, the preferred spin set by the molecules’ handedness: chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS). The size of the effect is larger than simple theories predict, and its mechanism is still debated. Turned around, spin-polarized electrons, for example from a magnetized surface, could favour one handedness. In 2023 S. Furkan Ozturk, Dimitar Sasselov and colleagues reported that a prebiotic RNA precursor crystallized on magnetite surfaces formed one enantiomer preferentially, the magnetic surface’s spin polarization supplying the bias, which the crystals then amplified. It is a testable, quantum-mechanical mechanism, but showing it operated on the early Earth is a far harder task. Speculative

What you now have

Schrödinger’s 1944 argument, biological homochirality, autocatalytic amplification of small biases, candidate sources of bias, chiral-induced spin selectivity, and the 2023 magnetite experiment.
