Part Two · Quantum fields and particles

Relativistic quantum mechanics

Try to pin an electron down more and more tightly. In ordinary quantum mechanics this costs momentum, by the uncertainty principle, and nothing worse. But squeeze it into a region much smaller than about 400 femtometres, its Compton wavelength, and the momentum spread carries enough energy to create a new electron and its antiparticle out of nothing. You do not get a better-located electron. You get three particles. Quantum mechanics and special relativity, taken together, will not allow a fixed number of particles. This chapter shows why, and why the answer is quantum field theory.

Start a particle exactly at the origin and ask for its amplitude to be found at distance rrr a time ttt later. For r>tr > tr>t, outside the light cone, it should vanish. It doesn’t: it falls as e−mr2−t2e^{-m\sqrt{r^2 - t^2}}e−mr2−t2​, small but not zero.

The price of confinement

Localize a particle to Δx\Delta xΔx. Its momentum spread is at least 1/(2Δx)1/(2\Delta x)1/(2Δx) in natural units, and its energy spread rises with it. When that energy exceeds 2m2m2m, the particle and its surroundings have enough energy to create a particle–antiparticle pair. Set the confinement to 100 fm for an electron.

On this rough estimate the threshold is crossed near 70 fm, a fraction of the reduced Compton wavelength ℏ/mc=386\hbar/mc = 386ℏ/mc=386 fm; for the proton the same scale is 0.21 fm. The exact number depends on how you define the spread; the scale does not. Below it, “the position of one electron” stops being meaningful, because there need not be just one.

Why fields

Three facts now point the same way. Particle number is not conserved at high energy; colliders make particles by the dozen. Negative-energy solutions cannot be thrown away, because a consistent, causal theory needs them. And causality requires particles and antiparticles to cancel each other’s leaks. A theory with these properties has a field at every point of space, whose excitations are the particles and whose equations are relativistic. Particles become quanta of fields, as photons were already quanta of the electromagnetic field in Chapter 36. The next chapters build such theories, one equation at a time.
