Entanglement and the EPR paradox

In May 1935 Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen published four pages arguing that quantum mechanics is incomplete. Their logic was careful and, step by step, sound. It rested on two assumptions that almost everyone would accept without thinking. Thirty years later John Bell showed that the two together make a prediction that can be tested, and the experiments say one of them is false. This chapter reconstructs the argument exactly, so that you can see which.

On the plate: a source sends pairs of particles to two distant detectors. Each detector flashes at random; together, they agree.

The argument

EPR used positions and momenta; David Bohm’s 1951 version uses spins, and it is cleaner. A source emits two spin-½ particles in the singlet state of Chapter 41, one to Alice and one to Bob, far apart. Whatever axis Alice measures along, Bob will find the opposite result along that axis, with certainty.

Let Alice choose: or . Either way she can predict Bob’s result for that axis without touching his particle. EPR then reasoned: (1) locality, nothing Alice does can change Bob’s particle instantly; (2) reality, if you can predict a value with certainty without disturbing a system, there is an element of reality corresponding to it. Since Alice could have chosen either axis, Bob’s particle must have had definite values for both SzS_zSz​ and SxS_xSx​ all along. Quantum mechanics says no state has both. So, they concluded, quantum mechanics is incomplete. Established, as a statement of the argument.

Bohr’s reply and Schrödinger’s word

Niels Bohr answered within months, in a paper with the same title. He accepted that Alice does not disturb Bob’s particle mechanically, but argued that her choice determines which kinds of prediction about it can meaningfully be made: the experimental arrangement is part of what defines a physical quantity. Many found this obscure, and the debate stalled.

That same year, Erwin Schrödinger gave the phenomenon its name. He called it Verschränkung, entanglement, and called it “not one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics”. He also noticed that Alice, by choosing her measurement, steers Bob’s particle into one set of states or another, a feature now called steering.

Trying to send a message

If Alice’s choice steers Bob’s particle, can she use it to signal? Try. Alice wants to send “0” by measuring along z and “1” by measuring along x. Pick her setting, or , and watch Bob’s results pile up. He always measures along z.

His counts come out fifty-fifty either way. Bob’s particle alone is in the state 1/2\mathbb 1/21/2 whatever Alice does: her z measurement leaves him an equal mixture of up and down, her x measurement an equal mixture of left and right, and Chapter 49 showed those are the same density matrix. Only when Alice phones Bob her results, at light speed or slower, do the correlations appear. This is the no-signalling theorem. Entanglement creates correlations, never messages. Established

Which assumption?

EPR’s two premises together imply that every particle carries predetermined answers to all questions, fixed locally at the source. That is a local hidden-variable theory. It reproduces perfect anti-correlation along any single shared axis easily. The question Bell asked was whether it can reproduce the correlations at different axes. It cannot, and the next chapter derives the inequality and the experiments that settled it.

What you now have

The EPR argument and its two premises, Bohr’s reply, Schrödinger’s entanglement and steering, the Schmidt decomposition, separability and the noisy singlet, and the no-signalling theorem.
