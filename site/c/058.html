Part Two · Quantum fields and particles

Classical field theory

Picture a mattress: a grid of springs, one at every point, each tied to its neighbours. Press one point and a ripple spreads. Now make the springs smaller and closer together, without limit. What is left is a field, a number at every point of space that wobbles and passes its wobble on. Every quantum field in the Standard Model starts as one of these, written down with a single line, its Lagrangian density.

On the plate: a chain of masses joined by springs. Increase the number of masses and the chain becomes a continuous string.

From beads to a field

Take NNN beads of mass MMM spaced aaa apart, joined by springs of stiffness κ\kappaκ. Each bead obeys Newton’s law with forces from its two neighbours. Let a→0a \to 0a→0 with the mass per length μ=M/a\mu = M/aμ=M/a and the tension T=κaT = \kappa aT=κa fixed. The bead displacements qi(t)q_i(t)qi​(t) become a field ϕ(x,t)\phi(x, t)ϕ(x,t), and the difference between neighbours becomes a derivative. The equation of motion becomes the wave equation of Chapter 5, with speed T/μ\sqrt{T/\mu}T/μ​.

Use 16 beads. With few beads the pulse breaks into jitter; with many it travels cleanly as a continuous wave.

Why Lagrangians

Writing equations of motion directly would also work. Lagrangians win for three reasons. Symmetry is visible: if L\mathcal LL is unchanged by a transformation, so is the physics, and Noether’s theorem (next chapter) turns each symmetry into a conservation law. Relativity is automatic: if L\mathcal LL is a Lorentz scalar, the equations are covariant. And quantization starts from it, through canonical momenta (Chapter 60) or the path integral of Chapter 53, where eiSe^{iS}eiS weighs each field configuration.

What you now have

Fields as limits of coupled oscillators, the Lagrangian density and the field Euler–Lagrange equation, the mass term as a spring to the ground, the scalar, Dirac and Maxwell Lagrangians, and why physics is written this way.
