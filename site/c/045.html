Time-dependent perturbation theory and Fermi's golden rule

The Schrödinger equation is perfectly reversible: run it backwards and every state returns to where it started. Yet an excited hydrogen atom in its 2p state decays to the ground state with a steady, irreversible half-life of about a nanosecond, like a radioactive nucleus. Where does a constant rate of decay come from, in a theory with no arrow of time? This chapter derives the answer, Fermi’s golden rule, and then shows where it breaks.

On the plate: the probability that an excited state is still excited, falling away as time passes.

Fermi’s golden rule

Now let the final states form a continuum, with ρ(E)\rho(E)ρ(E) states per unit energy: the many directions and energies a photon could fly off with, for instance. Summing the peak over them, its growing area times the density of states gives a probability that grows steadily, at a constant rate:

Dirac derived it in 1927; Fermi called it a golden rule. The plate tests it honestly. One level is coupled with equal strength to 101 evenly spaced levels, and the full Schrödinger equation is solved exactly. The survival probability, amber, follows e−Γte^{-\Gamma t}e−Γt, white, with Γ\GammaΓ from the golden rule. Then, at t=2πℏ/δt = 2\pi\hbar/\deltat=2πℏ/δ, where δ\deltaδ is the level spacing, the probability comes back. The decay was reversible all along; it only looks permanent when the levels are so dense that the return time is longer than the universe’s age. That is the answer to the opening question. Established

Einstein’s three processes

In 1916 and 1917, before quantum mechanics existed, Einstein showed that atoms in thermal radiation must do three things: absorb light, emit when light stimulates them, and emit spontaneously. Requiring Planck’s law (Chapter 22) fixed the ratio of the spontaneous rate AAA to the stimulated coefficient BBB, A/B=ℏω3/π2c3A/B = \hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3A/B=ℏω3/π2c3. Stimulated emission, which copies the incoming photon exactly, is the principle of the laser.

The golden rule gives AAA directly once light is quantized (Part Five): A=4αω33c2∣⟨f∣r∣i⟩∣2A = \frac{4\alpha\omega^3}{3c^2}|\langle f|\mathbf r|i\rangle|^2A=3c24αω3​∣⟨f∣r∣i⟩∣2. For hydrogen’s 2p state this is 6.27×1086.27\times10^86.27×108 per second, a lifetime of 1.60 ns, which matches measurement.

Which jumps are allowed

Light couples to an atom mainly through its electric dipole, ere\mathbf rer. The matrix element ⟨f∣r∣i⟩\langle f|\mathbf r|i\rangle⟨f∣r∣i⟩ vanishes unless the states have opposite parity and Δℓ=±1\Delta\ell = \pm1Δℓ=±1, Δm=0,±1\Delta m = 0, \pm1Δm=0,±1: the photon carries away one unit of angular momentum. These are the selection rules.

The plate shows hydrogen’s allowed jumps between the first three shells. The 2s state has nowhere to go: 1s has the same parity. So 2s lives about 0.12 seconds, a hundred million times longer than 2p, until it decays by emitting two photons at once. States trapped like this are called metastable, and the hydrogen 1s–2s transition, driven with two photons, is now one of the most precisely measured frequencies in physics.

What you now have

The interaction picture and Dyson series, the sinc² peak, Fermi’s golden rule and why decay looks irreversible, Einstein’s A and B coefficients, lifetimes, and dipole selection rules.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why does the transition probability grow linearly in time?tap
The sinc² peak’s height grows as t² and its width shrinks as 1/t, so its area, summed over a continuum, grows as t.
Is spontaneous decay truly irreversible?tap
Not fundamentally; with discrete levels the probability revives after 2πħ/δ. With a true continuum the return never comes.
Why is hydrogen’s 2s state long-lived?tap
A single-photon dipole jump to 1s is forbidden (no parity change, Δℓ = 0), so it must emit two photons.
