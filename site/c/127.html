Sampling advantage: random circuit sampling and boson sampling

In October 2019 Google announced that its 53-qubit Sycamore processor had, in 200 seconds, sampled from a random quantum circuit, a task it estimated would take the world’s best supercomputer 10,000 years. IBM replied within days that 2.5 days would do. By 2022 a Chinese group had spoofed the result classically in hours. Then the quantum machines grew, and the bar moved again. This is the story of “quantum supremacy”, or quantum advantage: what was claimed, how it is measured, and why the scoreboard keeps changing.

On the plate: what the output of a random circuit looks like, and how to score it.

Porter–Thomas

A random circuit on nnn qubits produces each of the N=2nN = 2^nN=2n bit strings with probability p(x)p(x)p(x). These probabilities are not uniform: they follow the Porter–Thomas distribution, first seen in nuclear physics, in which NpNpNp is exponentially distributed. Some strings are several times likelier than average, most are less likely. A noisy device that outputs uniform random strings gives every string Np=1Np = 1Np=1. The plate compares the two. The speckle-like structure is the fingerprint of quantum interference, and computing which strings are heavy appears to need exponential classical effort. Established

Boson sampling, and a moving scoreboard

Scott Aaronson and Alex Arkhipov proposed in 2011 sending photons through a random linear-optical network: output probabilities are permanents of matrices, which are #P-hard to compute, and approximate sampling is hard unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses, under plausible conjectures about anticoncentration. Jiuzhang, at USTC in China, did a Gaussian version with up to 76 detected photons in 2020.

The classical side keeps answering. Feng Pan, Keyang Chen and Pan Zhang spoofed Sycamore’s 2019 samples in 2022 with tensor-network contraction, exploiting its low fidelity. The quantum side moved: Google’s 105-qubit Willow chip did a harder random circuit sampling task in under five minutes in 2024, estimated at 10²⁵ classical years, and USTC’s Zuchongzhi 3.0 used 83 qubits over 32 cycles in 2025. Random circuit sampling has no known use; its role is to test whether quantum devices do something classical computers cannot. Each claim is a snapshot in a contest. Debated
