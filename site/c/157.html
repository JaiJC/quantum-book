Biochemistry and enzyme kinetics

Orotidine 5′-phosphate decarboxylase, an enzyme in the pathway that makes DNA’s building blocks, speeds its reaction by a factor of about 101710^{17}1017. Without it, the reaction has a half-life of 78 million years; with it, milliseconds. How enzymes achieve such accelerations is one of the central questions of biochemistry, and one place where quantum tunnelling turns out to matter. First, the classical framework every claim must be measured against.

Michaelis–Menten

Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten proposed in 1913 that an enzyme E binds substrate S into a complex ES, which then forms product: E+S⇌ES→E+PE + S \rightleftharpoons ES \to E + PE+S⇌ES→E+P. Assuming the complex reaches a steady state, the rate is

At low substrate the rate rises linearly; at high substrate it saturates, every enzyme busy. Transition-state theory and its limits

Henry Eyring’s 1935 transition-state theory gives the rate of crossing an energy barrier:

Plot ln⁡(k/T)\ln(k/T)ln(k/T) against 1/T1/T1/T: the slope gives ΔH‡\Delta H^\ddaggerΔH‡, the intercept ΔS‡\Delta S^\ddaggerΔS‡. Set ΔH‡=\Delta H^\ddagger =ΔH‡= 60 kJ/mol and ΔS‡=\Delta S^\ddagger =ΔS‡= -50 J/(mol K). Enzymes lower ΔG‡\Delta G^\ddaggerΔG‡ by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate, positioning catalytic groups, and organizing electrostatics, as Alan Fersht’s textbook explains. But transition-state theory treats the reacting atom as a classical particle passing over the barrier. For light particles, especially hydrogen, it can pass through. When rates show weak temperature dependence or huge isotope effects, classical theory is not enough (Chapters 164–165). Established
