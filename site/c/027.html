Part One · Quantum mechanics

Postulates of quantum mechanics

Quantum mechanics can be written on half a page. Six rules say what a state is, what can be measured, what the odds are, what happens to a state when it is measured, how it changes in time, and how to describe several systems at once. Every quantum experiment ever performed, from atomic clocks to quantum computers, fits these six rules.

This chapter states them plainly and tests each on the simplest system there is: the spin of an electron, which can only ever be found “up” or “down” along whatever axis you measure.

Rule one: states are vectors

The state of a system is a unit vector ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩ in a complex Hilbert space (Chapter 9), with vectors differing only by a global phase describing the same state. For a spin it is two-dimensional: ∣ψ⟩=α∣↑⟩+β∣↓⟩|\psi\rangle = \alpha|{\uparrow}\rangle + \beta|{\downarrow}\rangle∣ψ⟩=α∣↑⟩+β∣↓⟩ with ∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1.

Prepare a spin pointing at an angle of 0 ° from the vertical. The plate shows it as an arrow.

Rule two: observables are Hermitian operators

Every measurable quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator, and the only possible results are its eigenvalues (Chapters 8 and 9). For spin along an axis at angle θ\thetaθ in the x–z plane, the operator is cos⁡θ σz+sin⁡θ σx\cos\theta\,\sigma_z + \sin\theta\,\sigma_xcosθσz​+sinθσx​, with eigenvalues +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. Whatever the axis, the answer is always one of two values. Set the measuring axis to 60 °.

Rule four: measurement changes the state

Right after a measurement gives result aaa, the state is the projection of ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩ onto that eigenvector, renormalized. This is the projection rule, written precisely by Gerhart Lüders in 1951. Measure again along the same axis and you get the same answer every time.

Try a chain: measure along the vertical, keep only “up”, then along 90°, keep only “up”, then along the vertical again. A quarter of the spins come out “up” at the end, not all of them, and a quarter come out “down”. The middle measurement erased the first one. . It is the three-polarizer puzzle of Chapter 14, one particle at a time.

Rule five: smooth, reversible evolution

Between measurements, the state changes by a unitary operator, a rotation in Hilbert space that preserves total probability: ∣ψ(t)⟩=U(t)∣ψ(0)⟩|\psi(t)\rangle = U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle∣ψ(t)⟩=U(t)∣ψ(0)⟩, with U=e−iHt/ℏU = e^{-iHt/\hbar}U=e−iHt/ℏ for a Hamiltonian HHH (Chapter 31). For a spin in a magnetic field, this makes the arrow precess steadily, exactly like the protons in an MRI scanner. .

Rule six: combining systems

The state space of two systems together is the tensor product of their spaces (Chapter 9). Two spins live in four dimensions, with basis ∣↑↑⟩,∣↑↓⟩,∣↓↑⟩,∣↓↓⟩|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle, |{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle, |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle, |{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle∣↑↑⟩,∣↑↓⟩,∣↓↑⟩,∣↓↓⟩. Most vectors in that space cannot be written as one spin’s state times the other’s; those are entangled states, the subject of Part Three.

Rules versus meaning

These six rules are the operational core of quantum mechanics, and they are not in dispute: they predict results to as many decimal places as anyone has measured. Established

What the rules mean is another matter. Is the state a physical thing or a summary of knowledge? Does the projection rule describe a real physical change, or only an update? Physicists disagree, sometimes sharply, and Chapter 54 sets out the main positions and the evidence. Debated You can use the rules perfectly well without settling that, and this book will.

Six postulates: states, observables, the Born rule, projection, unitary evolution and tensor products, and the line between rules and interpretation.

Not the rules or predictions, which are established, but their interpretation: what the state and measurement really are.
