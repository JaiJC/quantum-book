Part Five · Convergence

Quantum sensing in biology: NV centers, single-cell magnetometry, MRI advances

Of everything in Part 5, quantum sensing is the part already reaching patients. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures the femtotesla magnetic fields of brain activity; for fifty years it needed superconducting sensors in a helmet of liquid helium, rigid and expensive. In 2018 Elena Boto, Matthew Brookes and colleagues at Nottingham showed a wearable MEG using optically pumped magnetometers, quantum sensors that work at room temperature, recording brain activity while people nodded, drank and played with a ball. For a biomedical engineer, this is the closest bridge from quantum physics to a career.

On the plate: how close a sensor sits to the brain, and why it matters.

Optically pumped magnetometers

Shine circularly polarized laser light through a small glass cell of alkali vapour (rubidium or caesium). The light pumps the atoms’ spins into alignment (optical pumping, a consequence of angular-momentum selection rules, Chapter 41). A magnetic field makes the spins precess, changing how much light passes; measuring the transmitted light measures the field. In the spin-exchange-relaxation-free regime, achieved by heating the cell and operating near zero field, sensitivity reaches a few femtotesla per root hertz. Unlike superconducting sensors (SQUIDs), OPMs need no cryogenics and can sit directly on the scalp. They still need a magnetically shielded room and active field nulling. Established

Standoff

A cortical source lies about 30 mm below the scalp. A SQUID sensor sits behind the dewar wall, about 25 mm off the scalp, and the helmet fits average heads, so children sit farther away still; an OPM sits about 6 mm off. The field of a current source falls roughly as the inverse square of distance at these ranges, so moving the sensor closer raises the signal by the square of the distance ratio, and improves spatial resolution. Boto and colleagues reported OPM signals several times larger than SQUID-MEG, with the subject free to move. Established

Cells, metabolism, and the clinic

NV-diamond sensors (Chapter 176) measure fields from single cells and magnetic nanoparticles, and nanodiamond thermometers measure temperature inside cells. Hyperpolarized carbon-13 MRI (Chapter 177) images metabolism in real time; Sarah Nelson and colleagues first imaged pyruvate-to-lactate conversion in human prostate cancer in 2013. Each faces the same path to the clinic: cost, shielding and robustness outside physics labs, and clinical validation trials showing that patients benefit, which take years. OPM-MEG is now in clinical research for epilepsy and paediatric neurology. For a biomedical engineer, the skills that matter are instrumentation, signal processing, shielding design, and clinical study design. Established technology; clinical adoption emerging.
