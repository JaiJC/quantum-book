Operators, observables, commutators

Measure a particle’s position and then its momentum, or its momentum and then its position: in everyday life the order would not matter. In quantum mechanics it does, and the difference is not vague. It is exactly iℏi\hbariℏ, a fixed number, the same for every particle in the universe.

This chapter is about operators, the objects that represent measurable quantities, and about their commutators, which measure how much order matters. One commutator, [x^,p^]=iℏ[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar[x^,p^​]=iℏ, is the seed of most of what is strange about quantum physics.

Three kinds of operator

Hermitian operators, A^†=A^\hat A^\dagger = \hat AA^†=A^, represent quantities you can measure: real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors. Unitary operators, U^†U^=1\hat U^\dagger\hat U = \mathbb 1U^†U^=1, represent changes that preserve probability: rotations, time evolution, quantum gates. Projectors, P^2=P^\hat P^2 = \hat PP^2=P^, represent yes-or-no questions: is the particle in this region, is the spin up?

Unitary operators are exponentials of Hermitian ones, U^=e−iA^s\hat U = e^{-i\hat A s}U^=e−iA^s, and the Hermitian operator is called the generator of the change (Chapter 20). Time evolution is generated by energy, rotations by angular momentum and translations by momentum.

For a small shift, Taylor’s theorem (Chapter 2) gives ψ(x)−a ψ′(x)\psi(x) - a\,\psi'(x)ψ(x)−aψ′(x), so the generator of translations is a derivative. No finite matrices can do this

Could position and momentum be finite matrices, like spin? Take the trace, the sum of the diagonal entries, of both sides of [X,P]=iℏ1[X, P] = i\hbar\mathbb 1[X,P]=iℏ1. The trace of any commutator is zero, because tr(XP)=tr(PX)\mathrm{tr}(XP) = \mathrm{tr}(PX)tr(XP)=tr(PX). The trace of iℏ1i\hbar\mathbb 1iℏ1 is iℏNi\hbar NiℏN. They cannot be equal, so position and momentum need infinitely many dimensions.

Multiplying exponentials

For numbers, eAeB=eA+Be^Ae^B = e^{A+B}eAeB=eA+B. For operators that do not commute, it fails. When the commutator [A,B][A, B][A,B] commutes with both, which is true for x^\hat xx^ and p^\hat pp^​, there is a clean replacement, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula:

eAeB=eA+B+12[A,B]e^{A}e^{B} = e^{A + B + \frac12[A,B]}eAeB=eA+B+21​[A,B]

It is the extra phase you saw on the plate two passages ago. In general there are more terms, nested commutators, and BCH is how quantum computers estimate the error of chopping a simulation into small time steps (Part Three).

Angular momentum

The components of angular momentum do not commute either:

[L^x,L^y]=iℏ L^zand cyclic[\hat L_x, \hat L_y] = i\hbar\,\hat L_z \quad\text{and cyclic}[L^x​,L^y​]=iℏL^z​and cyclic

So no state can have definite values of two components at once (except zero). That is why a spin measured along zzz has an undetermined xxx-component, as you saw in Chapter 27. But L^2\hat L^2L^2 commutes with every component, so a state can have definite total angular momentum and one definite component. Atomic orbitals are labelled by exactly those two numbers, ℓ\ellℓ and mmm.

What you now have

Hermitian, unitary and projection operators, momentum as the generator of translations, [x^,p^]=iℏ[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar[x^,p^​]=iℏ and the trace argument, BCH, and the angular-momentum algebra.
