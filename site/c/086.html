Accelerator physics

The LEP collider at CERN accelerated electrons to 104.5 GeV in a 27-kilometre ring. Every lap, each electron radiated away 3.4 GeV as X-rays, a thirtieth of its energy, which the accelerating cavities had to replace. The LHC, in the same tunnel, accelerates protons to 6.8 TeV, sixty-five times more energy, and each proton loses only about 6 keV per lap. The difference is a factor of nearly two thousand in mass, raised to the fourth power. That one law decides what the next collider will be.

Steering and focusing

The LHC’s 1,232 superconducting dipoles, at 8.3 T, keep 7 TeV protons on a 2.8 km radius. Quadrupole magnets focus the beam, but a quadrupole that focuses horizontally defocuses vertically. Alternating them, focusing–defocusing–focusing, focuses in both, as a pair of converging and diverging lenses does: strong focusing, invented in 1952. Circular or linear, and what is next

Because synchrotron loss grows as E4E^4E4, electron rings stop being practical somewhere above LEP’s energy; beyond that, electrons go in straight lines, at the cost of one pass per bunch. Protons can circle at far higher energy but collide as composite bags of quarks and gluons, so only part of the energy goes into each collision. Muons, 207 times heavier than electrons, would lose almost nothing, but they decay in 2.2 µs at rest.

The LHC’s Run 3 ended in mid-2026; the High-Luminosity upgrade is being installed, with beams expected around 2030. The 2026 update of the European Strategy for Particle Physics named the Future Circular Collider, a 91 km electron–positron ring operating first as a Higgs factory, as its preferred next machine, with a decision expected around 2028.
