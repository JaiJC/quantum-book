The harmonic oscillator

Cool helium to absolute zero and, at ordinary pressure, it never freezes. Its atoms are too light and too weakly attracted to settle into a crystal, because quantum mechanics will not let them stop moving. Every molecule in your body is the same: even at absolute zero, every chemical bond would still vibrate. This chapter is about the system behind that fact, the quantum harmonic oscillator, which is the most-used model in all of physics.

On the plate: a parabolic valley and the lowest state inside it. It is not at the bottom. It cannot be.

Everything is an oscillator

Near the bottom of any smooth valley, the potential looks like a parabola. An evenly spaced ladder

The levels are equally spaced, ℏω\hbar\omegaℏω apart, unlike the box of Chapter 33 where they spread as n2n^2n2. That is why a vibrating molecule absorbs infrared light at one main frequency, and why light itself, as you will see in the next chapter, comes in identical packets.

The bottom rung is not zero. The lowest energy is 12ℏω\tfrac12\hbar\omega21​ℏω, the zero-point energy. A particle at rest at the bottom would have exact position and exact momentum, which the uncertainty principle forbids. The ground state is the compromise that minimizes total energy: σxσp=ℏ/2\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2σx​σp​=ℏ/2 exactly, the minimum allowed.

Where the classical world comes back

A classical oscillator moves fastest at the centre and slowest at the ends, so it spends most of its time near the turning points. The ground state does the opposite; it is most likely at the centre. Raise the level to 2 and compare with the classical curve in white.

At high nnn the quantum probability oscillates faster and faster around the classical one. Averaged over any small region, the two agree. This is the correspondence principle, made visible.

Reading molecules by their vibrations

Each kind of bond is a spring with its own stiffness and masses, so it absorbs infrared light at its own wavenumber, ν~=12πck/μ\tilde\nu = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{k/\mu}ν~=2πc1​k/μ​, with μ\muμ the reduced mass. Choose a bond: , , , or .

Light atoms on stiff bonds sit high; heavy atoms on soft bonds sit low. This is how infrared spectroscopy identifies molecules. For a biomedical engineer, the most useful line is the amide I band near 1650 cm⁻¹, mostly the C=O stretch of the protein backbone: its exact position shifts with secondary structure, so infrared spectra can tell α-helix from β-sheet in a protein sample.

Heavy hydrogen vibrates less

Replace hydrogen by deuterium, twice as heavy. The bond is the same spring, since the electrons do not care about the nucleus’s mass, but ω=k/μ\omega = \sqrt{k/\mu}ω=k/μ​ falls by about 2\sqrt22​. So D₂’s zero-point energy is 0.193 eV against H₂’s 0.273 eV, and D₂ sits lower in its well, making it harder to break.

For a C–H bond against C–D, the zero-point difference is about 385 cm⁻¹, about 4.6 kJ/mol. If that bond breaks in the slowest step of a reaction, the deuterated version can be up to about six or seven times slower at room temperature. This kinetic isotope effect is a standard tool for working out how enzymes act, and Part Four will show that some enzymes exceed it, a sign that hydrogen may tunnel. Established

When the spring stretches too far

A real bond is not a parabola far from equilibrium; stretch it enough and it breaks. Philip Morse’s potential, V=De(1−e−a(r−re))2V = D_e(1 - e^{-a(r - r_e)})^2V=De​(1−e−a(r−re​))2, captures this and is still exactly solvable. Its levels crowd together as they climb and stop at the dissociation energy. For H₂ the Morse model gives about seventeen bound levels and a bond energy of 4.48 eV from the ground state, which matches experiment; the real molecule has fifteen. Those uneven spacings are why overtones, the jumps by two or more rungs, appear at slightly less than twice the fundamental frequency.
