Quantum key distribution: BB84, E91, security proofs, device independence

Every secure website you use agrees a secret key with mathematics that Shor’s algorithm would break. Quantum key distribution (QKD) offers something different: a key whose secrecy follows from the laws of physics, because measuring an unknown quantum state disturbs it, and the disturbance can be detected. The theory is beautiful and the proofs are rigorous. Real systems have nonetheless been hacked, not through the physics but through the hardware that implements it.

On the plate: you are Eve. Try to read Alice’s key without Bob noticing.

BB84

Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard proposed the first protocol in 1984. Alice sends single photons, each encoding a random bit in a randomly chosen basis: rectilinear (0° or 90°) or diagonal (45° or 135°). Bob measures each in a random basis. Afterwards they announce bases, not bits, over a public channel and keep only the rounds where the bases matched: the sifted key, about half the photons. They sacrifice a sample to estimate the quantum bit error rate (QBER). By no-cloning (Chapter 102), Eve cannot copy the photons; any measurement in the wrong basis randomizes them. Established

Hacking the hardware, and device independence

In 2010 Lars Lydersen and colleagues blinded the single-photon detectors of commercial QKD systems with bright light, turning them into classical detectors that Eve could control remotely. She obtained the full key with no rise in QBER. The proofs assumed devices behaved as modelled; they did not.

Artur Ekert’s 1991 protocol uses entangled pairs and a Bell test (Chapter 51). Its descendant, device-independent QKD, certifies security from the observed CHSH value SSS alone, treating the devices as black boxes. Why does intercept–resend cause 25% errors?tap
Eve picks the wrong basis half the time, and then Bob’s bit is wrong half the time.
What does privacy amplification do?tap
Hashes the corrected key into a shorter one about which Eve has negligible information.
What does device independence remove?tap
Trust in the devices’ inner workings; security follows from the observed Bell violation.
