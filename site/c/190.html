Case study: nitrogenase (FeMoco) and nitrogen fixation

Half the nitrogen atoms in your body passed through a factory. The Haber–Bosch process makes ammonia for fertilizer from nitrogen and hydrogen at about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres, using roughly 1–2% of the world’s energy. Bacteria do the same job at room temperature with an enzyme, nitrogenase, whose active site is a cluster of seven irons, a molybdenum, nine sulfurs and a central carbon: the FeMo cofactor, or FeMoco. How it breaks the triple bond of N₂ is still not fully understood. It is the flagship problem for quantum chemistry on quantum computers.

The biology question and why it is hard

Nitrogenase’s overall reaction is

N2+8H++8e−+16 ATP→2NH3+H2+16 ADP+16 Pi\begin{gathered}\mathrm{N_2 + 8H^+ + 8e^- + 16\,ATP}\\ \to \mathrm{2NH_3 + H_2 + 16\,ADP + 16\,P_i}\end{gathered}N2​+8H++8e−+16ATP→2NH3​+H2​+16ADP+16Pi​​

The cofactor cycles through several states, each adding an electron and a proton, before N₂ binds and is reduced. Which irons bind it, which intermediates form, and why the enzyme makes one H₂ per N₂ remain under study. Answering requires the relative energies of many electronic states of the cluster, whose seven iron atoms each carry several unpaired d electrons coupled together: a textbook strongly correlated system. Density functional theory gives answers that depend on the functional chosen, and the spin coupling is hard for any approximate method (Chapter 159). Established as a hard problem.

From cluster to qubits

Step through the chain: . Reiher and colleagues’ 2017 model used CAS(54e, 54o), 108 spin orbitals; Li and colleagues’ 2019 model used CAS(113e, 76o), 152 spin orbitals (Chapter 185). Lee and colleagues’ 2021 tensor-hypercontraction algorithm needs 2,142 logical qubits and about 5.3×1095.3\times10^95.3×109 Toffolis; on a surface-code machine with 0.1% errors, about four million physical qubits for under four days (Chapter 189). In October 2025 the company Alice & Bob reported, in a preliminary blog post rather than a peer-reviewed paper, that a cat-qubit architecture could reduce the requirement to about 99,000 cat qubits, from an earlier 2.7 million. Established as calculations; Speculative for the 2025 company estimate.

The critique

Three cautions. Classical methods keep improving: DMRG and AFQMC calculations on iron–sulfur clusters, by Garnet Chan’s group and others, have already clarified spin structures once thought out of reach. The active space defines the question, and the original one was too small. And a perfect ground-state energy at one geometry is not a mechanism: understanding nitrogenase needs many states, many geometries, protein environment (QM/MM), and kinetics. A quantum computer could supply the hardest energies in that workflow; it would not replace the workflow. What the answer would change: a better understanding of biological nitrogen fixation could guide catalysts that work under mild conditions, a large energy prize. Speculative until fault-tolerant hardware exists.
