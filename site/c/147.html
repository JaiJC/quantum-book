Photonic and measurement-based computing

Photons are ideal messengers: they travel at the speed of light, barely interact with their surroundings, and keep their quantum state across kilometres of fibre at room temperature. That same aloofness makes them terrible at logic, because a two-qubit gate needs two photons to interact. Every photonic quantum computing scheme is a workaround, and the most powerful one changes what computing means: prepare a large entangled resource first, then compute purely by measuring it.

On the plate: a computation driven only by measurements.

KLM: gates from measurement

Emanuel Knill, Raymond Laflamme and Gerard Milburn showed in 2001 that linear optics (beam splitters, phase shifters), single-photon sources and detectors suffice for universal quantum computing. The interaction is replaced by measurement: the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect (Chapter 43) and post-selection make a gate that succeeds only some of the time, heralded by which detectors click. Raising the success probability needs many extra photons, so KLM in its original form is extremely costly. It proved possibility, and pointed to a better idea. Established

Fusion, loss, and the companies

Large cluster states are built by fusing small entangled photon states with two-photon measurements that succeed 50% of the time without extra resources. Sara Bartolucci and colleagues at PsiQuantum formalized fusion-based quantum computing in 2023, designing codes that tolerate fusion failures and photon loss. Loss is the enemy: fibre loses about 0.2 dB per kilometre, so over 100 km the surviving fraction is 10−0.02L10^{-0.02L}10−0.02L. In January 2025 Xanadu reported Aurora, a modular machine of 35 photonic chips linked by 13 km of fibre, using squeezed light and GKP-type encodings (Chapter 141). PsiQuantum described a manufacturable chipset, Omega, in Nature in 2025; its claims about manufacturing scale come largely from the company. Established for the physics; Debated for the timelines.
