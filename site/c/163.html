Electron transfer and Marcus theory

Every breath you take ends in electron transfer: electrons hop, one at a time, along chains of metal centres and cofactors in mitochondria, tunnelling distances of 10–20 Å through protein. In 1956 Rudolph Marcus derived a rate law for such transfers with a strange prediction: make a reaction more favourable and, past a point, it gets slower. Chemists doubted it for nearly thirty years, until John Miller, Lawrence Calcaterra and Gerhard Closs observed this “inverted region” in 1984. Marcus received the 1992 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Electron tunnelling in proteins is the clearest non-trivial quantum effect in biology.

On the plate: two energy parabolas and the rate they imply.

Deriving the Marcus rate

Electron transfer couples two states: electron on the donor, or on the acceptor. Each has a free-energy parabola in a collective nuclear coordinate, the arrangement of surrounding atoms and solvent. Their minima differ by the driving force ΔG∘\Delta G^\circΔG∘; the energy needed to rearrange the surroundings to the product’s geometry without moving the electron is the reorganization energy λ\lambdaλ. The electron can only jump where the parabolas cross, conserving energy (Franck–Condon principle). Fermi’s golden rule (Chapter 45), averaged thermally over the crossing, gives

The inverted region

Set ΔG∘=\Delta G^\circ =ΔG∘= -0.40 eV and λ=\lambda =λ= 0.80 eV. As the driving force increases, the crossing point descends to the bottom of the reactant parabola: the barrier vanishes at ΔG∘=−λ\Delta G^\circ = -\lambdaΔG∘=−λ, and the rate peaks. Beyond that, the crossing climbs the far side, the barrier returns, and the rate falls again, symmetric about −λ-\lambda−λ in this classical form. Photosynthetic reaction centres exploit this: the forward charge separation sits near the peak, while the wasteful back-reaction, with much larger driving force, lies deep in the inverted region and is slow. Established

Tunnelling through protein
