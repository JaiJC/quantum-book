QMA-completeness and the local Hamiltonian problem

Finding the ground-state energy of a molecule or a material is the central problem of quantum chemistry and condensed-matter physics, and the most promising use of quantum computers. Yet in 1999 Alexei Kitaev proved that a version of this problem is as hard as anything a quantum computer can verify: QMA-complete. In the worst case, even a quantum computer is not expected to find ground energies efficiently. Understanding why, and why it does not doom chemistry, tells you a lot about what hardness means.

It is in QMA: the witness is the ground state itself, and the verifier estimates its energy with phase estimation. What it means for chemistry, and quantum PCP

Norbert Schuch and Frank Verstraete showed in 2009 that computing the exact universal functional of density functional theory is QMA-hard. These are worst-case statements about specially constructed Hamiltonians. Real molecules may be easier: nature finds their ground states by cooling, and good approximate starting states exist for most of chemistry (Part 5). The real question is whether quantum computers help for the molecules we care about, not in the worst case. Debated

A deeper open problem is the quantum PCP conjecture: is it still QMA-hard to estimate the energy to within a constant fraction of the number of terms? A necessary step, the NLTS conjecture (no low-energy trivial states), asserting Hamiltonians whose every low-energy state needs deep circuits, was proved by Anurag Anshu, Nikolas Breuckmann and Chinmay Nirkhe in 2023 using good quantum LDPC codes (Chapter 141). Quantum PCP itself remains open. Debated
