Electroweak theory

In December 1956, at the National Bureau of Standards in Washington, Chien-Shiung Wu and her collaborators cooled radioactive cobalt-60 to a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero, lined up its nuclear spins with a magnetic field, and counted the electrons from its decay. More went one way along the spin than the other. Its mirror image would have the opposite preference. The weak force tells left from right. It was the first of the surprises that led to electroweak theory, which unites the weak force with electromagnetism.

On the plate: electrons from aligned cobalt nuclei, emitted preferentially opposite the spin.

Only left-handed

Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang had asked in 1956 whether anyone had tested mirror symmetry in weak decays; no one had. Wu’s experiment and others showed that the weak force acts only on the left-handed Weyl components of particles (Chapter 73) and right-handed components of antiparticles. A neutrino emitted in beta decay is always left-handed. In the Standard Model, left-handed fermions form doublets of an SU(2) called weak isospin, (νe,e)L(\nu_e, e)_L(νe​,e)L​ and (u,d)L(u, d)_L(u,d)L​, while right-handed ones are singlets that ignore it. Established

Neutral currents, and then the bosons

The theory predicted a new kind of weak interaction with no change of charge, carried by the Z: a neutrino could scatter off an electron and stay a neutrino. In 1973 the Gargamelle bubble chamber at CERN found such events. Ten years later, Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer turned CERN’s SPS into a proton–antiproton collider, and the UA1 and UA2 experiments found the W in January 1983 and the Z that May, at the predicted masses. The ratio ρ=mW2/(mZ2cos⁡2θW)\rho = m_W^2/(m_Z^2\cos^2\theta_W)ρ=mW2​/(mZ2​cos2θW​) equals 1 at tree level, a direct test that the symmetry is broken by a doublet; it is measured to be 1 to a fraction of a percent. Established

The W mass puzzle

Given the Z mass, the Higgs mass, the top-quark mass and α\alphaα, the Standard Model predicts the W mass, with loop corrections: about 80,355 ± 6 MeV. In 2022 the CDF experiment at Fermilab reported 80,433.5 ± 9.4 MeV, seven standard deviations high. Since then ATLAS (80,366.5 ± 15.9) and CMS (2024: 80,360.2 ± 9.9) have measured it with comparable precision and agree with the prediction. The plate shows them together. Most physicists now suspect something in the CDF analysis, but no specific error has been identified. Debated
