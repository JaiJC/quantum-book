Second quantization and orbital choices (active spaces)

A quantum computer cannot hold every orbital of a large molecule, and does not need to. Most electrons sit in deep core orbitals or in well-behaved bonds that simple methods describe well. The hard part is a handful of orbitals where electrons are torn between configurations. Choosing that active space is the most consequential decision in a quantum chemistry calculation. When Markus Reiher and colleagues published the first quantum-computing resource estimate for FeMoco in 2017, later work argued their active space missed important electronic character, and estimates changed accordingly.

Pick an active space

Run an approximate correlated calculation and diagonalize its one-particle density matrix: its eigenvectors are natural orbitals, and their occupations lie between 0 and 2. Orbitals near 2 are essentially full, near 0 essentially empty; those in between carry the correlation. The plate shows occupations for a model transition-metal cluster with 40 orbitals. Choose a threshold ε=\varepsilon =ε= 0.020: orbitals with occupation between ε\varepsilonε and 2−ε2-\varepsilon2−ε become active. The plate reports the active space, the qubits needed (two per orbital), the FCI dimension, and the fraction of the total correlation captured. Smaller thresholds capture more but cost exponentially more. Automated schemes, such as those of Christopher Stein and Reiher based on orbital entanglement, make the choice reproducible. Established

Why the choice matters

An active space that is too small gives a precise answer to the wrong problem. For FeMoco, Reiher and colleagues used 54 electrons in 54 orbitals; Zhendong Li, Garnet Chan and colleagues argued in 2019 that a larger space, 113 electrons in 76 orbitals, with orbitals better suited to the iron–sulfur bonding, was needed to describe the cluster’s low-energy states. Different active spaces led to resource estimates differing by orders of magnitude. Orbital-entanglement measures, single-orbital entropies and mutual information, computed cheaply with DMRG, indicate which orbitals are strongly correlated with which. The lesson for any quantum chemistry claim: ask which active space was used, how it was chosen, and what was left out. Established
