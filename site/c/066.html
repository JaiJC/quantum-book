Quantizing the electromagnetic field

Place two uncharged, perfectly flat metal plates a micrometre apart in a vacuum. Nothing is between them, nothing is on them, and yet they are pushed together. In 1948 Hendrik Casimir predicted the force and its size exactly; in 1997 Steve Lamoreaux measured it to within 5%. The usual story is that the vacuum between the plates holds fewer modes of the electromagnetic field than the vacuum outside. This chapter quantizes that field, and then asks whether the usual story is right.

On the plate: two plates, and the field modes that fit between them.

Two polarizations

A massless particle moving along k\mathbf kk can have its spin only along or against its motion, helicity ±1\pm1±1: a spin-1 particle loses the zero state when it has no mass. Choose or polarization to see the two basis choices. A massive spin-1 particle, like the W and Z, has three states; Chapter 77 shows where the third comes from.

Counting modes between plates

Between perfectly conducting plates a distance ddd apart, the field must vanish at the metal, so only waves with kz=nπ/dk_z = n\pi/dkz​=nπ/d fit, like the box of Chapter 33. Outside, every kzk_zkz​ is allowed. Both regions have infinite zero-point energy (Chapter 60), but the difference between them, carefully computed, is finite. The result is an attractive pressure

Measured

Parallel plates are nearly impossible to align, so Lamoreaux in 1997 used a lens-shaped surface near a flat plate on a torsion pendulum, at separations of 0.6 to 6 µm, and found the predicted force within 5%. Umar Mohideen’s group, with an atomic-force microscope, soon reached 1%. Established

Is it really the vacuum?

Robert Jaffe pointed out in 2005 that the Casimir force can be derived without mentioning zero-point energy at all: it is the relativistic, retarded version of the van der Waals attraction between the fluctuating charges in the two metals. His calculation shows the force depends on the fine-structure constant and vanishes if the plates’ coupling to light is turned off, which a pure vacuum-energy effect would not. So the Casimir effect is consistent with vacuum energy but does not prove it exists. Whether vacuum energy is “real”, independent of the matter it interacts with, is argued over, and it bears directly on the cosmological constant problem of Chapter 60. Debated

What you now have

Gauge freedom and gauge fixing, the photon as a quantum of the electromagnetic field with two polarizations, the Casimir force and its 1/d41/d^41/d4 law, the experiments, and Jaffe’s caution about what it proves.
