The map: which biological problems are genuinely quantum

Part 5 brings the book’s threads together: quantum computers applied to biology and medicine. Every chapter follows one chain of reasoning: the biological question, why classical computers struggle, how the problem becomes a quantum one, the algorithm, the resource cost, the state of the hardware, what the answer would change, the strongest critique, and an honest status. This first chapter draws the map. Only a small part of biology needs a quantum computer, and it pays to know exactly which part.

On the plate: biological problems placed by how quantum they are and how valuable an answer would be. Tap a point.

Needs quantum mechanics, or needs a quantum computer?

All of chemistry needs quantum mechanics. Very little of it needs a quantum computer, because classical approximations, from force fields to density functional theory, work well for most molecules. A problem needs a quantum computer only if three things hold: its answer depends on electrons or nuclei behaving quantum mechanically in a way that classical methods cannot approximate reliably; the system is small enough to fit a realistic quantum machine; and the answer is worth the cost. Three groups emerge. Strongly correlated electronic structure: transition-metal active sites, bond-breaking, excited states with many configurations. Quantum dynamics and open systems: energy and charge transfer, radical-pair spin dynamics, tunnelling. Classical problems with a speculative quantum angle: folding, docking, sequence analysis, machine learning. Established as a framework.

The map

The plate places ten problems. The horizontal axis is how essential quantum simulation is, judged by how badly the best classical methods do; the vertical axis is the value of a better answer, scientific or medical. Colour gives the status of any quantum advantage: amber for credible with fault-tolerant hardware, white for debated, red for speculative. Tap a point to read why it sits there. Protein folding sits far left despite its value, because AlphaFold-style machine learning and classical simulation dominate (Chapter 193). Debated for individual placements.

How to use the map

When you meet a claim that a quantum computer will transform some area of medicine, place the problem. Ask: which step is the quantum step; is that step the bottleneck; and what does the best classical method achieve today? Many published proposals fail the second question: they speed up a part that was never the slow part. The map is not fixed. Better classical methods move points left, as tensor networks and quantum Monte Carlo keep doing; better hardware and algorithms move the cost line. The remaining chapters examine each region in turn. Established as method.
