Simon's algorithm and the hidden subgroup problem

In 1994 Daniel Simon, then at the Université de Montréal, found a problem that a quantum computer solves with about nnn queries and any classical computer, even one allowed to guess, needs about 2n/22^{n/2}2n/2. It was the first exponential separation against randomized classical algorithms, and its structure, a hidden period revealed by Fourier sampling, was exactly what Peter Shor needed. Weeks after reading Simon’s paper, Shor had his factoring algorithm.

On the plate: equations collected from quantum queries, solved by Gaussian elimination.

The problem

A function fff on nnn-bit strings is promised to satisfy f(x)=f(y)f(x) = f(y)f(x)=f(y) if and only if y=xy = xy=x or y=x⊕sy = x\oplus sy=x⊕s, for some unknown nonzero sss. Find sss. Classically you must find a collision, two inputs with the same output, and by the birthday paradox that takes about 2n/22^{n/2}2n/2 queries. Simon proved no classical algorithm does fundamentally better. Established

The quantum algorithm

Prepare the uniform superposition, compute fff into a second register, and measure that register. The first register collapses to (∣x0⟩+∣x0⊕s⟩)/2(|x_0\rangle + |x_0\oplus s\rangle)/\sqrt2(∣x0​⟩+∣x0​⊕s⟩)/2​ for a random x0x_0x0​. The hidden subgroup problem

Simon’s, Deutsch–Jozsa’s and Shor’s problems are all instances of one pattern: a function is constant on the cosets of a hidden subgroup HHH of a group GGG and different on different cosets; find HHH. For Simon, GGG is bit strings under XOR and H={0,s}H = \{0, s\}H={0,s}. For Shor, GGG is the integers and HHH the multiples of a period. Whenever GGG is abelian, the Fourier transform over GGG solves the problem efficiently, as Alexei Kitaev showed in 1995. Established

Where it gets hard

For non-abelian groups the story changes. The symmetric group’s HSP would solve graph isomorphism; the dihedral group’s HSP is closely related, as Oded Regev showed in 2002, to finding short vectors in lattices, the problem behind post-quantum cryptography (Chapter 133). Greg Kuperberg found a subexponential quantum algorithm for the dihedral case in 2003, but no polynomial one is known. That gap is part of why lattice cryptography is trusted to resist quantum computers. Debated whether efficient algorithms exist.

What you now have

Simon’s problem and its 2n/22^{n/2}2n/2 classical lower bound, the quantum algorithm and GF(2) elimination, the hidden subgroup framework that unites Simon and Shor, and why non-abelian cases connect to lattice cryptography.
