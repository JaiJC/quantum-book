The uncertainty principle

The uncertainty principle is often explained as clumsiness: to see an electron you must hit it with light, and the light kicks it. That story is not wrong, but it misses the point. The real statement is stronger and stranger. A particle with both a sharp position and a sharp momentum does not exist, before anyone measures anything.

It follows in a few lines from the commutator [x^,p^]=iℏ[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar[x^,p^​]=iℏ of Chapter 29, and from the Fourier trade-off of Chapter 6. The plate shows a wavefunction in position on the left and the same state in momentum on the right, with their spreads.

What uncertainty is, and is not

The Robertson relation is about preparation: prepare many particles in the same state, measure position on some and momentum on others, and the two spreads obey the bound. No single measurement disturbs anything in that statement.

A separate question is how much measuring xxx on one particle disturbs its ppp. Heisenberg’s original microscope argument suggested the product of measurement error and disturbance obeys the same bound. In 2003 Masanao Ozawa argued it need not, and proposed a corrected relation. In 2012 Jacqueline Erhart and colleagues measured neutron spins and found products below Heisenberg’s naive bound, consistent with Ozawa’s. Others, notably Paul Busch, Pekka Lahti and Reinhard Werner, define error and disturbance differently and prove a Heisenberg-like bound does hold. Which definitions are the right ones is still argued. Debated

Why atoms do not collapse

Here is the answer to the crisis of Chapter 16, in rough form. Squeeze an electron into a region of size rrr around a proton and its momentum spread is at least about ℏ/r\hbar/rℏ/r, costing kinetic energy ℏ2/2mr2\hbar^2/2m r^2ℏ2/2mr2. The electrical energy is −e2/4πε0r-e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r−e2/4πε0​r. Shrinking rrr lowers the electrical energy but raises the kinetic energy faster. Set the size to 2.00 Bohr radii and watch the total.

The minimum sits at exactly one Bohr radius, with energy −13.6-13.6−13.6 eV. The estimate is rough, but here it lands on the exact answer. Matter is stable because confinement costs energy.

Energy and time

There is also a relation ΔE Δt≳ℏ/2\Delta E\,\Delta t \gtrsim \hbar/2ΔEΔt≳ℏ/2, but it is a different kind of statement: time is not an operator in quantum mechanics, so Robertson’s proof does not apply. The precise version, due to Leonid Mandelstam and Igor Tamm in 1945, says Δt\Delta tΔt is how long it takes some observable to change by about one of its own spreads. A state with a sharp energy changes slowly.

In practice: an excited atom that lives for a time τ\tauτ emits light with a frequency spread of about 1/2πτ1/2\pi\tau1/2πτ. The yellow sodium line comes from a state living 16 ns, giving a natural width of about 10 MHz. Established
