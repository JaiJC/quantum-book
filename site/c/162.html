Quantum effects at room temperature: what survives

A warm, wet cell destroys most quantum superpositions within femtoseconds: a molecule is struck by its neighbours some 101310^{13}1013 times a second. Erwin Schrödinger, in his 1944 lectures What Is Life?, already wondered how ordered quantum structure could persist in that chaos. The modern answer is that two kinds of quantum effect plausibly survive: tunnelling, which needs no long-lived coherence, and electron-spin coherence, which is weakly coupled to its surroundings. Most others do not.

On the plate: a survival map of quantum effects in biology.

Compare the clocks

A quantum effect can do biological work only if it acts faster than the environment destroys it. Electronic coherence in a protein at 300 K typically decays within tens to hundreds of femtoseconds; vibrational coherence within picoseconds; electron spins, coupled to their surroundings only through weak magnetic interactions, can stay coherent for microseconds. The plate places each candidate effect by its decoherence time and by how long coherence must last for the effect to work. Above the diagonal, coherence is destroyed before the job is done; below it, the effect can survive. Tunnelling sits low because a particle crosses a barrier in femtoseconds or less. Established as a framework.

Why tunnelling is robust

Tunnelling through a barrier does not require a superposition to last; it is a transition probability, like a reaction rate. Why spin coherence can survive

An electron spin’s energy in Earth’s 50 µT field is about 2×10−7kBT2\times10^{-7}k_BT2×10−7kB​T. That sounds hopeless, but it cuts both ways: the spin is so weakly coupled to its surroundings that molecular collisions barely disturb it. Two electron spins created together in a radical pair can stay correlated for microseconds, long enough for a tiny magnetic field to steer a chemical outcome. This is the basis of the radical-pair mechanism (Chapter 168) and the leading theory of bird magnetoreception (Chapter 169). By contrast, the claims that long-lived electronic coherence drives photosynthesis (Chapter 167) or that coherence underlies cognition (Chapter 174) run against these clocks. Lambert and colleagues (2013) and Jianshu Cao and colleagues (2020) weigh the evidence. Established for the timescales; individual claims are labelled in their chapters.

What you now have

A way to compare decoherence and functional timescales, why tunnelling survives warmth while long-lived electronic coherence usually does not, the strong mass dependence of tunnelling, and why weakly coupled spin coherence can survive in cells.
