Part Two · Quantum fields and particles

Noether's theorem

Why is energy conserved? Not because someone measured it carefully, though they did. Energy is conserved because the laws of physics are the same today as they will be tomorrow. Momentum is conserved because they are the same here as over there; angular momentum, because they don’t care which way you face; electric charge, because the phase of a quantum field can be turned without anyone noticing. In 1918 Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of this kind comes with a conserved quantity, and that the connection runs both ways.

On the plate: a particle orbiting in a perfectly round bowl. Its angular momentum never changes.

Conservation of electric charge is this symmetry. In Chapter 74, demanding that the phase may be turned differently at each point will force the photon to exist.

When energy is not conserved

The theorem works both ways: no symmetry, no guarantee. An expanding universe is not the same today as tomorrow, and light crossing it loses energy. The cosmic microwave background was released when the universe was 1,100 times smaller; each photon then carried about 0.26 eV and now carries about a thousandth of that. Where did the energy go? Nowhere: in an expanding spacetime there is in general no conserved total energy to account for. Energy is still conserved locally, ∇μTμν=0\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0∇μ​Tμν=0, but a global total needs a time symmetry that the universe does not have. Established, though how to phrase it is a matter of taste.
