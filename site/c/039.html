Angular momentum

Turn a neutron through a full circle, 360 degrees, and it does not come back to itself. Its wavefunction has changed sign. Turn it a second full circle and it is back. This was measured in 1975, by splitting neutron beams in a crystal interferometer and turning one half in a magnetic field. The possibility follows from three commutators and nothing else, and this chapter derives it.

On the plate: an arrow turning, and beside it, the sign of a spin-½ state turned with it.

The vector model

A state ∣j,m⟩|j, m\rangle∣j,m⟩ has a definite length ℏj(j+1)\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)}ℏj(j+1)​ and a definite zzz-component mℏm\hbarmℏ, but its xxx and yyy components are uncertain. Picture it as a vector somewhere on a cone around the zzz-axis. Choose m=m =m= 1.5 for your jjj.

The vector is longer than its largest projection, since j(j+1)>j\sqrt{j(j+1)} > jj(j+1)​>j, so it can never point straight along zzz. It cannot, because that would make JxJ_xJx​ and JyJ_yJy​ both exactly zero, and they do not commute. The cone is a picture, not a mechanism; there is no hidden vector going round.

Turn through 360°. The arrow is back after 360°. The spin-½ amplitude, drawn as a pointer turning at half the speed, is reversed, and needs 720° to return. A sign on its own is unobservable, but a sign relative to an unrotated partner is not.

Seeing the sign

In 1975 two groups, Samuel Werner’s in Missouri and Helmut Rauch’s in Vienna, split a neutron beam in two inside a single silicon crystal, passed one half through a magnetic field that turned its spin by a controllable angle, and recombined them. The interference pattern repeats only every 720° of turning, not every 360°; the measured periods agreed with 720° to within a few percent. The plate shows the expected intensity, I∝1+cos⁡(θ/2)I \propto 1 + \cos(\theta/2)I∝1+cos(θ/2).

So the minus sign is physical. It is how fermions, all particles with half-integer spin, differ from bosons, and Part Five will show it is linked to the Pauli exclusion principle. Established

Two descriptions of turning

Ordinary rotations in space form the group SO(3) (Chapter 20). The 2×22\times22×2 unitary matrices with determinant one form SU(2). Every rotation corresponds to two SU(2) matrices, UUU and −U-U−U. Integer spins see only SO(3). Half-integer spins see all of SU(2), the minus sign included. The quantum world turned out to use the larger group.

DeeperSU(2) is topologically a three-sphere, which is simply connected; SO(3) is the three-sphere with opposite points identified, so a loop of rotations through 2π2\pi2π cannot be shrunk to nothing, but one through 4π4\pi4π can. Dirac’s belt trick shows the same fact with a ribbon.

What you now have

The full spectrum of angular momentum from its commutators, half-integers included, ladder factors, the vector model, rotation operators, the 720° spinor and the neutron experiments that saw it, and SU(2) as the double cover of rotations.
