Spin and topological qubits

Two very different bets close the hardware survey. One uses the spin of single electrons trapped in silicon, made in the same factories that make computer chips: if it works, billions of qubits could be manufactured as transistors are. The other seeks qubits protected by topology, storing information non-locally so that local noise cannot touch it. Microsoft says it has built one. Many physicists are not convinced. This chapter shows how to read that evidence yourself.

On the plate: how electrons fill two neighbouring quantum dots.

Spins in silicon

Daniel Loss and David DiVincenzo proposed in 1998 using single electron spins in semiconductor quantum dots, tiny regions where gate electrodes confine electrons one by one. Spin-up and spin-down in a magnetic field form the qubit (Chapter 42); bringing two dots together turns on the exchange interaction J S1⋅S2J\,\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2JS1​⋅S2​, giving a two-qubit gate. Silicon is the natural host: its main isotope, ²⁸Si, has no nuclear spin. Natural silicon contains 4.7% ²⁹Si, whose nuclear spins create a fluctuating magnetic field; isotopically purified silicon, with ²⁹Si below 0.1%, extends coherence dramatically. In 2025 Diraq and imec reported spin qubits made on a 300 mm industrial production line with over 99% fidelity for every operation. Nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond and phosphorus donors in silicon are related spin platforms; NV centres return as sensors in Chapter 154 and Part 4. Established

Charge stability diagrams

Before spins can be used, each dot must hold exactly the right number of electrons. Sweep two gate voltages and record the ground-state occupation (n1,n2)(n_1, n_2)(n1​,n2​). Each dot costs charging energy to add an electron, and electrons on neighbouring dots repel each other with mutual energy EmE_mEm​ = 0.30 (relative to the charging energy). With no coupling the regions form a square grid; coupling turns it into a honeycomb. Experimenters navigate these diagrams, now often with machine-learning autotuners, to reach the (1,1) region where each dot holds one spin. Established

Majoranas and topological qubits

Alexei Kitaev showed in 2001 that a superconducting wire of a certain kind hosts Majorana zero modes at its ends: two halves of one fermion, separated in space. A pair of them stores one bit, the fermion parity, which no local disturbance can read or flip. Exchanging (braiding) Majoranas applies gates that depend only on the topology of the exchange. Watch the plate braid two of four Majoranas while the total parity stays fixed.

The difficulty is proving that the modes exist. Earlier claims of Majorana signatures, including a 2018 Nature paper, were retracted. In February 2025 Microsoft announced “Majorana 1” and published in Nature an interferometric parity measurement in indium-arsenide–aluminium devices; the paper’s own editorial note said it did not establish Majorana modes. Microsoft announced a follow-up device in 2026. In June 2026 Nature published a critique by Henry Legg of the “topological gap protocol” used to identify the topological phase, alongside a Microsoft reply. Topological protection remains undemonstrated. Debated
