Ordinary differential equations

A sample of radon gas fading away, a capacitor draining through a resistor, and a footbridge settling after a crowd crosses it look like three unrelated stories. They are told by the same few equations. Learn those equations once and you can read all three, along with most of the physics in this book.

An ordinary differential equation says how a quantity changes in terms of what it currently is. Physics is mostly written this way, because nature is local in time: what happens next depends on what is happening now.

Two numbers describe its state at any moment: where it is and how fast it moves. Plot them as a point in a plane and the motion becomes a path. Set the friction to 0.20 (with ω0=1\omega_0 = 1ω0​=1). With no friction the path is a closed loop: the energy never leaves. With a little, it spirals inward. At γ=2\gamma = 2γ=2 the system is critically damped and returns to rest as fast as possible without overshooting, which is how car suspensions and door closers are tuned.

The bridge that was not a resonance

Textbooks often say the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 because the wind pushed it at its resonant frequency. That is not what happened. A steady wind does not push at a single frequency. The deck’s own twisting changed how the air flowed around it, and that flow fed energy back into the twisting. The motion drove itself. Established

Engineers call this aerodynamic flutter, a kind of self-excitation. In the equation it looks like negative damping: γ\gammaγ turns negative, and the spiral in the phase plane runs outward instead of in. K. Yusuf Billah and Robert Scanlan set the record straight in a 1991 paper written for physics teachers.

When the computer lies

Most equations in this book cannot be solved with a pen, so we step them forward on a computer. The simplest method, Euler’s, takes the current slope and walks in a straight line for a time step hhh. For an oscillator with no friction, that is quietly wrong: every step adds a little energy. Watch the outer spiral on the plate.

Set the step to 0.10. With h=0.1h = 0.1h=0.1, after ten time units Euler’s energy has grown by a factor of 2.7. The fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, which samples the slope four times per step, keeps it correct to six decimal places in the same run.

The lesson goes beyond oscillators: a simulation that does not conserve what physics conserves will drift into nonsense. Always check.

What you now have

Exponential decay, oscillation with friction, resonance and its sharpness, a picture of motion as a path in the phase plane, and a healthy suspicion of naive numerical methods.
