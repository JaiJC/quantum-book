Phase estimation

Give a quantum computer a unitary UUU and one of its eigenstates, U∣u⟩=e2πiφ∣u⟩U|u\rangle = e^{2\pi i\varphi}|u\rangleU∣u⟩=e2πiφ∣u⟩, and it can measure the phase φ\varphiφ to ttt binary digits using ttt extra qubits. That one routine, phase estimation, sits inside Shor’s algorithm, quantum chemistry, the HHL linear solver and much more. Most of the famous exponential speedups are phase estimation in disguise.

The Hadamard test, and versions used on hardware

With a single control qubit, the Hadamard test measures Re⟨u∣U∣u⟩\mathrm{Re}\langle u|U|u\rangleRe⟨u∣U∣u⟩ (and, with an S gate, the imaginary part) as the bias of a coin. Repeating with different powers and using classical post-processing gives iterative phase estimation: one ancilla, many shots, each bit fed back into the next measurement, as in Kitaev’s original 1995 scheme. Bayesian versions update a probability distribution for φ\varphiφ after each shot and choose the next experiment adaptively. These one-ancilla versions are what current devices run, because they need short circuits and few qubits. Established

Why it is everywhere

Factoring: the eigenphases of modular multiplication encode the period (Chapter 114). Chemistry: the eigenphases of e−iHte^{-iHt}e−iHt are energies, so phase estimation on a molecule’s Hamiltonian, given a good approximate ground state, yields its ground-state energy (Part Five). Linear systems: phase estimation reveals the eigenvalues of a matrix, which HHL inverts (Chapter 120). Amplitude estimation (Chapter 116) is phase estimation of a Grover operator. Quantum signal processing (Chapter 119) is a refined descendant that avoids the counting register altogether.
