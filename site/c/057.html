The Dirac equation and antimatter

In 1928 Paul Dirac wanted a relativistic equation that, unlike Klein–Gordon, was first order in time, so that probability would stay positive. To take the square root of E2=p2+m2E^2 = p^2 + m^2E2=p2+m2 he had to use matrices instead of numbers. What came out was not what he asked for. The equation described particles with spin ½, predicted that the electron’s magnet is exactly twice as strong as a simple spinning charge, and insisted on a second kind of electron with positive charge. Four years later Carl Anderson photographed one.

On the plate: the four Dirac matrices, and the rule that ties them together.

Four components, two of them spin

Because the matrices are 4 × 4, ψ\psiψ has four components, a Dirac spinor. Under a Lorentz transformation it changes by a matrix built from [γμ,γν][\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu][γμ,γν], and the equation keeps its form in every frame. The orbital angular momentum L\mathbf LL is not conserved by the Dirac Hamiltonian, but J=L+12Σ\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \tfrac12\boldsymbol\SigmaJ=L+21​Σ is, where Σ\boldsymbol\SigmaΣ is a pair of Pauli matrices. Spin ½ was not put in. It is required for the equation to respect rotations.

Two of the four components describe the electron’s two spin states; the other two describe a second particle.

g = 2

Put the electron in a magnetic field and take the slow-motion limit. The Dirac equation reduces to Pauli’s equation with an extra term, −e2m g S⋅B-\frac{e}{2m}\,g\,\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf B−2me​gS⋅B, and g=2g = 2g=2 exactly. A classical spinning charge has g=1g = 1g=1. The value 2, measured in the Zeeman effect (Chapter 42), had to be put in by hand before Dirac. Dirac’s equation also gives hydrogen’s fine structure exactly as in Chapter 44, including the 10.95 GHz splitting of the n=2n = 2n=2 level, and leaves 2s and 2p1/2 together until QED splits them. The tiny deviation of ggg from 2 is the subject of Chapter 72.

Antimatter

The equation has negative-energy solutions, and an electron, it seemed, should fall into them and radiate away. Dirac’s 1930 answer: they are all already full, a “sea”, and the exclusion principle (Chapter 43) forbids falling in. A missing electron in the sea, a hole, behaves like a particle of positive energy and positive charge. In 1931 he predicted it: an anti-electron, with the electron’s mass.

In August 1932 Carl Anderson, photographing cosmic rays in a cloud chamber in a magnetic field, saw a track that curved like an electron’s but the wrong way. A lead plate in the chamber showed which way it was moving: it lost energy crossing the plate, from 63 to 23 MeV, so it was a positive particle as light as an electron. The positron. The sea picture was later replaced by field theory (Chapter 60), but the prediction stood. Established

The trembling electron

In a free Dirac particle built from both positive and negative energies, the position jitters at frequency 2m2m2m with amplitude about half a Compton wavelength, 193 fm for an electron. Schrödinger called it Zitterbewegung. Mix in a negative-energy share of 0.20 and watch the drift and the tremble. For an electron the frequency is 2.5×10202.5\times10^{20}2.5×1020 Hz, far too fast to see. In 2010 Rainer Blatt’s group simulated the Dirac equation with a trapped ion, where the parameters can be tuned, and recorded the jitter directly.

What you now have

The Dirac equation and the Clifford algebra {γμ,γν}=2ημν\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}{γμ,γν}=2ημν, four-component spinors, spin as a consequence of relativity, g=2g = 2g=2, exact hydrogen fine structure, the prediction and discovery of the positron, and Zitterbewegung.
