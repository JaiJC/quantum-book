Debate: quantum advantage and quantum biology

The second debate puts four live controversies from this book on trial: Google’s 2025 “Quantum Echoes” advantage claim, D-Wave’s 2025 beyond-classical simulation claim, long-lived coherence in photosynthesis, and the robin’s quantum compass. For each you argue for and against, then weigh the evidence explicitly. The tool is simple Bayesian bookkeeping: start from a prior, multiply by how much more likely each piece of evidence is if the claim is true than if it is false, and see where you end up.

On the plate: an evidence-weighing ledger for each case.

Weighing evidence

Write the claim’s odds as P/(1−P)P/(1-P)P/(1−P). Each piece of evidence EEE has a likelihood ratio LR=P(E∣true)/P(E∣false)\mathrm{LR} = P(E\mid\text{true})/P(E\mid\text{false})LR=P(E∣true)/P(E∣false): above 1 supports the claim, below 1 opposes it. Posterior odds are prior odds times the product of the likelihood ratios, assuming the pieces are independent. Independent replication is worth far more than a second result from the same group, because it removes shared errors; a classical simulation that reproduces a claimed quantum result is strong evidence against advantage. Assigning likelihood ratios is a judgement, which is the point: it makes your judgement visible and arguable. Established as method.

The four cases

Case: ; prior probability 0.50. The plate lists the main pieces of evidence for each claim with this book’s likelihood ratios (from Chapters 125, 126, 150, 167 and 169) and computes the posterior. The FMO claim (long-lived electronic coherence plays a functional role) ends low; the robin compass (a radical-pair mechanism) ends high but short of proof; the two 2025 advantage claims sit in between, pending independent classical attempts. Change the prior and watch how much the evidence, rather than your starting belief, decides. Debated for every likelihood ratio.

The debate

For one case, write 400 words for the claim and 400 against, each citing specific evidence, then a 200-word verdict that states your likelihood ratios and why. Graded on: whether each side uses the strongest evidence available to it, whether the likelihood ratios are justified, whether independence between pieces of evidence is respected, and whether the verdict says what new evidence would change it. Established as method.

What you now have

Likelihood ratios and posterior odds as a way to weigh evidence, why independent replication and classical reproductions carry so much weight, four worked cases, and a debate format with a verdict that names what would change it.
