Dirac notation

The state of one qubit, the state of an electron in a hydrogen atom, and the state of every field in the universe can all be written with the same three symbols: a vertical bar, a name, and an angle bracket. Paul Dirac introduced this notation in 1939, and it has never been replaced, because it lets you calculate without first deciding what kind of space you are in.

This chapter teaches you to read and write it fluently. Each idea is shown three ways at once on the plate: as a ket, as a column of numbers, and as a picture.

Kets and bras

A state is a ket, ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩. In a chosen basis it is a column of complex numbers. Its partner, the bra ⟨ψ∣\langle\psi|⟨ψ∣, is the row of complex conjugates. Put a bra and a ket together and you get a number, the inner product ⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩\langle\phi|\psi\rangle⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩: a “bra-ket”, which is where the names come from.

Take a qubit at angle 20 °, ∣ψ⟩=cos⁡θ∣0⟩+sin⁡θ∣1⟩|\psi\rangle = \cos\theta|0\rangle + \sin\theta|1\rangle∣ψ⟩=cosθ∣0⟩+sinθ∣1⟩. The plate shows the ket, its column of numbers, and the arrow in the plane.

Projectors and the identity

Put them the other way round, ket then bra, and you get not a number but an operator: ∣a⟩⟨a∣|a\rangle\langle a|∣a⟩⟨a∣, which projects any state onto ∣a⟩|a\rangle∣a⟩. Add the projectors for a complete set of perpendicular basis states and you get the identity, the operator that does nothing:

∑a∣a⟩⟨a∣=1\sum_a |a\rangle\langle a| = \mathbb 1a∑​∣a⟩⟨a∣=1

Continuous bases

A particle’s position can take any real value, so its basis is continuous: one ket ∣x⟩|x\rangle∣x⟩ for every point. Sums become integrals, and the wavefunction is simply the list of components in this basis:

ψ(x)=⟨x∣ψ⟩,∫∣x⟩⟨x∣ dx=1,⟨x∣x′⟩=δ(x−x′)\psi(x) = \langle x|\psi\rangle, \qquad \int |x\rangle\langle x|\,dx = \mathbb 1, \qquad \langle x|x'\rangle = \delta(x - x')ψ(x)=⟨x∣ψ⟩,∫∣x⟩⟨x∣dx=1,⟨x∣x′⟩=δ(x−x′)

Choosing the basis in which an operator is diagonal is how most quantum problems are solved.
