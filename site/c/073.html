Lie groups and representations

By 1960 particle physicists had found dozens of strongly interacting particles, with no apparent order: a zoo. Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne’eman independently noticed that, plotted by charge and strangeness, they fell into hexagons and triangles. The pattern was the representation theory of a group called SU(3): the particles were its multiplication table. A gap in one of the triangles predicted a particle no one had seen, and it was found. This chapter gives you the mathematics of those patterns.

Casimirs label representations

An operator built from the generators that commutes with all of them, a Casimir, takes one value on a whole representation. For SU(2) it is J2=j(j+1)J^2 = j(j+1)J2=j(j+1), which you met as total angular momentum. For SU(3), C2=∑aTaTaC_2 = \sum_aT^aT^aC2​=∑a​TaTa equals 4/34/34/3 on the 3 and 3 on the 8. These numbers reappear as the colour factors in QCD (Chapter 78), measuring how strongly quarks and gluons radiate.

The Lorentz group in disguise

The six generators of rotations and boosts (Chapter 17) can be recombined into two commuting sets, each obeying the SU(2) algebra. So representations of the Lorentz group are labelled by two spins, (j1,j2)(j_1, j_2)(j1​,j2​). (12,0)(\tfrac12, 0)(21​,0) and (0,12)(0, \tfrac12)(0,21​) are the left- and right-handed Weyl spinors, two-component objects; a Dirac spinor (Chapter 57) is one of each. (12,12)(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)(21​,21​) is a four-vector. The weak force acts only on left-handed Weyl spinors (Chapter 81), so this split is not a technicality.

What you now have

Lie groups and algebras, structure constants, representations and weight diagrams for SU(3), tensor-product decompositions, Casimir operators, and the Lorentz group as two copies of SU(2) with Weyl spinors.
