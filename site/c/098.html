Multi-qubit systems and tensor products

One qubit needs two complex numbers to describe. Two qubits need four; three need eight; fifty need about a million billion. Store each as 16 bytes and fifty qubits fill 18 petabytes, more than any supercomputer’s memory. That doubling, one new qubit, twice the description, is both why quantum computers might be powerful and why simulating them classically is so hard. This chapter builds multi-qubit states and introduces the small simulator the rest of Part Three uses.

On the plate: the eight amplitudes of a three-qubit register. Height shows magnitude; colour shows phase.

Tensor products

Two systems together are described by the tensor product of their state spaces (Chapter 8). For qubits, ∣a⟩⊗∣b⟩=∣ab⟩|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle = |ab\rangle∣a⟩⊗∣b⟩=∣ab⟩, and the basis of nnn qubits is the 2n2^n2n bit strings. Matrices combine by the Kronecker product: (A⊗B)(ij),(kl)=AikBjl(A\otimes B)_{(ij),(kl)} = A_{ik}B_{jl}(A⊗B)(ij),(kl)​=Aik​Bjl​. A gate UUU on qubit 0 alone is U⊗1⊗⋯U\otimes\mathbb 1\otimes\cdotsU⊗1⊗⋯. Most states of several qubits are not products of single-qubit states; those are entangled (Chapter 99). Established

Measuring part of a register

Measure just one qubit. The probability of 0 is the sum of ∣amplitude∣2|\text{amplitude}|^2∣amplitude∣2 over every basis state with that qubit 0. Afterwards, the amplitudes inconsistent with the result are removed and the rest renormalized. . On the GHZ state, measuring q0 instantly fixes q1 and q2 too: entanglement. On a product state, the other qubits are unaffected.
