A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit can be in any of a continuum of states: any point on a sphere. That sounds like infinitely more information. But measure it and you get one bit, 0 or 1, and the state is gone. Alexander Holevo proved in 1973 that no cleverness can get more than one classical bit out of one qubit. The power of quantum computing comes not from storing more, but from how amplitudes combine before you look.

On the plate: a qubit on the Bloch sphere, and the outcomes of measuring many copies.

The state of one qubit

Any two-level quantum system, the spin of Chapter 40, a photon’s polarization, two levels of an ion, is a qubit. Its state is {{tex:\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle}} with {{tex:|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1}}. Multiplying the whole state by a phase {{tex:e^{i\gamma}}} changes nothing measurable, so up to that global phase,

{{texd:|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\,|1\rangle}}

a point on the Bloch sphere at polar angle {{tex:\theta}} and azimuth {{tex:\phi}} (Chapter 40). Set {{tex:\theta =}} 60°, {{tex:\phi =}} 0°. The relative phase {{tex:\phi}} is physical: it decides how the state interferes. Established

Holevo’s bound

Suppose Alice encodes a message by sending one of several qubit states, each with some probability, and Bob measures however he likes. The information Bob can extract is at most the Holevo quantity

{{texd:\chi = S\!\left(\sum_ip_i\rho_i\right) - \sum_ip_iS(\rho_i) \le \log_2d}}

with {{tex:S}} the quantum entropy of Chapter 105 and {{tex:d = 2}} for a qubit. So one qubit carries at most one bit. Sending {{tex:|0\rangle, |1\rangle, |+\rangle, |-\rangle}} with equal probability, the four states of the BB84 key-distribution protocol (Chapter 132), gives {{tex:\chi = 1}}: the average state is maximally mixed, and four states still give only one bit. Established

So where does the power come from?

Not from storage. From three things you will see repeatedly: a register of {{tex:n}} qubits is described by {{tex:2^n}} amplitudes (Chapter 98); gates act on all of them at once; and amplitudes, unlike probabilities, can cancel. An algorithm arranges for wrong answers to interfere destructively and right ones constructively, so that the one bit string you finally read is likely to be useful. Without interference, a quantum computer is just a randomized classical one.
