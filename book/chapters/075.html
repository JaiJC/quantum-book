In 1954 Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills asked what happens if the phase you are free to choose at each point is not a single angle but a matrix: a rotation in an internal space, like the isospin that turns a proton into a neutron. The answer was a theory of several force carriers that, unlike photons, carry the charge of the force themselves, and so attract and repel one another. When Yang presented it, Wolfgang Pauli kept asking from the audience what the mass of these particles was. Yang had no answer. Twenty years later, this theory, with three colours, was the theory of the strong force.

A matrix-valued phase

Let the field {{tex:\psi}} have several components, transforming as {{tex:\psi \to U(x)\psi}} with {{tex:U = e^{i\alpha^a(x)T^a}}} in a group like SU(2) or SU(3) (Chapter 73). The covariant derivative needs one gauge field per generator:

{{texd:D_\mu = \partial_\mu - igA^a_\mu T^a}}

and for invariance the gauge fields must transform as {{tex:A_\mu \to UA_\mu U^{-1} - \frac{i}{g}(\partial_\mu U)U^{-1}}}. Because the matrices don’t commute, the gauge fields themselves rotate under the group: they carry charge. SU(N) has {{tex:N^2 - 1}} generators, so SU(3) needs eight gauge fields, the gluons. The field strength

The gauge-covariant field strength picks up a new term:

{{texd:F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu + g\,f^{abc}A^b_\mu A^c_\nu}}

The Lagrangian {{tex:-\tfrac14F^a_{\mu\nu}F^{a\mu\nu}}} now contains products of three and four gauge fields. Choose a theory: or . QED has one vertex, matter–photon. Yang–Mills adds a three-gluon vertex, proportional to {{tex:gf^{abc}}}, and a four-gluon vertex, proportional to {{tex:g^2ff}}. Gluons interact with gluons. That single difference is responsible for asymptotic freedom and, probably, for confinement (Chapter 79).

Pauli’s question

A mass term {{tex:m^2A^a_\mu A^{a\mu}}} breaks gauge invariance, exactly as for the photon (Chapter 67). So Yang–Mills quanta must be massless, and Pauli’s objection seemed fatal: no massless strongly interacting particles were seen. Two answers emerged. For the weak force, the bosons do get mass, through the Higgs mechanism (Chapter 77), without breaking the gauge invariance of the equations. For the strong force, the gluons are massless but never seen alone; they are confined inside hadrons.

Ghosts and renormalizability

Quantizing Yang–Mills with path integrals (Chapter 53) overcounts gauge-equivalent configurations. Ludvig Faddeev and Victor Popov showed in 1967 how to divide out the redundancy; the price is extra fields, ghosts, that circulate in loops but never appear as particles. They have spin zero yet obey Fermi statistics, which is allowed because they are not physical. Then in 1971 Gerard ’t Hooft, a student of Martinus Veltman, proved that Yang–Mills theories, including those with masses from the Higgs mechanism, are renormalizable. That proof made the Standard Model a consistent theory. Established

What you now have

Non-abelian gauge transformations, the covariant derivative with {{tex:N^2 - 1}} gauge fields, the non-abelian field strength and gluon self-interaction, why gauge bosons start massless, Faddeev–Popov ghosts, and ’t Hooft’s renormalizability proof.
