Many enzymes break carbon–hydrogen bonds, moving a proton, a hydrogen atom or a hydride ion from one molecule to another. Classical chemistry pictures the hydrogen climbing over an energy barrier. Since the late 1980s, Judith Klinman, Nigel Scrutton and others have shown that in many enzymes the hydrogen goes through the barrier instead. That it tunnels is established. How the protein helps it tunnel, and whether evolution tuned protein motions for the purpose, is still argued.

Why mass matters so much

Tunnelling probability falls as {{tex:e^{-2d\sqrt{2mV}/\hbar}}} (Chapter 162): exponentially in the square root of the mass. Hydrogen is the lightest nucleus, so it tunnels far more readily than anything else, and its isotopes give a built-in experiment. Through a barrier 0.5 eV high and 0.5 Å wide, hydrogen passes with probability about {{tex:2\times10^{-7}}}, deuterium {{tex:3\times10^{-10}}}, tritium {{tex:2\times10^{-12}}}. Doubling the mass costs a factor of 600. The WKB approximation (Chapter 47) generalizes this to curved barriers: {{tex:T\approx\exp\big(-\tfrac2\hbar\int\sqrt{2m(V(x)-E)}\,dx\big)}}. Established

Distance is everything, and the protein moves

Because the barrier width is set by how far apart donor and acceptor atoms are, small changes in distance make large changes in rate. In environmentally coupled or vibrationally assisted tunnelling models, developed by Klinman, Amnon Kohen, Sharon Hammes-Schiffer, Scrutton and others, the reaction needs the protein first to bring donor and acceptor close, a “gating” motion, then the hydrogen tunnels from the compressed geometry. The plate oscillates the donor–acceptor distance by 0.10 Å around its mean. Because the rate is exponential in distance, the brief close approaches dominate: the average rate exceeds the rate at the average distance, by more for deuterium than hydrogen. That is one reason the temperature dependence of isotope effects carries information about protein motion (Chapter 165). Established that tunnelling occurs.

The dynamics debate

A sharper claim is that enzymes evolved specific fast motions, “promoting vibrations” on femtosecond to picosecond timescales, that couple to the reaction and speed tunnelling. Supporters point to mutations far from the active site that change isotope-effect temperature dependence, and to heavy-enzyme experiments, in which replacing all of a protein’s carbon, nitrogen and hydrogen with heavier isotopes changes its catalysis. Critics, prominently Arieh Warshel, argue that the electrostatic preorganization of the active site explains catalysis, and that dynamical effects are small or are already included in transition-state theory with tunnelling corrections.
