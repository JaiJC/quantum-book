In 1994 Daniel Simon, then at the Université de Montréal, found a problem that a quantum computer solves with about {{tex:n}} queries and any classical computer, even one allowed to guess, needs about {{tex:2^{n/2}}}. It was the first exponential separation against randomized classical algorithms, and its structure, a hidden period revealed by Fourier sampling, was exactly what Peter Shor needed. Weeks after reading Simon’s paper, Shor had his factoring algorithm.

On the plate: equations collected from quantum queries, solved by Gaussian elimination.

The problem

A function {{tex:f}} on {{tex:n}}-bit strings is promised to satisfy {{tex:f(x) = f(y)}} if and only if {{tex:y = x}} or {{tex:y = x\oplus s}}, for some unknown nonzero {{tex:s}}. Find {{tex:s}}. Classically you must find a collision, two inputs with the same output, and by the birthday paradox that takes about {{tex:2^{n/2}}} queries. Simon proved no classical algorithm does fundamentally better. Established

The quantum algorithm

Prepare the uniform superposition, compute {{tex:f}} into a second register, and measure that register. The first register collapses to {{tex:(|x_0\rangle + |x_0\oplus s\rangle)/\sqrt2}} for a random {{tex:x_0}}. Apply Hadamards and measure: you get a random {{tex:y}} with {{tex:y\cdot s = 0 \pmod 2}}, every such {{tex:y}} equally likely. Each run gives one linear equation for {{tex:s}}. About {{tex:n}} runs suffice on average.

The hidden subgroup problem

Simon’s, Deutsch–Jozsa’s and Shor’s problems are all instances of one pattern: a function is constant on the cosets of a hidden subgroup {{tex:H}} of a group {{tex:G}} and different on different cosets; find {{tex:H}}. For Simon, {{tex:G}} is bit strings under XOR and {{tex:H = \{0, s\}}}. For Shor, {{tex:G}} is the integers and {{tex:H}} the multiples of a period. Whenever {{tex:G}} is abelian, the Fourier transform over {{tex:G}} solves the problem efficiently, as Alexei Kitaev showed in 1995. Established

Where it gets hard

For non-abelian groups the story changes. The symmetric group’s HSP would solve graph isomorphism; the dihedral group’s HSP is closely related, as Oded Regev showed in 2002, to finding short vectors in lattices, the problem behind post-quantum cryptography (Chapter 133). Greg Kuperberg found a subexponential quantum algorithm for the dihedral case in 2003, but no polynomial one is known. That gap is part of why lattice cryptography is trusted to resist quantum computers. Debated whether efficient algorithms exist.
