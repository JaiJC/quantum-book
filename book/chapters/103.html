In 2017 a team led by Jian-Wei Pan sent the quantum state of a photon from a ground station in Tibet to the satellite Micius, up to 1,400 kilometres away. The photon itself never went. What crossed the distance was half of an entangled pair, sent earlier, and two ordinary classical bits. With those, the state was rebuilt at the far end and destroyed at the near end. That is quantum teleportation. Its mirror image, superdense coding, sends two classical bits by transmitting one qubit.

On the plate: the teleportation circuit, one stage at a time.

Superdense coding

Alice and Bob share {{tex:|\Phi^+\rangle}}. To send two bits, Alice applies one of I, X, Z or XZ to her half: these turn {{tex:|\Phi^+\rangle}} into each of the four Bell states (Chapter 99). She sends her qubit to Bob, who does a Bell measurement and reads both bits. One qubit carried two bits, which Holevo’s bound (Chapter 96) seems to forbid, but it doesn’t: the pre-shared ebit was used up. Charles Bennett and Stephen Wiesner proposed it in 1992. Established

Teleportation

Alice holds an unknown qubit {{tex:|\psi\rangle}} and half of {{tex:|\Phi^+\rangle}}; Bob holds the other half. Alice does a Bell measurement on her two qubits, getting two random bits, and phones them to Bob. Depending on the bits, Bob applies I, X, Z or ZX, and his qubit is now exactly {{tex:|\psi\rangle}}. Set the state to teleport: {{tex:\theta =}} 60°, {{tex:\phi =}} 45°, and choose which outcome Alice gets: . Each happens with probability 1/4; each, after Bob’s correction, gives fidelity exactly 1. Charles Bennett and five colleagues proposed it in 1993; Dik Bouwmeester and colleagues in Anton Zeilinger’s group first did it with photons in 1997. Established

No faster than light

Before Bob gets Alice’s two bits, his qubit is maximally mixed whatever {{tex:|\psi\rangle}} was: averaged over the four equally likely outcomes, he has nothing (Chapter 50). The state appears at Bob’s end only when classical information, limited by light speed, arrives. And Alice’s original is destroyed by her measurement, as no-cloning requires (Chapter 102). The accounting: one ebit plus two classical bits buys one qubit of transmission.

Entanglement swapping, and space

Teleport one half of an entangled pair and you entangle two particles that never met: entanglement swapping, the basis of quantum repeaters (Chapter 153). In the 2017 Micius experiment, the satellite received photons from a ground source and verified the teleported states with average fidelity 0.80 ± 0.01. The best possible without entanglement, by measuring and resending, is 2/3, so the result is genuinely quantum, though far from the ideal of 1, mostly because of photon loss and atmospheric noise.
