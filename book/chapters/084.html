In the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang, matter and antimatter were made in nearly equal amounts. Nearly: for every billion antiquarks there were a billion and one quarks. Then everything that could annihilate did, leaving radiation and that one-in-a-billion surplus. The surplus is every star, planet and person. For the surplus to arise, the laws of physics must treat matter and antimatter differently. They do. But not, as far as anyone has found, by nearly enough.

C, P, T and CPT

Three discrete transformations: C swaps particles and antiparticles; P mirrors space; T reverses time. The weak force violates C and P separately (Chapter 81): a left-handed neutrino becomes, under C, a left-handed antineutrino, which does not exist; under P, a right-handed neutrino, which does not interact. The combination CP turns it into a right-handed antineutrino, which does exist. For a while CP seemed exact.

The CPT theorem, proved from Lorentz invariance and locality in the 1950s, says the combination of all three is always a symmetry of a relativistic quantum field theory. So particles and antiparticles have exactly equal masses and lifetimes; the antiproton’s charge-to-mass ratio matches the proton’s to sixteen parts in a trillion. Established

Kaons that change into their antiparticles

The neutral kaon {{tex:K^0 = d\bar s}} and its antiparticle {{tex:\bar K^0 = s\bar d}} can turn into each other through the weak force, a two-level system like the spin of Chapter 40. The states of definite lifetime are mixtures: a short-lived {{tex:K_S}} (0.09 ns) and a long-lived {{tex:K_L}} (51 ns). Start with pure {{tex:K^0}} and the probability of finding {{tex:\bar K^0}} oscillates at the mass difference {{tex:\Delta m = 3.5\times10^{-12}}} MeV, the smallest mass difference ever measured directly. Watch it for 2.00 ns.

If CP held, {{tex:K_L}} would be pure CP-odd and could never decay to two pions. In 1964 James Cronin, Val Fitch and colleagues found that it does, about 2 in 1,000 times. CP is violated. Established

B mesons, and baryons

In the Standard Model, CP violation comes from the CKM phase (Chapter 83). It predicted large effects in B mesons, containing a bottom quark, and in 2001 the BaBar experiment at SLAC and Belle at KEK, each built for the purpose, measured them: {{tex:\sin2\beta \approx 0.7}}, matching the triangle. Kobayashi and Maskawa received their Nobel Prize in 2008.

All these observations involved mesons. In 2025 LHCb reported the first CP violation in baryons, a 2.45% difference between the decays of the {{tex:\Lambda_b}} and its antiparticle, at 5.2 standard deviations. Established

What the universe needs

Andrei Sakharov listed in 1967 what any mechanism must have to make more matter than antimatter from a symmetric start. Check them off: , , . The Standard Model has all three in principle. But the CKM phase is suppressed by small quark mass differences and mixing angles; estimates of the resulting asymmetry come out around {{tex:10^{-20}}}, against the observed {{tex:6\times10^{-10}}} baryons per photon. That is short by roughly ten orders of magnitude.

So there must be another source of CP violation. Candidates include CP violation among neutrinos, feeding leptogenesis through heavy partners, and extra Higgs fields. None is established. Established that the Standard Model falls short; Speculative what fills the gap.
