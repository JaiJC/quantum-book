Send two identical photons at the same moment into opposite sides of a half-silvered mirror. Each, alone, would pass or reflect with equal odds, so you would expect them to leave by different exits half the time. They never do. They always leave together. Two electrons, on the other hand, can never be found in the same state at all. Every particle in nature belongs to one of these two clubs, and the rule that sorts them builds the periodic table, holds up dead stars, and makes metals conduct.

Swapping identical particles

Two electrons have no labels. If {{tex:\psi(x_1, x_2)}} describes them, swapping them cannot change anything measurable, so {{tex:|\psi(x_2, x_1)|^2 = |\psi(x_1, x_2)|^2}}. Swapping twice is doing nothing, so the swap can only multiply {{tex:\psi}} by {{tex:+1}} or {{tex:-1}}. Particles whose wavefunctions are symmetric are bosons; antisymmetric ones are fermions. The exclusion principle

For fermions, the antisymmetric combination of states {{tex:a}} and {{tex:b}} is {{tex:\frac{1}{\sqrt2}[\phi_a(x_1)\phi_b(x_2) - \phi_b(x_1)\phi_a(x_2)]}}. Put both in the same state, {{tex:a = b}}, and it is zero. Two identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state: Pauli’s exclusion principle, which he proposed in 1925 to explain atomic spectra before anyone knew why it held.

For {{tex:N}} fermions, the antisymmetric state is a determinant, the Slater determinant, {{tex:\det[\phi_i(x_j)]/\sqrt{N!}}}. A determinant with two equal rows is zero, which is the exclusion principle again. Electrons, protons, neutrons and quarks, all with half-integer spin, are fermions. Photons, gluons, the Higgs and helium-4 atoms, with integer spin, are bosons. Why spin decides this is the spin–statistics theorem, proved from relativity in Chapter 61.

Two photons that always leave together

At a 50:50 beam splitter, the two ways both photons can leave by different exits are both-transmitted and both-reflected. Reflection gives each photon a factor {{tex:i}}, so the two amplitudes are {{tex:1\cdot1}} and {{tex:i\cdot i = -1}}, and for identical bosons they cancel exactly. Chong Ki Hong, Zhe-Yu Ou and Leonard Mandel saw this in 1987: as they slid one photon’s arrival time past the other’s, the rate of coincidences dropped almost to zero.

Set the delay to 120 fs. The dip’s width is the photons’ coherence time, here about a hundred femtoseconds. Distinguishable in time, they behave independently; indistinguishable, they bunch. This Hong–Ou–Mandel dip is now a standard test that two photons from different sources are truly identical, which photonic quantum computers depend on. Established

The exchange interaction

Electrons also carry spin, and it is the whole state, space and spin together, that must be antisymmetric. The spin triplet of Chapter 41 is symmetric, so it needs an antisymmetric spatial part, which keeps the electrons apart; the singlet allows a symmetric one, which lets them come close. Since electrons repel, the triplet has lower repulsion energy.

In helium with one electron excited to 2s, the triplet sits at 19.82 eV above the ground state and the singlet at 20.62 eV: an 0.8 eV gap made by symmetry acting through the Coulomb force. This exchange energy is behind Hund’s rule that electrons in an atom prefer parallel spins, and behind ferromagnetism in iron. It is not a new force, only the old one seen through the exclusion principle.

Building the periodic table

Fill the hydrogen-like orbitals of Chapter 38, each holding two electrons of opposite spin, from the lowest energy up. Choose an element with 11 electrons.

In a real atom the other electrons screen the nucleus, and s orbitals, which reach the nucleus (Chapter 37), are screened less than p and d. So 4s fills before 3d, and the order runs 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. Each row of the table ends when a p shell closes, giving the inert noble gases. The rule has exceptions, chromium and copper among them, where a half-full or full d shell wins.

Crowds of fermions, crowds of bosons

Fill a metal’s conduction electrons into states one by one and the last ones sit far up, at the Fermi energy: about 7 eV in copper, moving at 1,600 km/s even at absolute zero. The same pressure of exclusion holds up white dwarf stars against gravity, up to about 1.4 solar masses. Bosons do the opposite: below a critical temperature, a large fraction pile into the single lowest state, a Bose–Einstein condensate.

Compare how particles share out energy at temperature 0.30. Fermions fill to a sharp edge; bosons pile up at low energy; at high temperature both look classical. Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman condensed rubidium atoms at about 170 billionths of a kelvin in 1995, and Wolfgang Ketterle sodium soon after; they shared the 2001 Nobel Prize. Established
