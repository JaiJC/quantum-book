The dumbbell of a p orbital, the cloverleaf of a d orbital, the round s: chemistry students learn these shapes as facts to memorize. They are not separate facts. Every one of them follows from a single property of the atom, that the force on the electron points straight at the nucleus and is the same in every direction. This chapter derives the shapes from that symmetry alone.

On the plate, the angular shapes turn slowly in space. Amber and red mark the two signs of the wavefunction.

Separating the directions

When {{tex:V}} depends only on the distance {{tex:r}}, use spherical coordinates. The kinetic-energy operator splits into a radial part and an angular part, and the angular part is exactly {{tex:\hat L^2/2mr^2}}, the squared angular momentum. So try

{{texd:\psi(r, \theta, \phi) = R(r)\,Y(\theta, \phi)}}

The angular factor must be an eigenfunction of {{tex:\hat L^2}} and, without loss, of {{tex:\hat L_z}}. Those are the spherical harmonics {{tex:Y_\ell^m}}, with {{tex:\hat L^2 Y = \hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y}} and {{tex:\hat L_z Y = \hbar m Y}}. They are the same for every central potential: the hydrogen atom, a nucleus, a spherical quantum dot. Only {{tex:R(r)}} knows what the potential is. Established

The shapes

The letters s, p, d, f stand for {{tex:\ell = 0, 1, 2, 3}}. The {{tex:\phi}} dependence of a true {{tex:\hat L_z}} eigenstate is {{tex:e^{im\phi}}}, so its magnitude is the same all the way round the {{tex:z}}-axis: a doughnut, with the phase winding {{tex:m}} times. Chemists add {{tex:+m}} and {{tex:-m}} states to make real functions with lobes, {{tex:\cos m\phi}} and {{tex:\sin m\phi}}. Both sets are correct; they are different bases for the same space (Chapter 8), and the real one is better for bonds.

The radial equation

Writing {{tex:u(r) = rR(r)}} turns the radial part into a one-dimensional problem, like Chapters 31 to 35:

{{texd:-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u'' + \left[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2mr^2}\right]u = Eu}}

The extra term is the centrifugal barrier: angular momentum keeps the particle away from the centre. The plate shows the effective potential for an electron around a proton with {{tex:\ell =}} 1. For {{tex:\ell = 0}} the electron can reach the nucleus; that is why s electrons, and only s electrons, feel the nucleus directly, which matters for hyperfine structure and for electron capture in radioactive decay.

Counting states

Since the energy depends on {{tex:\ell}} but not on {{tex:m}}, each level is at least {{tex:(2\ell + 1)}}-fold degenerate: rotating a state cannot change its energy when the potential is round. Some potentials have more. The three-dimensional harmonic oscillator has levels {{tex:(N + \tfrac32)\hbar\omega}} with degeneracy {{tex:(N+1)(N+2)/2}}: 1, 3, 6, 10. The plate draws them. Each level holds several {{tex:\ell}} values at once. Extra degeneracy like this always signals a hidden symmetry beyond rotation; hydrogen, next chapter, has one too.

A particle in a sphere

Inside a hard sphere of radius {{tex:R}} with no potential, the radial solutions are spherical Bessel functions {{tex:j_\ell(kr)}}, and the wall forces {{tex:j_\ell(kR) = 0}}. The energies are {{tex:E = \hbar^2 x_{n\ell}^2/2mR^2}}, where {{tex:x_{n\ell}}} are the zeros: 3.14 for 1s, 4.49 for 1p, 5.76 for 1d, 6.28 for 2s. This is the better model of the spherical quantum dots of Chapter 33, and the same ordering, with corrections, gives the shell structure of atomic nuclei and of metal clusters.
