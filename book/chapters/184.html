A chemist draws a molecule as letters and lines. A quantum computer needs a Hamiltonian: a list of numbers multiplying operators. Between them lies a translation that every quantum chemistry calculation performs, classical or quantum, and that is usually hidden inside software. This chapter does it by hand for the hydrogen molecule, in six steps, computing every number exactly. The same steps, with bigger tables, give the Hamiltonian of any molecule.

On the plate: each translation step for H₂, with its numbers.

Six steps

(1) Geometry: fix the nuclei (Born–Oppenheimer, Chapter 158). (2) Basis set: choose atomic orbitals, here STO-3G, three Gaussians fitted to each hydrogen 1s orbital. (3) One-electron integrals: overlap {{tex:S_{\mu\nu}}}, kinetic energy {{tex:T_{\mu\nu}}} and nuclear attraction {{tex:V_{\mu\nu}}}, combined into the core Hamiltonian {{tex:h_{\mu\nu}}}. (4) Two-electron integrals {{tex:(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)}}: the repulsion between electron densities. (5) Orbitals: transform to molecular orbitals, here {{tex:\sigma_g}} and {{tex:\sigma_u}} fixed by symmetry, usually from Hartree–Fock. (6) Hamiltonian: assemble the second-quantized Hamiltonian of Chapter 185, with these {{tex:h_{pq}}} and {{tex:(pq|rs)}} as its coefficients. Established

Units and accuracy

Quantum chemistry works in atomic units: lengths in bohr (0.529 Å), energies in hartree (27.21 eV, or 627.5 kcal/mol). Chemistry cares about energy differences, reaction energies and barriers, to about 1 kcal/mol, called chemical accuracy: 1.6 millihartree. The total electronic energy of even a small drug is thousands of hartree, so chemical accuracy in a difference requires errors to cancel or to be controlled to a part in a million. And the basis set itself has an error: STO-3G is far too small for real chemistry, and larger basis sets add orbitals, and so qubits, quickly. Established
