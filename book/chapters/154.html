The most mature quantum technology is not computing. It is measurement. Atomic clocks keep time to a second in the age of the universe; magnetometers built from defects in diamond have detected the magnetic signal of a single neuron firing; and the LIGO detectors, which heard black holes merging a billion light-years away, now inject specially prepared “squeezed” vacuum light to listen more sharply. The same fragility that makes qubits hard to protect makes them exquisite sensors.

On the plate: how precision improves with the number of probes.

Ramsey interferometry

Put a qubit into superposition with a {{tex:\pi/2}} pulse, let it evolve for time {{tex:T}} so that a frequency difference {{tex:\delta}} accumulates a phase {{tex:\varphi = \delta T}}, then apply a second {{tex:\pi/2}} pulse and measure. The probability of finding the qubit excited is {{tex:\tfrac12(1+\cos\delta T)}}: a fringe whose spacing narrows as {{tex:T}} grows, until decoherence (Chapter 135) washes it out. Interrogation time 1.0 (in units of the coherence time). Norman Ramsey’s method, which earned the 1989 Nobel Prize, underlies every atomic clock. Established

Standard quantum limit and Heisenberg limit

With {{tex:N}} independent atoms, the phase uncertainty falls as {{tex:1/\sqrt N}}: the standard quantum limit (SQL), just like averaging coin flips. Entangling the atoms, for example in a GHZ state where the phase accumulates {{tex:N}} times faster, can reach {{tex:1/N}}: the Heisenberg limit. The quantum Cramér–Rao bound {{tex:\Delta\varphi\ge1/\sqrt{\nu F_Q}}}, with {{tex:F_Q}} the quantum Fisher information and {{tex:\nu}} the number of repetitions, sets the ultimate precision for any state and measurement; {{tex:F_Q = N}} for independent atoms and up to {{tex:N^2}} for entangled ones. Vittorio Giovannetti, Seth Lloyd and Lorenzo Maccone laid out the framework in 2004. Choose {{tex:N = 10^x}} with {{tex:x =}} 6.0. Entangled states are fragile, so in practice gains come from squeezing: redistributing uncertainty between two quadratures. LIGO’s frequency-dependent squeezing, reported by Dhruva Ganapathy and colleagues in 2023, cut quantum noise by up to 5.8 dB and extended the detection range by 15–18%. Established

NV magnetometry

A nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centre in diamond, a nitrogen atom next to a missing carbon, has a spin-1 ground state with a zero-field splitting {{tex:D = 2.87}} GHz between {{tex:m_s = 0}} and {{tex:m_s = \pm1}}. Green light pumps it into {{tex:m_s = 0}}, and it fluoresces less when microwaves drive it to {{tex:\pm1}}: optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR), readable at room temperature. A magnetic field {{tex:B}} along the NV axis splits the {{tex:\pm1}} lines by {{tex:2\gamma B}}, with {{tex:\gamma\approx28}} GHz/T (the Zeeman effect, Chapter 42). Field 1.0 mT. NV sensors work inside living cells and near tissue, which is why they return in Parts 4 and 5 as probes of biology. Christian Degen, Friedemann Reinhard and Paola Cappellaro reviewed quantum sensing in 2017. Established

Three questions to carry with you.

Why does independent averaging give 1/√N?tap
Independent errors add in quadrature, as for coin flips.
How does entanglement reach 1/N?tap
An N-atom GHZ state accumulates phase N times faster, raising the Fisher information to N².
Why are NV centres useful in biology?tap
They read magnetic fields optically at room temperature, at nanometre to micrometre scales.
