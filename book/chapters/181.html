Quantum biology is young enough that a single good experiment could change it. Some of its questions are close to settled; some are open and testable with today’s tools; a few may never be testable. This last chapter of Part 4 picks five open questions, names for each the experiment that would settle it, and asks you to rank them and defend your ranking. It is the kind of thinking a research proposal needs.

On the plate: a board of open questions.

Five questions

1. Is the bird compass a cryptochrome radical pair, in the living bird? Decisive experiment: knock out or modify cryptochrome 4 in a migratory species, or alter its magnetic sensitivity (for example by changing tryptophans in the chain), and show that orientation changes accordingly. 2. Do enzymes use evolved fast motions to promote tunnelling? Decisive experiment: targeted changes to specific vibrational modes with predicted, measured effects on isotope-effect temperature dependence. 3. Does spin chemistry affect neurobiology or anaesthesia? Decisive experiment: independent, preregistered replication of the xenon isotope result, with measurement of the proposed radicals. 4. Do vibronic effects, not long-lived electronic coherence, measurably aid light harvesting? Decisive experiment: compare natural and engineered complexes where specific vibrational modes are shifted. 5. Did spin-selective chemistry set life’s handedness? Decisive experiment: show magnetite-driven enantioselection under plausible early-Earth conditions, and a geological record consistent with it. Speculative until tested.

Rank them

Sort the board by . The scores are this book’s judgement, out of 5; Adriana Marais and colleagues (2018) and Youngchan Kim and colleagues (2021) offer their own agendas. The bird compass scores high on all three: a clear question, a tractable organism, strong supporting evidence. The origin-of-life question scores high on importance and low on testability, because the early Earth cannot be rerun. Where you put the others depends on what you value, which is the point of the exercise. Debated for the scores.

Argue it

Write one page: the open question you would work on, the experiment you would do first, the result that would support the quantum hypothesis, and the result that would refute it. Then write the strongest objection a sceptical reviewer would make, and answer it. A useful rule from Chapter 180: specify in advance the classical explanation your experiment must exclude, and how you will tell the two apart. For a biomedical engineer, questions 1 and 3 are especially reachable: they combine animal physiology, controlled electromagnetic environments and careful statistics, all within engineering practice. Established as method.
