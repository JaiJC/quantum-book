Every term in the Dyson series of Chapter 62 is the vacuum average of a product of many field operators. Computing such averages directly would be hopeless. In 1950 Gian-Carlo Wick found that they reduce to something a child could do: pair the fields up, two by two, in every possible way. Each pairing is one Feynman diagram, and each pair is one line.

On the plate: a row of fields joined in pairs. Step through every possible pairing.

Time ordering

Field operators at different times don’t commute, so the order matters. The S-matrix needs the time-ordered product, which simply puts later fields to the left:

{{texd:T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\} = \begin{cases}\phi(x)\phi(y) & x^0 > y^0\\ \phi(y)\phi(x) & y^0 > x^0\end{cases}}}

Its vacuum average defines the Feynman propagator, {{tex:D_F(x - y) = \langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}|0\rangle}}: the amplitude for a quantum created at the earlier point to be absorbed at the later one. Whichever comes first, the creation always happens before the absorption.

Pair everything

Wick’s theorem says the time-ordered product of free fields equals the sum, over all ways of pairing some of them, of the propagators for the pairs times normal-ordered products of the rest (Chapter 60). In the vacuum, normal-ordered pieces vanish, so

{{texd:\langle0|T\{\phi_1\phi_2\cdots\phi_n\}|0\rangle = \sum_{\text{pairings}}\,\prod D_F(x_i - x_j)}}

The propagator in momentum space

Fourier transformed, the propagator is strikingly simple:

{{texd:D_F(p) = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon}}}

It blows up when {{tex:p^2 = m^2}}, the condition for a real particle. The tiny {{tex:i\epsilon}} decides how to go around the two poles at {{tex:p^0 = \pm E}} when integrating over energy. The plate shows the complex {{tex:p^0}} plane: the {{tex:+i\epsilon}} pushes the positive pole just below the axis and the negative one just above. Closing the contour below for later times picks up the positive-energy pole, a particle moving forward; closing above for earlier times picks up the negative one, an antiparticle moving forward the other way (Chapter 56). The {{tex:i\epsilon}} is the mathematical form of causality.

Symmetry factors

Pairings that give the same picture must be counted correctly. With one {{tex:\phi^4/4!}} vertex and nothing else, the four fields at the same point pair in 3 ways, each joining the point to itself twice; the {{tex:1/4!}} and the 3 combine into a factor {{tex:3/24 = 1/8}}. That {{tex:1/8}} is the diagram’s symmetry factor, one over the number of ways to rearrange its lines without changing it. The {{tex:1/4!}} in the Lagrangian is chosen precisely so that most diagrams come out with factor one.

What you now have

Time ordering, the Feynman propagator, Wick’s theorem as complete pairings with {{tex:(n-1)!!}} terms, the momentum-space propagator and the {{tex:i\epsilon}} prescription, and symmetry factors.
