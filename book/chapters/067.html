Start with an electron field and one demand: the phase of the field may be chosen independently at every point of space and time, and the physics must not care. That single requirement cannot be met by the electron field alone. To satisfy it you are forced to introduce a new field that couples to the electron in exactly one way, and which turns out to obey Maxwell’s equations. Light is the price of local phase freedom. This is quantum electrodynamics, the most precisely tested theory in science.

On the plate: a row of phase arrows, one per point. Twist them differently from place to place.

The demand

The Dirac Lagrangian {{tex:\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi}} is unchanged by a global phase, {{tex:\psi \to e^{i\alpha}\psi}} (Chapter 59). Now let {{tex:\alpha}} depend on position. The derivative picks up an extra piece: {{tex:\partial_\mu(e^{i\alpha(x)}\psi) = e^{i\alpha}(\partial_\mu\psi + i\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\psi)}}, and the Lagrangian changes.

Twist the phases by 1.0. With the plain derivative, the energy stored in the phase gradient, in red, changes, so the twist is detectable. Switch on the compensating field, or , and its links absorb the twist: the energy returns to its untwisted value.

The cure: a gauge field

Replace the derivative by a covariant derivative {{tex:D_\mu = \partial_\mu + ieA_\mu}}, and let the new field shift as {{tex:A_\mu \to A_\mu - \frac1e\partial_\mu\alpha}} when the phase turns. The extra pieces now cancel exactly. Add the simplest term for the new field that respects the same freedom, {{tex:-\tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}}}, and you have

{{texd:\mathcal L_{\text{QED}} = \bar\psi\,(i\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m)\,\psi - \tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}}}

Expanding {{tex:D_\mu}} gives the interaction {{tex:-e\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu}}: the current of Chapter 59 coupled to the potential, exactly the form required by Maxwell’s equations. A photon mass term {{tex:m^2A_\mu A^\mu}} would break the symmetry, so the photon must be massless. Established; measured photon mass below {{tex:10^{-18}}} eV.

The Feynman rules

QED has one vertex: an electron line in, an electron line out, and a photon, with factor {{tex:-ie\gamma^\mu}}. Electron lines carry {{tex:\frac{i(\gamma\cdot p + m)}{p^2 - m^2}}}; photon lines carry {{tex:\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{q^2}}}. External electrons bring spinors {{tex:u, \bar u}}, positrons {{tex:v, \bar v}}, photons a polarization vector. Every QED process, from an atom’s Lamb shift to light scattering off light, is built from these few pieces. The coupling is {{tex:e^2/4\pi = \alpha \approx 1/137}}.

A first calculation

Electron and positron annihilate into a photon, which becomes a muon pair: {{tex:e^+e^- \to \mu^+\mu^-}}, one diagram. Averaging over the initial spins and summing over the final ones turns spinor products into traces of gamma matrices, which follow from {{tex:\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}}} alone. At energies well above the muon mass,

{{texd:\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{\alpha^2}{4s}\left(1 + \cos^2\theta\right), \qquad \sigma = \frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3s} \approx \frac{86.8\ \text{nb}}{s\,[\text{GeV}^2]}}}

Set the collision energy {{tex:\sqrt s}} to 10 GeV. The {{tex:1 + \cos^2\theta}} shape, preferring the beam direction, is the signature of a spin-1 photon decaying to spin-½ particles, and it was measured at every electron–positron collider from the 1970s on.

What you now have

Local phase invariance and the covariant derivative, the QED Lagrangian and why the photon is massless, the single QED vertex and the Feynman rules, spin sums as traces, and the {{tex:e^+e^- \to \mu^+\mu^-}} cross section.
