The previous chapters asked whether biology uses quantum mechanics. This one turns the question around: quantum devices are becoming tools for biology. A nitrogen-vacancy centre in diamond, a single defect a few atoms across, works at room temperature, survives inside living cells, and measures magnetic fields, temperature and electric fields at the nanometre scale. In 2013 researchers used a diamond chip to map the magnetic fields of individual magnetotactic bacteria. These are quantum technologies applied to biology, not quantum effects in biology, and they are firmly established.

On the plate: the magnetic field of a tiny magnet, read by an NV centre.

The NV centre as a sensor

As Chapter 154 showed, the NV centre’s spin-1 ground state has a zero-field splitting {{tex:D = 2.87}} GHz, is initialized and read out with green light, and shifts its {{tex:m_s = \pm1}} lines by 28 kHz per microtesla of field along its axis. Its sensitivity is limited by photon shot noise and spin coherence: roughly {{tex:\delta B\approx\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{1}{C\sqrt{\eta T_2^*}}}} per root hertz, where {{tex:C}} is readout contrast and {{tex:\eta}} photon count rate. Single NVs reach about 1 µT/√Hz; dense ensembles in a diamond chip reach picotesla levels over larger areas. Romana Schirhagl and colleagues reviewed biological applications in 2014. Established

Imaging a magnetic bacterium

Magnetotactic bacteria build chains of magnetite nanocrystals, magnetosomes, and swim along Earth’s field. David Le Sage, Ronald Walsworth and colleagues placed bacteria on a diamond with a thin layer of NV centres and imaged the field from each cell in 2013. Model a magnetic dipole with moment 10^-15.0 A·m², typical of a magnetosome chain, at a distance of 1.0 µm. On axis, {{tex:B = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\tfrac{2m}{r^3}}}: the field falls as {{tex:1/r^3}}, so standoff distance is everything. Established

Thermometry and neurons

The zero-field splitting {{tex:D}} also depends on temperature, falling about 74 kHz per kelvin, because the lattice expands. Georg Kucsko, Mikhail Lukin and colleagues put nanodiamonds inside human cells in 2013 and measured local temperature to millikelvin precision, even creating temperature gradients with laser-heated gold nanoparticles. In 2016 John Barry, Walsworth and colleagues detected the magnetic signal of single action potentials from a marine worm’s and a squid’s giant axons with an NV ensemble. Mammalian neurons are smaller and their fields weaker, so imaging individual human neuron activity this way remains a goal, not an achievement. For a biomedical engineer, NV magnetometry, optically pumped magnetometers for wearable brain imaging (MEG), and NV-based detection of magnetic labels in diagnostics are the most direct entry points into quantum technology. Established
