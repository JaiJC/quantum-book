How do we tell one smell from another? The textbook answer is shape: odorant molecules fit receptors like keys in locks. In 1996 the biophysicist Luca Turin revived an old alternative: receptors might sense a molecule’s vibrations, through electrons tunnelling inelastically and losing a quantum of vibrational energy. The idea makes a crisp prediction. Replace an odorant’s hydrogens with deuterium: the shape is essentially unchanged, but C–H vibrations drop in frequency by a third. If vibration matters, the smell should change.

On the plate: the published isotope tests, and what each found.

The proposed mechanism

In Turin’s model, an electron in the receptor tunnels from a donor site to an acceptor site whose energy is lower by {{tex:\hbar\omega}}. Elastic tunnelling is blocked by the energy mismatch; it can proceed only if the electron hands energy {{tex:\hbar\omega}} to a vibration of the bound odorant. The receptor would thus act as a tiny inelastic-tunnelling spectrometer, firing when an odorant has a vibration at the right frequency. Jennifer Brookes, Marshall Stoneham and colleagues showed in 2007 that the physics is not impossible for plausible parameters. The question is whether receptors actually work this way. Speculative

Isotope tests

A C–H stretch near 2,900 cm⁻¹ (0.36 eV) becomes about 2,130 cm⁻¹ for C–D, by the reduced-mass ratio (Chapter 165). Choose a study: . The results disagree. Human panels could not tell acetophenone from its deuterated form; flies could distinguish isotopomers and be trained to avoid one; humans in the 2013 study distinguished deuterated musks but not acetophenone. In 2015 Eric Block, Hiroaki Matsunami and colleagues tested the human musk receptor OR5AN1 and 25 other receptor–odorant pairs in cells and found no difference between isotopomers. Turin and colleagues replied that impurities and assay sensitivity could explain the null result; Block’s group replied that trace impurities could equally explain the positive behavioural results. Speculative, with strong evidence against.

What a decisive test needs

Behavioural tests suffer from a confound: deuterated samples are made by different chemistry and can carry trace impurities that animals smell at parts per billion. A decisive experiment would use rigorously purified isotopomers verified by gas chromatography, measure single identified receptors with known ligands, include a positive control showing the assay can detect small differences, and test a quantitative prediction of the tunnelling model, for example the shift in response with the isotopic frequency change. As of 2026, receptor-level data do not support vibrational sensing, and structural studies of insect and mammalian olfactory receptors bound to odorants are consistent with shape-based, promiscuous binding.
