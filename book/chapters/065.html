A theorist writes {{tex:|\mathcal M|^2}} on a blackboard. Two years later, an experiment at the Large Hadron Collider reports a few hundred events in a narrow bump. Between the two lies a short chain of formulas: integrate over the ways the products can fly apart, divide by how fast the beams approach, and multiply by how many collisions the machine delivers. This chapter is that chain, and at the end of it you will predict how long a muon lives.

Decay rates

A particle of mass {{tex:M}} at rest decays at a rate given by Fermi’s golden rule (Chapter 45) dressed relativistically: {{tex:\Gamma = \frac{1}{2M}\int|\mathcal M|^2\,d\Phi}}, where {{tex:d\Phi}} counts final states consistent with energy and momentum conservation. For two products the integral is simple:

{{texd:\Gamma(A \to 1 + 2) = \frac{|\mathbf p|}{8\pi M^2}\,|\mathcal M|^2}}

with {{tex:|\mathbf p|}} the momentum of either product in the rest frame. The lifetime is {{tex:\tau = 1/\Gamma}}, and a resonance’s width in energy (Chapter 48) is the same {{tex:\Gamma}}. Established

The muon’s lifetime, predicted

A muon decays to an electron and two neutrinos through the weak force. Treating the force as a contact interaction with Fermi’s constant {{tex:G_F = 1.166\times10^{-5}}} GeV⁻², the three-body phase space integral gives

{{texd:\Gamma_\mu = \frac{G_F^2\,m_\mu^5}{192\pi^3}}}

With {{tex:m_\mu = 105.66}} MeV this is {{tex:3.01\times10^{-19}}} GeV, a lifetime of 2.187 microseconds. The measured value is 2.197 µs. The half-percent gap is filled by QED corrections and the electron’s mass. The fifth power of the mass is worth noticing: a particle twice as heavy decays 32 times faster. The tau lepton, 17 times heavier, lives about {{tex:17^5}} times shorter, less a correction for its extra decay routes.

Cross sections and Mandelstam variables

For two particles colliding, divide by their flux instead of by {{tex:2M}}: {{tex:\sigma = \frac{1}{4E_1E_2|v_1 - v_2|}\int|\mathcal M|^2d\Phi}}. For 2 → 2 in the centre-of-mass frame with light particles,

{{texd:\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{|\mathcal M|^2}{64\pi^2 s}}}

Kinematics is best written with three Lorentz-invariant combinations: {{tex:s = (p_1 + p_2)^2}}, the total energy squared; {{tex:t = (p_1 - p_3)^2}}, the momentum transfer squared; {{tex:u = (p_1 - p_4)^2}}. They satisfy {{tex:s + t + u = \sum m_i^2}}. Amplitudes are functions of them, and diagrams are named for them: an s-channel diagram has energy {{tex:\sqrt s}} flowing through its internal line, as at a resonance.
