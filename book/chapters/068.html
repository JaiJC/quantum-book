In 1923 Arthur Compton shone X-rays on graphite and found the scattered rays had longer wavelengths, by an amount that depended only on the angle (Chapter 23). That settled that light comes in particles. Six years later, Oskar Klein and Yoshio Nishina computed how often X-rays scatter at each angle, using Dirac’s brand-new equation, and found something classical physics could not give: at high energies, light scatters less, and mostly forwards. Every CT scanner and radiotherapy plan depends on their formula.

On the plate: how often a photon scatters into each direction off a free electron.

Compton scattering from QED

Two diagrams contribute to {{tex:\gamma e \to \gamma e}}: the electron absorbs the photon then emits one (s-channel), or emits first then absorbs (u-channel). Squaring, summing spins with traces (Chapter 67) and integrating gives the Klein–Nishina cross section. With {{tex:E'/E = 1/[1 + \varepsilon(1 - \cos\theta)]}} and {{tex:\varepsilon = E/m_ec^2}}:

{{texd:\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{r_e^2}{2}\left(\frac{E'}{E}\right)^2\left(\frac{E'}{E} + \frac{E}{E'} - \sin^2\theta\right)}}

Set the photon energy to 140 keV. At a few keV the pattern is symmetric front to back; at MeV energies it leans forward. Established

The low-energy check

As {{tex:E \to 0}}, {{tex:E' \to E}} and the formula becomes {{tex:\frac{r_e^2}{2}(1 + \cos^2\theta)}}, J. J. Thomson’s classical result for light shaking a free charge. Integrated, {{tex:\sigma_T = \frac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2 = 0.665}} barn, with {{tex:r_e = 2.818}} fm. A quantum field theory calculation has to reduce to classical physics where classical physics works, and this one does exactly. At 511 keV, the energy of the photons in a PET scanner, the cross section has dropped to 0.287 barn; at 10 MeV, to 0.051.

Why it matters in a hospital

In soft tissue, from about 30 keV to 20 MeV, Compton scattering is the main way photons lose energy. Diagnostic X-rays sit at the low end, where the photoelectric effect still gives bone its contrast. Radiotherapy beams of a few MeV deposit dose almost entirely through Compton electrons, so treatment planning systems carry Klein–Nishina inside them. Scattered photons also blur images, which is why the collimators and anti-scatter grids exist. The plate shows the total cross section against energy, with markers at typical medical energies.

Bhabha and Møller

Electron–positron scattering, {{tex:e^+e^- \to e^+e^-}}, has two diagrams: annihilation into a photon (s-channel) and photon exchange (t-channel). Homi Bhabha computed it in 1936. Because the t-channel blows up at small angles, its rate is huge and precisely known, and colliders use it to measure their own luminosity.

Electron–electron scattering, {{tex:e^-e^- \to e^-e^-}}, also has two diagrams, t and u, since the two identical outgoing electrons can be swapped. Christian Møller computed it in 1932. Fermi statistics (Chapter 61) put a minus sign between the two, and that sign is measured: Møller scattering at 90° is suppressed relative to what distinguishable particles would give.

Compton scattering in QED, the Klein–Nishina formula, the Thomson limit, Compton’s role in medical imaging and radiotherapy, and Bhabha and Møller scattering with their channels and signs.
