Textbook quantum mechanics describes isolated systems. A molecule in a protein is never isolated: it is coupled to thousands of vibrating atoms and water molecules, which constantly measure it, jostle it and drain its energy. The theory of open quantum systems describes a small quantum system plus a large environment that you cannot track. Its surprising lesson for biology: the environment is not only noise to be overcome. Sometimes it helps.

On the plate: energy moving between two molecules, with and without environmental noise.

System, bath, and master equations

Write {{tex:H = H_S + H_B + H_{SB}}}: system, bath (often modelled as many harmonic oscillators), and their coupling. The bath is summarized by a spectral density {{tex:J(\omega)}}, how strongly it couples at each frequency. Trace out the bath (Chapter 104). If the coupling is weak (Born approximation) and the bath forgets quickly compared with the system’s dynamics (Markov approximation), the system’s density matrix obeys the Redfield equation, and with one more approximation the Lindblad form, which Göran Lindblad proved in 1976 is the most general Markovian evolution preserving positivity:

{{texd:\dot\rho = -\tfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho] + \sum_k\Big(L_k\rho L_k^\dagger - \tfrac12\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\rho\}\Big)}}

Each jump operator {{tex:L_k}} is a channel: decay, dephasing, transfer to a sink. Established

Noise-assisted transport

Two sites: an excitation starts on site 1, and site 2 delivers it to a “sink” (a reaction centre, say) at rate 1; everywhere it can also be lost at rate 0.1. The sites are coupled with strength 0.5 but their energies differ by 4.0. Add pure dephasing at rate {{tex:\gamma = 10^x}}, {{tex:x =}} 0.6. With a large detuning and no noise, the excitation stays stuck on site 1 (efficiency 0.14); moderate dephasing broadens the levels until they overlap, and efficiency more than doubles, to about 0.34 near {{tex:\gamma\approx4}}; strong dephasing freezes motion (the quantum Zeno effect) and efficiency falls. Martin Plenio and Susana Huelga, Masoud Mohseni and colleagues, and Patrick Rebentrost and colleagues described this environment-assisted quantum transport in 2008–09. With zero detuning, noise only hurts. Established as theory; its biological importance is Debated (Chapter 167).

When Markov fails

In photosynthetic complexes, the protein’s vibrations have memory times comparable to the transfer itself, and the coupling is neither weak nor strong. Neither Redfield theory (weak coupling) nor Förster hopping theory (strong) applies. Yoshitaka Tanimura and Ryogo Kubo’s 1989 hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM) treat a harmonic bath exactly by tracking a hierarchy of auxiliary density matrices; Akihito Ishizaki and Graham Fleming used it in 2009 to model the Fenna–Matthews–Olson complex. Heinz-Peter Breuer and Francesco Petruccione’s textbook is the standard reference for the whole field. Established

What you now have

System–bath models and spectral densities, Born–Markov and Redfield equations, the Lindblad form, environment-assisted quantum transport and its optimum, and non-Markovian methods such as HEOM.

Three questions to carry with you.

What does the Markov approximation assume?tap
The bath forgets its interaction with the system faster than the system evolves.
How can dephasing help transport?tap
It broadens mismatched energy levels so they overlap, unblocking transfer.
Why does very strong dephasing hurt?tap
Constant effective measurement freezes the excitation in place: the Zeno effect.
