Every electron in the universe is exactly identical to every other: same mass, same charge, same magnetism, to as many digits as anyone has measured. Why? John Wheeler once suggested to Richard Feynman that there might be only one electron, zigzagging back and forth through time. The real answer is almost as striking. Every electron is an excitation of the same field, the electron field, which fills all of space. They are identical because they are ripples on one thing.

On the plate: a board of field modes. Add quanta to them, as bosons or as fermions.

States with any number of particles

Chapter 60 built single-particle states, {{tex:a^\dagger_{\mathbf k}|0\rangle}}. Apply more creation operators and you get two, three, any number of particles. The space of all such states, with any particle count, is Fock space, after Vladimir Fock (1932). A convenient basis lists how many quanta sit in each mode: {{tex:|n_1, n_2, n_3, \dots\rangle}}.

Choose or and a few times; to start again. The number operator {{tex:N = \sum_k a^\dagger_ka_k}} counts them. Because the field creates particles in modes, not as labelled individuals, swapping two particles gives nothing new: identity is built in. Established

Bosons: commutators

With {{tex:[a_k, a^\dagger_{k'}] = \delta_{kk'}}}, as for the oscillator, creation operators commute, {{tex:a^\dagger_1a^\dagger_2 = a^\dagger_2a^\dagger_1}}, so the two-particle state is automatically symmetric: bosons. Any number can share a mode, and putting the {{tex:n}}th quantum into a mode already holding {{tex:n - 1}} comes with a factor {{tex:\sqrt n}}. That factor is why stimulated emission and lasers work (Chapter 45): photons prefer modes already occupied.

Fermions: anticommutators

Now demand instead {{tex:\{b_k, b^\dagger_{k'}\} = \delta_{kk'}}} and {{tex:\{b^\dagger_k, b^\dagger_{k'}\} = 0}}, with {{tex:\{A, B\} = AB + BA}}. Then {{tex:b^\dagger_1b^\dagger_2 = -b^\dagger_2b^\dagger_1}}: swapping two particles flips the sign, so states are antisymmetric. And setting {{tex:k = k'}} gives {{tex:(b^\dagger_k)^2 = 0}}: no mode can hold two. The Pauli exclusion principle of Chapter 43 is now a one-line consequence of the algebra. On the plate, fermion modes refuse a second quantum.

Which particles get which rule

In 1940 Wolfgang Pauli proved that the choice is not free. If you quantize a spin-0 field with anticommutators, measurements at spacelike separation fail to commute, breaking causality (Chapter 60). If you quantize a spin-½ field with commutators, the energy has no lower bound: the vacuum would be unstable. Only integer spin with commutators, and half-integer spin with anticommutators, give a consistent relativistic theory. This is the spin–statistics theorem. It has no proof within non-relativistic quantum mechanics; it needs relativity. Established

DeeperIn two space dimensions the argument changes, and particles called anyons, whose exchange gives any phase {{tex:e^{i\theta}}}, are allowed. They have been observed in fractional quantum Hall systems (Part Four) and underlie proposals for topological quantum computing.
