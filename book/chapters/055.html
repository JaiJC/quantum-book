Try to pin an electron down more and more tightly. In ordinary quantum mechanics this costs momentum, by the uncertainty principle, and nothing worse. But squeeze it into a region much smaller than about 400 femtometres, its Compton wavelength, and the momentum spread carries enough energy to create a new electron and its antiparticle out of nothing. You do not get a better-located electron. You get three particles. Quantum mechanics and special relativity, taken together, will not allow a fixed number of particles. This chapter shows why, and why the answer is quantum field theory.

Natural units

Particle physicists set {{tex:\hbar = c = 1}}. Then energy, mass, momentum and inverse length all share one unit, usually MeV or GeV, and the single conversion you need is

{{texd:\hbar c = 197.327\ \text{MeV fm}}}

A mass of 197 MeV corresponds to a length of 1 fm. Convert 938 MeV into a length. Cross sections come out in {{tex:\text{GeV}^{-2}}}, and {{tex:1\ \text{GeV}^{-2} = 0.389}} millibarn. From here on, the book writes {{tex:m}} for {{tex:mc^2}} and {{tex:1/m}} for {{tex:\hbar/mc}} without comment. Established

Four-vectors and the metric

Gather time and space into {{tex:x^\mu = (t, \mathbf x)}} and energy and momentum into {{tex:p^\mu = (E, \mathbf p)}}. This book uses the metric {{tex:\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)}}, so

{{texd:p\cdot p = E^2 - |\mathbf p|^2 = m^2, \qquad p\cdot x = Et - \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}}

These products are the same in every inertial frame (Chapter 17). A plane wave {{tex:e^{-ip\cdot x}}} is then manifestly relativistic. The relation {{tex:E^2 = p^2 + m^2}} has two branches, {{tex:E = \pm\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}}}, drawn on the plate as a hyperbola above and its mirror below, separated by a gap of {{tex:2m}}. The lower branch will not go away.

A single relativistic particle leaks

The obvious way to make a relativistic Schrödinger equation is to keep only positive energies: {{tex:i\partial_t\psi = \sqrt{-\nabla^2 + m^2}\,\psi}}. Start a particle exactly at the origin and ask for its amplitude to be found at distance {{tex:r}} a time {{tex:t}} later. For {{tex:r > t}}, outside the light cone, it should vanish. It doesn’t: it falls as {{tex:e^{-m\sqrt{r^2 - t^2}}}}, small but not zero.

Set the mass to 1.0. The leak extends about one Compton wavelength {{tex:1/m}} beyond the light cone. A one-particle theory violates causality. In quantum field theory the same leak is cancelled exactly by the amplitude for an antiparticle to travel the other way, and measurements at spacelike separation commute (Chapter 60). Established

The price of confinement

Localize a particle to {{tex:\Delta x}}. Its momentum spread is at least {{tex:1/(2\Delta x)}} in natural units, and its energy spread rises with it. When that energy exceeds {{tex:2m}}, the particle and its surroundings have enough energy to create a particle–antiparticle pair. Set the confinement to 100 fm for an electron.

On this rough estimate the threshold is crossed near 70 fm, a fraction of the reduced Compton wavelength {{tex:\hbar/mc = 386}} fm; for the proton the same scale is 0.21 fm. The exact number depends on how you define the spread; the scale does not. Below it, “the position of one electron” stops being meaningful, because there need not be just one.

Why fields

Three facts now point the same way. Particle number is not conserved at high energy; colliders make particles by the dozen. Negative-energy solutions cannot be thrown away, because a consistent, causal theory needs them. And causality requires particles and antiparticles to cancel each other’s leaks. A theory with these properties has a field at every point of space, whose excitations are the particles and whose equations are relativistic. Particles become quanta of fields, as photons were already quanta of the electromagnetic field in Chapter 36. The next chapters build such theories, one equation at a time.

What you now have

Natural units and {{tex:\hbar c = 197.3}} MeV fm, four-vectors in the metric {{tex:(+,-,-,-)}}, the two energy branches, the causality leak of one-particle theory, the Compton wavelength as the scale of pair creation, and the case for quantum fields.
