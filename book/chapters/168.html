A fridge magnet’s field is about 5 millitesla. Earth’s is a hundred times weaker, and its energy for an electron spin is ten million times smaller than thermal energy. Chemists long assumed such fields could not affect chemical reactions. They can, through a mechanism that relies on the quantum evolution of two electron spins: the radical-pair mechanism. In chemistry it is firmly established, with well-understood theory and decades of measurements. It is also the leading explanation for how birds sense magnetic direction.

On the plate: a radical pair whose reaction yield depends on the direction of a weak magnetic field.

Singlets, triplets and hyperfine coupling

A light-triggered electron transfer can create two radicals, molecules each with one unpaired electron, born with their spins in a singlet state (total spin 0, Chapter 41). Many reactions can only recombine from the singlet; from a triplet they go elsewhere, to a different product. The pair’s spin state is not frozen. Each electron precesses in the external field and in the local magnetic fields of nearby nuclei, the hyperfine interaction, typically about a millitesla. If the two electrons feel different fields, their precession gets out of step and the singlet converts coherently into triplet and back. The fraction ending in each product depends on how fast that interconversion runs compared with the reaction rate, and that depends on the external field. Klaus Schulten and colleagues developed the theory in the 1970s; Ulrich Steiner and Thomas Ulrich reviewed magnetic field effects in chemistry in 1989. Established

A chemical compass

The plate simulates the simplest model exactly: two electrons and one nucleus (an 8-dimensional spin space), with an anisotropic hyperfine coupling to one electron, along a molecular axis. Field strength 50 µT, angle to the molecular axis 30°, hyperfine coupling 1.0 mT, and recombination rate 1.0 µs⁻¹. The singlet yield is computed from the eigenstates: {{tex:\Phi_S = \tfrac1M\sum_{m,n}|\langle m|P_S|n\rangle|^2\,k^2/(k^2+\omega_{mn}^2)}}. With Earth-strength fields it changes from 0.50 along the axis to 0.26 perpendicular: a compass. It needs the spins to stay coherent for around a microsecond, which electron spins in a protein plausibly can (Chapter 162). Make {{tex:k}} large and the effect vanishes: the pair reacts before the field can act. In 2008 Kiminori Maeda and colleagues showed a synthetic molecule responding to fields below 50 µT. Established in chemistry.

Why such a weak field works

The field does not need to compete with thermal energy, because it acts on spin dynamics, not on equilibrium populations. Thermal motion barely couples to electron spins; what matters is whether the Larmor precession, 1.4 MHz at 50 µT, has time to act before the pair reacts. The external field’s effect is comparable to the hyperfine interaction, a millitesla, so even 50 µT changes the interconversion noticeably. At very low fields there is an unusual “low-field effect”, where a weak field can increase singlet–triplet mixing by lifting degeneracies. The size and direction of these effects are calculable, testable and observed. Established
