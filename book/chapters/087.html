Inside ATLAS and CMS, bunches of protons cross forty million times a second, and each crossing produces dozens of collisions. Recording all of it would mean storing about a petabyte every second. So within four millionths of a second, custom electronics decide whether each crossing is worth a second look, and within a fraction of a second more, computer farms decide whether to keep it. About one crossing in ten thousand is written to disk. Everything else, including any physics no one thought to look for, is gone forever. This chapter is about how a particle detector sees, and what it chooses to remember.

Layers, and what each sees

A general-purpose detector is an onion. Innermost, a silicon tracker records the path of every charged particle in a magnetic field. Next, an electromagnetic calorimeter absorbs electrons and photons; then a hadronic calorimeter absorbs protons, pions and neutrons; outermost, muon chambers catch the muons that pass through everything. Momentum from curvature

A charged track in field {{tex:B}} curves with radius {{tex:R}}; its momentum across the field is {{tex:p_T[\text{GeV}] = 0.3\,B[\text{T}]\,R[\text{m}]}}. High-momentum tracks are nearly straight, so precision depends on measuring a tiny sagitta: the resolution worsens in proportion to {{tex:p}} and improves as {{tex:BL^2}}, with {{tex:L}} the track length. That is why CMS chose a 4-tesla solenoid, and ATLAS a large volume. Set the momentum to 5.0 GeV in a 4 T field.

How particles lose energy, and proton therapy

A charged particle crossing matter ionizes atoms along its path. Hans Bethe’s formula, refined by Felix Bloch, gives the loss per distance: it rises steeply as the particle slows, roughly as {{tex:1/\beta^2}}. So a proton deposits little energy where it enters, and most of it just before it stops: the Bragg peak.

That shape is the reason for proton therapy. Choose the beam energy, 150 MeV, and the peak moves to a depth in water (and roughly in tissue) set by it: about 16 cm at 150 MeV. Tumour gets the peak; tissue beyond it gets almost nothing, unlike X-rays, which deposit dose all the way through. Established

Showers

A high-energy electron in dense material radiates photons; each photon makes an electron–positron pair; each of those radiates. The cascade doubles roughly every radiation length {{tex:X_0}}, 0.56 cm in lead and 0.89 cm in the lead tungstate crystals of the CMS calorimeter, until the particles fall below about 10 MeV and stop. The total light collected measures the original energy, with a resolution that improves as {{tex:1/\sqrt E}}. Hadrons shower too, over the longer nuclear interaction length, about 17 cm in iron, and less precisely, because some energy is lost breaking nuclei.

The trigger

Forty million crossings a second, a first hardware decision in microseconds, about 100,000 kept for software, and about one to three thousand a second written out. Set a first-level threshold on the energy of the highest-energy object, 25 GeV. Lower it and the rate exceeds what the readout can handle; raise it and you lose Higgs bosons decaying to photons of about 60 GeV. Trigger design is a choice of what physics to make possible. Neural networks running on fast programmable chips now make some of these decisions within the microsecond budget.

What you now have

The layered detector and particle signatures, momentum from curvature and its resolution, Bethe–Bloch energy loss and the Bragg peak with its medical use, electromagnetic and hadronic showers, and the trigger chain from 40 MHz to kilohertz.
