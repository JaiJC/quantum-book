Turn a neutron through a full circle, 360 degrees, and it does not come back to itself. Its wavefunction has changed sign. Turn it a second full circle and it is back. This was measured in 1975, by splitting neutron beams in a crystal interferometer and turning one half in a magnetic field. The possibility follows from three commutators and nothing else, and this chapter derives it.

On the plate: an arrow turning, and beside it, the sign of a spin-½ state turned with it.

Everything from three commutators

Forget orbits and spherical harmonics. Define angular momentum as any three Hermitian operators obeying {{tex:[J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z}} and its cyclic partners (Chapter 29). Then {{tex:J^2}} commutes with each {{tex:J_i}}, so label states by {{tex:J^2}} and {{tex:J_z}}: {{tex:|j, m\rangle}}. Build the ladder operators {{tex:J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y}}. The commutators give {{tex:[J_z, J_\pm] = \pm\hbar J_\pm}}, so {{tex:J_\pm}} raise or lower {{tex:m}} by one, just like {{tex:a^\dagger}} and {{tex:a}} in Chapter 36.

But {{tex:J_z^2 \le J^2}}, so the ladder has a top {{tex:m = j}} and a bottom {{tex:m = -j}}, and you get from one to the other in whole steps. So {{tex:2j}} is a whole number. That allows {{tex:j = 0, \tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, \dots}}: half-integers are not forbidden. Choose {{tex:j =}} 1.5 to see the {{tex:2j + 1}} rungs. Established

Deeper{{tex:J_-J_+ = J^2 - J_z^2 - \hbar J_z}}. Applied to the top state, where {{tex:J_+|j,m_{\max}\rangle = 0}}, this gives the eigenvalue {{tex:\hbar^2 m_{\max}(m_{\max}+1)}}. Calling {{tex:m_{\max} = j}} gives {{tex:J^2 = \hbar^2 j(j+1)}}; the bottom gives {{tex:m_{\min} = -j}}.

How hard each step is

The size of each step is fixed by the algebra:

{{texd:J_\pm|j, m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m\pm1)}\;|j, m\pm1\rangle}}

The plate labels each rung with the factor for stepping up. Steps are largest in the middle of the ladder and vanish off the ends. From these numbers you can write down the matrices of {{tex:J_x}} and {{tex:J_y}} for any {{tex:j}}. For {{tex:j = \tfrac12}} they are the Pauli matrices of Chapter 8, times {{tex:\hbar/2}}.

The vector model

A state {{tex:|j, m\rangle}} has a definite length {{tex:\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)}}} and a definite {{tex:z}}-component {{tex:m\hbar}}, but its {{tex:x}} and {{tex:y}} components are uncertain. Picture it as a vector somewhere on a cone around the {{tex:z}}-axis. Choose {{tex:m =}} 1.5 for your {{tex:j}}.

The vector is longer than its largest projection, since {{tex:\sqrt{j(j+1)} > j}}, so it can never point straight along {{tex:z}}. It cannot, because that would make {{tex:J_x}} and {{tex:J_y}} both exactly zero, and they do not commute. The cone is a picture, not a mechanism; there is no hidden vector going round.

Rotations and the minus sign

Angular momentum generates rotations (Chapter 29): turning a state by angle {{tex:\theta}} about {{tex:z}} is {{tex:R(\theta) = e^{-i\theta J_z/\hbar}}}, which multiplies {{tex:|j, m\rangle}} by {{tex:e^{-im\theta}}}. For whole-number {{tex:m}}, a turn of {{tex:2\pi}} gives 1. For half-integer {{tex:m}}, it gives {{tex:e^{\mp i\pi} = -1}}.

Turn through 360°. The arrow is back after 360°. The spin-½ amplitude, drawn as a pointer turning at half the speed, is reversed, and needs 720° to return. A sign on its own is unobservable, but a sign relative to an unrotated partner is not.

Seeing the sign

In 1975 two groups, Samuel Werner’s in Missouri and Helmut Rauch’s in Vienna, split a neutron beam in two inside a single silicon crystal, passed one half through a magnetic field that turned its spin by a controllable angle, and recombined them. The interference pattern repeats only every 720° of turning, not every 360°; the measured periods agreed with 720° to within a few percent. The plate shows the expected intensity, {{tex:I \propto 1 + \cos(\theta/2)}}.

So the minus sign is physical. It is how fermions, all particles with half-integer spin, differ from bosons, and Part Five will show it is linked to the Pauli exclusion principle. Established

Two descriptions of turning

Ordinary rotations in space form the group SO(3) (Chapter 20). The {{tex:2\times2}} unitary matrices with determinant one form SU(2). Every rotation corresponds to two SU(2) matrices, {{tex:U}} and {{tex:-U}}. Integer spins see only SO(3). Half-integer spins see all of SU(2), the minus sign included. The quantum world turned out to use the larger group.

DeeperSU(2) is topologically a three-sphere, which is simply connected; SO(3) is the three-sphere with opposite points identified, so a loop of rotations through {{tex:2\pi}} cannot be shrunk to nothing, but one through {{tex:4\pi}} can. Dirac’s belt trick shows the same fact with a ribbon.

What you now have

The full spectrum of angular momentum from its commutators, half-integers included, ladder factors, the vector model, rotation operators, the 720° spinor and the neutron experiments that saw it, and SU(2) as the double cover of rotations.
