Someone hands you a qubit prepared in one of two states that are not orthogonal, say {{tex:|0\rangle}} or a state tilted 30° from it, each with equal probability. No measurement can always tell which. But you have choices. You can maximize your chance of guessing right. Or you can insist on never being wrong, at the price of sometimes answering “I don’t know”. These are different measurements, and neither is the textbook projective measurement in a fixed basis. Quantum measurement is richer than Chapter 27 let on.

On the plate: a discrimination game. Choose a strategy and play rounds.

POVMs

The most general measurement assigns to each outcome {{tex:i}} a positive operator {{tex:E_i}}, with {{tex:\sum_iE_i = \mathbb 1}}, and the probability of {{tex:i}} is {{tex:\mathrm{Tr}(\rho E_i)}}. This is a positive operator-valued measure. The {{tex:E_i}} need not be projectors, need not be orthogonal, and there can be more outcomes than the dimension. Every POVM can be realized as an ordinary projective measurement on a larger system, the qubit plus an auxiliary one, by Naimark’s dilation. So nothing new is assumed; it is projective measurement seen from inside a subsystem. Established

Weak measurement

Couple a system only slightly to a meter, so each reading is very noisy and barely disturbs the system. Averaged over many runs, a weak measurement gives the expectation value without collapsing the state. Yakir Aharonov, David Albert and Lev Vaidman showed in 1988 that if you also post-select on a later outcome, the average reading is a weak value, {{tex:\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle/\langle\phi|\psi\rangle}}, which can lie far outside the eigenvalue range: a spin-½ reading of 100. Weak values were used to map average photon trajectories in a double slit (Chapter 54) and to amplify tiny signals. What they mean physically is argued over. Debated
