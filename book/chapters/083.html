A strange quark is the lightest particle carrying strangeness, so the strong and electromagnetic forces cannot make it decay. Yet strange particles do decay, in about a ten-billionth of a second. The reason is that the weak force does not see the same quarks the strong force sees. The quarks with definite mass are tilted, slightly, relative to the quarks the W boson connects. The table of tilts is the CKM matrix, and one number in it, a single complex phase, is the only known source of matter–antimatter difference among quarks.

Two bases that don’t line up

The Yukawa couplings of Chapter 82 are matrices in generation space. Diagonalizing them to find the mass eigenstates requires different rotations for up-type and down-type quarks. The W, which couples an up-type to a down-type quark, then carries the mismatch:

{{texd:\mathcal L \supset \frac{g}{\sqrt2}\,\bar u_{iL}\gamma^\mu V_{ij}\,d_{jL}\,W^+_\mu}}

{{tex:V}} is the Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa matrix, unitary, 3 × 3. Nicola Cabibbo introduced the two-quark version in 1963, with an angle of about 13°, to explain why strange decays are about twenty times slower than non-strange ones. The plate shows the magnitudes {{tex:|V_{ij}|}}: nearly diagonal, with small, ordered leakage. Established

Why three generations allow CP violation

An {{tex:n \times n}} unitary matrix has {{tex:n^2}} parameters, but quark-field phases can absorb {{tex:2n - 1}} of them. What remains: {{tex:n(n-1)/2}} rotation angles and {{tex:(n-1)(n-2)/2}} complex phases. With two generations, one angle and no phase; with three, three angles and one phase. A complex phase in the couplings makes matter and antimatter behave differently. Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa saw this in 1973, when only three quarks were known, and so predicted a third generation. The bottom and top quarks were found in 1977 and 1995. They shared the 2008 Nobel Prize.

The unitarity triangle

Unitarity means columns of {{tex:V}} are orthogonal, for example {{tex:V_{ud}V_{ub}^* + V_{cd}V_{cb}^* + V_{td}V_{tb}^* = 0}}. Three complex numbers adding to zero form a triangle. Divided by {{tex:V_{cd}V_{cb}^*}}, its apex sits at {{tex:(\bar\rho, \bar\eta)}} in Lincoln Wolfenstein’s parametrization. Move the apex: {{tex:\bar\rho =}} 0.16, {{tex:\bar\eta =}} 0.35.

The world fit, from dozens of measurements of decay rates and asymmetries, puts it at {{tex:(0.159, 0.352)}}, the white dot. Its angles are about 23°, 66° and 92°. The triangle’s height measures CP violation; its area is proportional to the Jarlskog invariant {{tex:J \approx 3\times10^{-5}}}, the same for every one of the six possible triangles. That all the measurements meet at one apex is one of the Standard Model’s most demanding tests. Established

The GIM mechanism

Some decays that the CKM picture seems to allow are suppressed almost to nothing: the neutral kaon decays to two muons a million times more rarely than naive estimates. In 1970 Sheldon Glashow, John Iliopoulos and Luciano Maiani showed that if a fourth quark existed, its contribution in loops would cancel the up quark’s, leaving only a small term proportional to their mass difference. They predicted the charm quark and its mass, a few GeV. It was found in 1974 in the J/ψ (Chapter 78). Flavour-changing neutral currents remain strongly suppressed, which makes rare decays sensitive probes for new particles in loops.
