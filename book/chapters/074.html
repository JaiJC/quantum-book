Every force in the Standard Model comes from the same principle: gauge symmetry. It is usually called the deepest symmetry in physics. Strictly, it is not a symmetry at all. A true symmetry relates different physical situations that happen to behave alike, such as a ball here and the same ball over there. A gauge “symmetry” relates different descriptions of one and the same situation. It is a redundancy in the mathematics, and that redundancy is exactly what makes the forces consistent.

Global and local

A global symmetry acts the same way everywhere: turning every electron field’s phase by the same angle. By Noether’s theorem (Chapter 59) it gives a conserved charge, and it is physical in the sense that it relates distinct states. A local transformation can differ from point to point. Chapter 67 showed that demanding local invariance forces a gauge field {{tex:A_\mu}} and a covariant derivative {{tex:D_\mu = \partial_\mu + ieA_\mu}}, which compares the field at neighbouring points while correcting for the arbitrary local phase choices. A lattice picture

Put the matter field on the sites of a lattice: a phase arrow at each. Put the gauge field on the links: a phase {{tex:U = e^{ieaA}}} for each step between neighbours. A gauge transformation turns each site’s arrow by its own angle and adjusts each link to compensate. .

Every arrow and every link changes. But multiply the link phases around a closed loop, a Wilson loop, and the adjustments cancel in pairs. The plate prints the total phase around each small square, the plaquette: unchanged. Those loop phases encode the magnetic flux through each square (Chapter 42). They are physical; the individual link phases are not. Kenneth Wilson built lattice gauge theory on this in 1974, and it is how QCD is computed today.

Why it is a redundancy

Two field configurations related by a gauge transformation describe the same physical state: no experiment can tell them apart. So the space of field values overcounts reality, and the gauge transformations are the overcounting. Three signs of this: the photon has two polarizations, not four (Chapter 66); Gauss’s law is a constraint, not an equation of motion; and Elitzur’s theorem (1975) proves that a local gauge symmetry cannot break spontaneously, because there is nothing physical to break. When people say “the Higgs breaks the gauge symmetry” (Chapter 77), they are using a convenient shorthand for something subtler. Established

Then why does it matter?

Because the redundancy is the price of describing massless spin-1 particles with fields that behave simply under relativity. A spin-1 field naturally has four components; a massless particle has two states. Gauge invariance is what removes the extra two consistently, and in doing so it fixes how the field may couple to matter: through the covariant derivative, with a single universal charge. The structure of electromagnetism, the weak force and the strong force follows from asking which redundancy to build in. Hermann Weyl introduced the idea in 1929, in the form used here.
