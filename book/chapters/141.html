The surface code is robust and local, but wasteful: each logical qubit costs hundreds of physical ones, and the overhead grows with the square of the distance. Two very different escapes are being built. One uses cleverer codes whose checks reach across the chip, storing 12 logical qubits in 288 physical ones. The other stores a qubit in the many energy levels of a single oscillator, so that much of the protection comes from physics rather than numbers of qubits.

On the plate: the cost of storing 12 logical qubits, two ways.

Quantum LDPC codes

The surface code’s parameters are {{tex:[[\sim2d^2, 1, d]]}}: rate falling to zero. Classical LDPC codes (Chapter 136) achieve constant rate and growing distance. Their quantum versions were long elusive, because X and Z checks must commute. Jean-Pierre Tillich and Gilles Zémor’s 2009 hypergraph product combined two classical codes into a quantum one with constant rate and distance {{tex:\sqrt n}}. In 2022 Pavel Panteleev and Gleb Kalachev proved that good quantum LDPC codes exist, with both rate and relative distance constant, settling a twenty-year question; their constructions also underpin the NLTS proof (Chapter 129).

IBM’s 2024 gross code, a bivariate bicycle code, is {{tex:[[144, 12, 12]]}}: 144 data qubits and 144 check qubits hold 12 logical qubits at distance 12. Each check touches six qubits, two of them far away, so the chip needs long-range couplers. Protecting 12 logical qubits at distance 12 with surface codes would need about {{tex:12\times(2\cdot12^2 - 1) = 3444}} qubits. IBM’s roadmap targets “Starling”, 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates, by 2029. Established for the code; Speculative for the roadmap.

Cat qubits

A cat qubit stores 0 and 1 as coherent states {{tex:|\alpha\rangle}} and {{tex:|-\alpha\rangle}} of a microwave resonator (Chapter 36), stabilized by engineered two-photon loss. A bit flip needs the field to jump between two well-separated blobs, which becomes exponentially unlikely as the mean photon number {{tex:\bar n = |\alpha|^2}} grows, roughly as {{tex:e^{-2\bar n}}}. Phase flips grow only linearly with {{tex:\bar n}}. The noise becomes strongly biased, so a simple repetition code against phase flips suffices. Set {{tex:\alpha =}} 2.0 and see the Wigner function of the even cat {{tex:|\alpha\rangle + |-\alpha\rangle}}: two blobs, with interference fringes between them, negative in places, the mark of a non-classical state. In February 2025 AWS reported Ocelot, a chip with five cat data qubits in a repetition code. Established

GKP grid states

Daniel Gottesman, Kitaev and Preskill proposed in 2001 encoding a qubit in an oscillator as a grid of sharp peaks in position: {{tex:|0_L\rangle}} has peaks at even multiples of {{tex:\sqrt\pi}}, {{tex:|1_L\rangle}} at odd multiples. A small shift in position or momentum, the oscillator’s natural error, is detected by measuring position modulo {{tex:\sqrt\pi}} and undone. In 2023 Volodymyr Sivak and colleagues at Yale ran GKP error correction in a superconducting cavity that extended the qubit’s lifetime beyond the best uncorrected encoding in the same system by a factor of 2.27: beyond break-even.
