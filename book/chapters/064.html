In 1949 Richard Feynman began drawing little pictures: straight lines for electrons, wiggly ones for photons, meeting at points. Each picture is shorthand for one term in the Dyson series, one complete pairing from Wick’s theorem, and it can be translated line by line into an integral. Within a decade the pictures had spread through physics, carried, as the historian David Kaiser tells it, by young physicists who learned them in person. They are now the most successful calculating tool science has.

Counting powers

Every vertex brings a power of the coupling. Tree diagrams are the lowest order; each loop adds more vertices. For QED, with coupling {{tex:\alpha \approx 1/137}}, each loop costs about a factor of {{tex:\alpha/\pi \approx 0.002}}, so a handful of loops gives ten-digit accuracy (Chapter 72). For the strong force at low energies the coupling is near one, every loop matters as much as the last, and diagrams fail; Chapter 79 explains why.

What virtual particles are not

An internal line has a four-momentum that does not satisfy {{tex:p^2 = m^2}}: it is “off shell”. It is called a virtual particle, and popular accounts say virtual particles pop in and out of existence, borrowing energy for a short time. That is a misleading picture. Internal lines are terms in a perturbative expansion of an amplitude; they are not objects that exist between the vertices, and no measurement detects one. A different choice of expansion gives different diagrams for the same physics. What is real is the amplitude, and the forces, shifts and rates it predicts. Established, though the language is still used loosely, including in this book.
