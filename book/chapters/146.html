Focus a laser tightly and its light pulls an atom toward the brightest spot: an optical tweezer. Split the beam into thousands of spots and you have thousands of individually held atoms, arranged in any pattern you like, inside a vacuum chamber at room temperature. Only the atoms are cold. Excite two of them to giant “Rydberg” states, and they interact strongly across several micrometres. In five years, neutral atoms went from curiosity to the platform with the largest qubit arrays and some of the most advanced error-correction demonstrations.

Rydberg atoms

A Rydberg atom has one electron excited to a very high principal quantum number {{tex:n}}, around 50 to 100. From the hydrogen-like scaling of Chapter 38, its orbit radius grows as {{tex:n^2}}, its dipole moment as {{tex:n^2}}, and its lifetime as about {{tex:n^3}}. Two Rydberg atoms interact by the van der Waals energy {{tex:C_6/r^6}}, with {{tex:C_6\propto n^{11}}}: enormous. For rubidium in the 70S state, {{tex:C_6/h \approx 863}} GHz µm⁶. Qubits are stored in two ground hyperfine levels, which live for seconds, and briefly excited to the Rydberg state to interact. Established

Blockade

Drive two atoms toward the Rydberg state with Rabi frequency {{tex:\Omega}}. If both were excited, their interaction would shift the doubly-excited energy far off resonance. Within the blockade radius

{{texd:R_b = \Big(\frac{C_6}{\hbar\Omega}\Big)^{1/6}}}

only one can be excited. Dieter Jaksch and colleagues proposed in 2000 using this for a controlled-phase gate. Set the site spacing to 6.0 µm and {{tex:\Omega/2\pi =}} 2.0 MHz. Circles show each atom’s blockade radius; lines join blockaded pairs. Tap sites to rearrange the array, as experiments do with moving tweezers before every run. Modern gates use global laser pulses on all atoms at once, with timing and phases designed so that pairs within the blockade acquire a CZ phase; Simon Evered and colleagues reached 99.5% fidelity in 2023. Moving qubits, losing atoms

Tweezers can carry atoms around mid-computation, so connectivity is reconfigurable. Dolev Bluvstein and colleagues at Harvard, MIT and QuEra used this in 2024 to run algorithms on up to 48 logical qubits, moving whole code blocks in parallel. In 2025 Manuel Endres’s group at Caltech trapped 6,100 atomic qubits with coherence times of 12.6 seconds, and the Harvard–MIT–QuEra team demonstrated a 448-atom fault-tolerant architecture operating below threshold. The characteristic error is atom loss: an atom simply disappears. Because loss can be detected, it can be treated as an erasure, an error at a known location, which codes correct far more easily than unknown errors. The drawback is speed: cycles take around a millisecond. Established
