In 1962, at a conference at CERN, Murray Gell-Mann looked at nine known heavy baryons arranged in a triangle, the decuplet of Chapter 73. One corner was empty. From the pattern he predicted a particle with charge −1, three units of strangeness, and a mass about 1,680 MeV, and said how it would decay. In 1964 a bubble chamber at Brookhaven photographed one, at 1,672 MeV. The Ω⁻ turned an arrangement of particles into a theory of their constituents: quarks.

Quarks as building blocks

In 1964 Gell-Mann and, independently, George Zweig proposed that hadrons are made of three types of constituent with fractional charges: up ({{tex:+\tfrac23}}), down ({{tex:-\tfrac13}}) and strange ({{tex:-\tfrac13}}, strangeness {{tex:-1}}). Mesons are a quark and an antiquark; baryons are three quarks, with baryon number {{tex:\tfrac13}} each.

Isospin and the multiplets

Up and down quarks have nearly equal masses, so the strong force treats them almost alike. Swapping them is the approximate symmetry isospin, SU(2), introduced by Heisenberg for the proton and neutron in 1932. Adding the strange quark extends it to the approximate SU(3) of Chapter 73. The decuplet’s rows differ by one strange quark each, and the strange quark is about 150 MeV heavier, so the masses step evenly: Δ 1232, Σ* 1385, Ξ* 1530 MeV. The next step gives about 1,675 MeV for the Ω⁻; it was found at 1,672.

A ratio from symmetry alone

Treat the proton as uud and the neutron as udd, with spins combined to total spin ½ (Chapter 41), and give each quark a magnetic moment proportional to its charge. The ratio of the neutron’s moment to the proton’s comes out with no adjustable parameters:

{{texd:\frac{\mu_n}{\mu_p} = -\frac23 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\mu_p}{\mu_n} = -\frac32}}

Measured: {{tex:\mu_p/\mu_n = -1.460}}, within 3%. This success in 1964–65 was one of the first signs that quarks were more than bookkeeping.

Seeing inside the proton

Were quarks real particles? In 1968 the SLAC–MIT experiment fired electrons of 7 to 17 GeV at protons and measured how often they scattered at large angles. Like Rutherford’s gold foil (Chapter 48), the rate was far higher than a smooth proton would give, and it depended on energy and angle only through one ratio, as if the electrons were hitting point-like parts. Richard Feynman called them partons; they turned out to be quarks and gluons. Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall and Richard Taylor won the 1990 Nobel Prize.

Beyond three quarks

QCD allows any colourless combination. In 2003 Belle found the X(3872), which fits no simple quark–antiquark picture; in 2015 LHCb reported states containing five quarks, pentaquarks, decaying to a J/ψ and a proton. Dozens of such “exotic” hadrons are now known. Whether each is a compact object of four or five quarks or a loose molecule of two ordinary hadrons is argued case by case. Established that they exist; Debated what they are.

What you now have

The quark model and quark quantum numbers, mesons and baryons, isospin and SU(3) multiplets, the Ω⁻ prediction, the magnetic-moment ratio, deep inelastic scattering, and exotic hadrons.
