Part 4 asks whether living things use quantum mechanics in ways beyond the ordinary chemistry every molecule relies on. Before any claim can be judged, you need the stage on which life plays out: warm, wet, crowded and noisy. Every protein and membrane in your body is jostled constantly by water molecules carrying a thermal energy of about 27 milli-electronvolts. Any quantum effect that matters biologically must survive that jostling. This chapter sets out the energy scales and forces that decide what can.

On the plate: the energy ladder of life, from Earth’s magnetic field to a visible photon.

Two kinds of quantum effect

Neill Lambert and colleagues drew a line in 2013 that runs through all of Part 4. Trivial quantum effects are the ordinary quantum mechanics behind all chemistry: why bonds form, why chlorophyll is green, why DNA bases pair. No one doubts these. Non-trivial effects are cases where coherence, entanglement or tunnelling does a specific biological job that a classical description would miss. These are the interesting claims, and they range from well established (enzyme tunnelling) to highly contested (quantum effects in consciousness). Every chapter here says which kind it concerns, with a status label and the strongest objection. Established as a framework.

The energy ladder

At body temperature, 310 K, {{tex:k_BT = 26.7}} meV, or 2.58 kJ/mol. Compare: a hydrogen bond is worth about 5–30 kJ/mol, a few to ten {{tex:k_BT}}, so they form and break constantly, which is why proteins can fold and unfold and DNA can unzip. Hydrolysing ATP releases about 50 kJ/mol in the cell, some 20 {{tex:k_BT}}: enough to drive a molecular motor one step. A carbon–carbon covalent bond holds about 3.6 eV, so it never breaks thermally. A green photon at 500 nm carries 2.48 eV, enough to excite an electron. At the bottom, the Zeeman energy of an electron spin in Earth’s 50 µT field is about {{tex:2\times10^{-7}k_BT}}: absurdly small, which makes its role in bird navigation (Chapter 169) so surprising. Forces in water

Water is unusual: a small molecule with a large dipole, forming a dynamic hydrogen-bonded network, with a dielectric constant of about 80 that weakens electrostatic forces eightyfold. Dissolved ions screen charges further: a charge’s field falls off as {{tex:e^{-r/\lambda_D}/r}}, with the Debye length {{tex:\lambda_D\approx0.304/\sqrt{I}}} nm for ionic strength {{tex:I}} in mol/L. Set {{tex:I =}} 0.150 M; physiological saline, 150 mM, gives under a nanometre. Electrostatic interactions in cells are therefore short-ranged. Van der Waals forces, from correlated fluctuations of electron clouds (a trivial quantum effect), hold nonpolar groups together, and the hydrophobic effect, water’s preference to keep its hydrogen-bond network intact, drives proteins to bury their oily residues and membranes to form. Rob Phillips, Jané Kondev and Julie Theriot’s Physical Biology of the Cell and Philip Nelson’s Biological Physics develop all of this. Established
