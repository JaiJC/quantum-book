Why rereading fails

The most popular way to study is to read the material again. It feels like learning, because the words become familiar. Familiar is not the same as remembered.

In 2013 John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed hundreds of studies of ten common study techniques. Two came out on top: practice testing, meaning pulling an answer out of your own memory, and distributed practice, meaning spreading reviews out over days. Rereading and highlighting came near the bottom. Established

Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke showed the effect cleanly in 2006. Students who tested themselves on a passage remembered more of it a week later than students who spent the same time rereading it, even though the rereaders felt more confident. Established

Thinking with units

The second tool in this book costs nothing and catches most mistakes. Every physical quantity carries units, and an equation is only possible if the units on both sides match.

Take a pendulum. Its period, the time for one full swing, can only depend on things you could change: the length of the string {{tex:L}}, the strength of gravity {{tex:g}}, and the mass of the bob {{tex:m}}. Suppose {{tex:T \propto L^{a}\, g^{b}\, m^{c}}}. A period is measured in seconds, so the powers must cancel every unit except seconds.

Find the powers. Length to the power 1.0, gravity to the power 1.0, mass to the power 1.0.

The plate shows each unit’s leftover power. Make metres and kilograms vanish, and leave seconds to the first power.

The answer, and a surprise

The only choice that works is {{tex:a = \tfrac12}}, {{tex:b = -\tfrac12}}, {{tex:c = 0}}, so {{tex:T \propto \sqrt{L/g}}}. The full calculation adds a factor of {{tex:2\pi}} that units alone cannot give you:

{{texd:T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}}}

Set the string to 1.00 m and it swings once every 2.01 s. A metre-long pendulum ticks almost exactly every two seconds, which is why grandfather clocks are about that tall.

Look at the mass: its power is zero. The period does not care how heavy the bob is, for the same reason the hammer and the feather fell together. Units told you something true about nature before you did any physics.

Estimating anything

The third tool is the Fermi estimate, named after Enrico Fermi, who liked to ask questions such as how many piano tuners work in Chicago. You will rarely know the answer, but you can almost always get within a factor of ten.

How many atoms are in one of your cells? A typical cell is about 10 µm across. Treat it as a cube of water: volume {{tex:d^3}}, a thousand kilograms per cubic metre, eighteen grams per mole of water, three atoms per molecule, and {{tex:6\times10^{23}}} molecules per mole.

The answer comes out at about 10^14 atoms: a hundred trillion. The plate puts it on a ladder of powers of ten, beside the number of people on Earth and the stars in our galaxy.

The smallest length

Units can also point at physics nobody has reached. Quantum mechanics brings in Planck’s constant {{tex:\hbar}}, gravity brings Newton’s {{tex:G}}, and relativity brings the speed of light {{tex:c}}. There is exactly one way to combine them into a length:

{{texd:\ell_P = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^{3}}} \approx 1.6\times10^{-35}\ \text{m}}}

That is the Planck length, about twenty powers of ten smaller than a proton. The number itself is certain. What it means is not: many physicists expect space and time to stop behaving smoothly near this scale, but no experiment has come close to testing that. Debated

DeeperCheck the units yourself. {{tex:\hbar}} is in joule-seconds, {{tex:G}} in {{tex:\text{m}^3\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-2}}}, and {{tex:c}} in metres per second. Writing a joule as {{tex:\text{kg}\,\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2}}}, the product {{tex:\hbar G/c^3}} comes out in square metres.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why does rereading feel effective even when it is not?tap
Rereading makes the words familiar, and familiarity feels like knowing. Recalling without looking is what actually strengthens memory.
Why does a pendulum’s period not depend on the mass of the bob?tap
No other quantity in the problem carries kilograms, so mass cannot appear in a formula for a time. Physically, a heavier bob feels a larger pull but is just as much harder to move.
What is a Fermi estimate for?tap
Getting the right power of ten from rough, honest guesses, so you know whether an answer is plausible before you calculate it exactly.
