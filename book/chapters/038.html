Heat hydrogen gas and look at its glow through a prism: a red line, a blue-green one, two violet ones, at wavelengths measured in the nineteenth century to a few parts in a hundred thousand. Solve one equation, the Schrödinger equation with the Coulomb force, and those wavelengths come out to the same precision. Then look harder, and the lines turn out to be split by tiny amounts the equation does not predict. One of those splittings, measured in 1947, launched quantum electrodynamics.

The energies

Put {{tex:V = -e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}} into the radial equation of Chapter 37. Use the Bohr radius {{tex:a_0 = 0.529}} Å as the unit of length. Far out, {{tex:u \sim e^{-\kappa r}}}; near the nucleus, {{tex:u \sim r^{\ell+1}}}. What is left is again a power series, and once more it must stop or the wavefunction cannot be normalized. It stops only when {{tex:\kappa a_0 = 1/n}} for a whole number {{tex:n > \ell}}:

{{texd:E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}}}

with {{tex:\ell = 0, 1, \dots, n-1}} and {{tex:m = -\ell, \dots, \ell}}. That is Bohr’s formula of Chapter 24, now with a real wavefunction behind it. The energy depends only on {{tex:n}}. Counting {{tex:\ell}} and {{tex:m}}, level {{tex:n}} holds {{tex:n^2}} states, more than the {{tex:2\ell + 1}} rotation alone requires. How far out is the electron?

The probability of finding the electron between {{tex:r}} and {{tex:r + dr}} is {{tex:r^2|R(r)|^2dr}}: the {{tex:r^2}} counts the area of the shell. The plate plots it for the state you chose. For the ground state the peak is at exactly {{tex:a_0}}, Bohr’s radius, but the average is {{tex:\tfrac32 a_0}}, because the tail reaches out.

In general {{tex:\langle r\rangle = \tfrac12[3n^2 - \ell(\ell+1)]\,a_0}}: atoms grow as {{tex:n^2}}. A hydrogen atom with {{tex:n = 100}}, a Rydberg atom, is about a micrometre across, the size of a bacterium, and such atoms are now used as sensitive detectors of electric fields and as qubits.

The virial theorem

For any potential that goes as a power of {{tex:r}}, the averages of kinetic and potential energy are locked together. For {{tex:1/r}}, {{tex:2\langle T\rangle = -\langle V\rangle}}. So {{tex:\langle T\rangle = -E_n}} and {{tex:\langle V\rangle = 2E_n}}. In the ground state the electron has 13.6 eV of kinetic energy and −27.2 eV of potential energy. Binding an electron more tightly makes it move faster, not slower.

For {{tex:V \propto r^k}}, {{tex:\mathbf r\cdot\nabla V = kV}}, so {{tex:2\langle T\rangle = k\langle V\rangle}}; {{tex:k = -1}} for Coulomb, {{tex:k = 2}} for the oscillator.

The spectrum, predicted

A jump from level {{tex:n_2}} down to {{tex:n_1}} emits a photon with {{tex:1/\lambda = R_H(1/n_1^2 - 1/n_2^2)}}. Choose the lower level: (ultraviolet), (visible) or (infrared).

With the reduced mass of the electron and proton, the predicted red Balmer line is at 656.47 nm in vacuum; it is measured at 656.46 nm. They differ by about one part in fifty thousand, and that small remainder is real physics, the subject of the next passage.

The splitting that launched QED

Looked at closely, the {{tex:n = 2}} level is not one level. Relativity and the electron’s spin, which Dirac’s equation of 1928 includes, split it: the {{tex:2P_{3/2}}} states sit about 11 GHz above the rest. Dirac’s theory says {{tex:2S_{1/2}}} and {{tex:2P_{1/2}}} should stay exactly together.

In 1947 Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford shone microwaves on hydrogen and found that they do not. {{tex:2S_{1/2}}} lies about 1058 MHz higher. Explaining that Lamb shift, which comes from the electron’s interaction with the fluctuating vacuum electromagnetic field, forced the development of quantum electrodynamics by Bethe, Feynman, Schwinger and Tomonaga. Part Five derives it. Established

How big is a proton?

Because s electrons reach the nucleus (Chapter 37), hydrogen’s levels depend slightly on the proton’s size. In 2010 Randolf Pohl’s team replaced the electron with a muon, 207 times heavier, which orbits 200 times closer and feels the proton’s size far more. They found a radius of 0.842 fm, against 0.877 fm from ordinary hydrogen and electron scattering: a gap of more than five standard deviations, later seven.

Since then, most new measurements on ordinary hydrogen and new electron-scattering experiments have found the smaller value, and CODATA now recommends 0.841 fm. A few older results still disagree, and some tension remains in the data. Debated, leaning toward resolved, with the smaller radius.
