A single charged atom, held in empty space by oscillating electric fields, is about as perfect a qubit as nature offers. Every ion of a given isotope is identical, its internal states can live for minutes or longer, and lasers can control it with extraordinary precision. Trapped ions hold the records for gate fidelity. Their weakness is speed: gates take tens to hundreds of microseconds, a thousand times slower than superconducting circuits.

On the plate: the stability map of an ion trap.

The Paul trap

A static electric field cannot trap a charge in three dimensions (Earnshaw’s theorem). Wolfgang Paul’s solution, which shared the 1989 Nobel Prize, is a saddle-shaped potential that rotates at radio frequency: the ion feels a force alternately toward and away from the centre, and on average is pushed in. Its motion obeys the Mathieu equation {{tex:\ddot x + (a - 2q\cos2\tau)x = 0}}, with {{tex:a}} set by the static voltage and {{tex:q}} by the RF voltage. Only some {{tex:(q, a)}} give bounded motion. Choose {{tex:q =}} 0.30, {{tex:a =}} 0.00. The plate integrates one period of the equation and checks the monodromy matrix: motion is stable if its trace is less than 2 in magnitude. The ion’s slow average oscillation, the secular motion, is what gates use as a quantum bus. Established

Qubits and the Mølmer–Sørensen gate

Hyperfine qubits use two ground-state levels split by gigahertz microwaves; they essentially never decay. Optical qubits use a long-lived excited state reached by a laser. Ignacio Cirac and Peter Zoller proposed in 1995 using shared motion to couple ions. Klaus Mølmer and Anders Sørensen’s 1999 gate is more forgiving: laser beams detuned by {{tex:\pm\delta}} from a motional sideband push the ions’ shared motion around a loop in phase space. After time {{tex:2\pi K/\delta}} the loop closes, the motion returns exactly to where it began, so its temperature does not matter, and the spins have acquired a phase proportional to the enclosed area: an entangling {{tex:XX}} rotation. Loops: 1. The light’s momentum kick relative to the ion’s motional spread is the Lamb–Dicke parameter {{tex:\eta = k x_0}}, with {{tex:x_0 = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}}}. Established

Scaling: shuttling ions

A single chain of ions gets harder to control as it grows: motional modes crowd together. David Wineland and colleagues proposed the quantum charge-coupled device (QCCD) in 2002: many small trapping zones on a chip, with ions physically moved between memory and gate zones. Quantinuum’s machines use it, so any ion can interact with any other: all-to-all connectivity, at the cost of transport time. Its Helios system, announced in November 2025, holds 98 barium ions with two-qubit gate fidelity 99.921% and single-qubit fidelity 99.9975%. Other approaches link separate traps with photons (Chapter 153). Colin Bruzewicz and colleagues reviewed the platform in 2019. Established
