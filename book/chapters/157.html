Orotidine 5′-phosphate decarboxylase, an enzyme in the pathway that makes DNA’s building blocks, speeds its reaction by a factor of about {{tex:10^{17}}}. Without it, the reaction has a half-life of 78 million years; with it, milliseconds. How enzymes achieve such accelerations is one of the central questions of biochemistry, and one place where quantum tunnelling turns out to matter. First, the classical framework every claim must be measured against.

Michaelis–Menten

Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten proposed in 1913 that an enzyme E binds substrate S into a complex ES, which then forms product: {{tex:E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \to E + P}}. Assuming the complex reaches a steady state, the rate is

{{texd:v = \frac{V_\text{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]},\qquad V_\text{max} = k_\text{cat}[E]_0}}

At low substrate the rate rises linearly; at high substrate it saturates, every enzyme busy. {{tex:K_m}} is the substrate concentration giving half the maximum rate; {{tex:k_\text{cat}}} is the turnover number, reactions per enzyme per second; their ratio {{tex:k_\text{cat}/K_m}} measures efficiency. Some enzymes approach the diffusion limit, about {{tex:10^8}}–{{tex:10^9}} M⁻¹s⁻¹: they react with nearly every substrate molecule they meet. Established

The plate shows measured rates at ten substrate concentrations, with experimental noise. Adjust {{tex:K_m =}} 5.0 mM and {{tex:V_\text{max} =}} 14.0 µM/s to minimize the squared residuals. The best fit is near {{tex:K_m = 2}} mM, {{tex:V_\text{max} = 10}} µM/s, the values used to make the data. Older textbooks linearize with a double-reciprocal (Lineweaver–Burk) plot, which badly overweights the noisiest low-concentration points; direct nonlinear fitting is better. Established

Transition-state theory and its limits

Henry Eyring’s 1935 transition-state theory gives the rate of crossing an energy barrier:

{{texd:\begin{aligned}k &= \frac{k_BT}{h}\,e^{-\Delta G^\ddagger/RT}\\ &= \frac{k_BT}{h}\,e^{\Delta S^\ddagger/R}\,e^{-\Delta H^\ddagger/RT}\end{aligned}}}

Plot {{tex:\ln(k/T)}} against {{tex:1/T}}: the slope gives {{tex:\Delta H^\ddagger}}, the intercept {{tex:\Delta S^\ddagger}}. Set {{tex:\Delta H^\ddagger =}} 60 kJ/mol and {{tex:\Delta S^\ddagger =}} -50 J/(mol K). Enzymes lower {{tex:\Delta G^\ddagger}} by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate, positioning catalytic groups, and organizing electrostatics, as Alan Fersht’s textbook explains. But transition-state theory treats the reacting atom as a classical particle passing over the barrier. For light particles, especially hydrogen, it can pass through. When rates show weak temperature dependence or huge isotope effects, classical theory is not enough (Chapters 164–165). Established

What does K_m mean?tap
The substrate concentration at which the rate is half its maximum.
What does an Eyring plot give?tap
ΔH‡ from the slope and ΔS‡ from the intercept of ln(k/T) against 1/T.
Where does transition-state theory fail?tap
When light particles such as hydrogen tunnel through the barrier.
