Everywhere else in this book, hardware noise is the enemy: it limits circuits, demands error correction, and costs millions of qubits. Biology, though, happens in noisy environments, and simulating a molecule in a protein means simulating the noise too, which classically costs extra. Why not let the quantum computer’s own decoherence play the role of the protein’s? Several groups have tried, with some success. The idea is appealing and hard to make rigorous.

On the plate: matching a device’s noise to a target environment.

The idea

An open biological system evolves by a master equation with dephasing and relaxation (Chapter 161). A real qubit evolves by approximately the same kind of equation, with relaxation rate {{tex:1/T_1}} and pure dephasing rate {{tex:\gamma_\varphi = 1/T_2 - 1/2T_1}} (Chapter 135). If a simulated time step is mapped to a physical time chosen so that the qubit’s accumulated dephasing equals the target environment’s, the device’s own noise implements the bath. Groups simulating radical-pair dynamics on IBM processors reported that leaning on the native noise reproduced noisy quantum beats better than some simple classical approximations, with good agreement with experimental data. Debated

Match the bath

Device {{tex:T_1 =}} 100 µs and {{tex:T_2 =}} 80 µs; target dephasing over one simulated time unit 0.50. The plate computes the device’s pure dephasing rate and the physical idle time per simulated time unit needed to match. Then it adds the catch: device parameters drift by tens of percent over hours, and the noise spectrum is not the one the protein has. The target curve (white) and the device-emulated curve (amber, with its drift band) separate. Debated

The critique

Device noise is uncontrolled. It includes relaxation, dephasing, crosstalk, leakage and slow drift, with spectra set by fabrication defects, not by biology. Matching a single rate is easy; matching a structured, temperature-dependent spectral density with memory is not. And results can be hard to verify: if a noisy simulation agrees with experiment, is it because the noise models the protein, or because the observable is insensitive to the details? Controlled noise injection, as in the analog experiments of Chapter 195, is more defensible than relying on native noise. The approach is a clever use of imperfect hardware, not yet a reliable method. Debated

What you now have

The idea of using hardware decoherence as a simulated bath, how to compute a device’s pure dephasing rate and match a target, why drift and spectral mismatch limit it, and why controlled noise injection is preferable.
