Shine a dim blue light on a sheet of sodium and electrons leap out at once. Shine a red light a thousand times brighter and not a single electron escapes. Brightness, which is energy per second, does not decide whether electrons come out. Colour does.

In 1905 Einstein proposed that light itself comes in lumps, each carrying energy {{tex:hf}}. This chapter tests that idea with the photoelectric effect and with X-rays bouncing off electrons, and then asks a sharper question: do those experiments really prove that photons exist? Surprisingly, the photoelectric effect does not. Something else does.

The photoelectric lab

Light falls on a metal plate in a vacuum tube. Choose the metal: , or . Set the light’s wavelength to 450 nm and its brightness to 4.

Brightness changes how many electrons fly out. Wavelength changes how fast. Make the light too red for the metal and nothing comes out, however bright.

Einstein’s equation

Each lump of light gives all its energy to one electron. The electron spends some of it, the work function {{tex:\phi}}, escaping the metal, and keeps the rest as kinetic energy:

{{texd:K_\text{max} = hf - \phi}}

Below the threshold frequency {{tex:f_0 = \phi/h}}, no single lump has enough energy, and piling on more lumps one at a time does not help. Measure {{tex:K_\text{max}}} by the reverse voltage needed to stop the fastest electrons, and plot it against frequency: every metal gives a straight line with the same slope, {{tex:h/e}}. Robert Millikan, who expected to disprove Einstein, measured that slope in 1916 and got Planck’s constant to within about one percent. Established

X-rays bouncing off electrons

In 1923 Arthur Compton scattered X-rays off the electrons in graphite and found the scattered X-rays had longer wavelengths, by an amount depending only on the angle. Treat each X-ray as a particle with energy {{tex:hf}} and momentum {{tex:h/\lambda}}, collide it with an electron using the energy-momentum rules of Chapter 17, and you get exactly what he saw:

{{texd:\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}\,(1 - \cos\theta)}}

The constant {{tex:h/m_ec = 2.426}} pm is the electron’s Compton wavelength. Scatter at 90 ° and the wavelength grows by 2.43 pm. Light carries momentum in lumps, just as Einstein said. Established

DeeperWrite the four-momenta {{tex:p}} (photon in), {{tex:p'}} (photon out), {{tex:P}} (electron at rest), {{tex:P'}} (electron out). Conservation gives {{tex:P' = p + P - p'}}. Square both sides using {{tex:p^2 = p'^2 = 0}} and {{tex:P^2 = P'^2 = m^2c^2}}: {{tex:0 = p\cdot P - p'\cdot P - p\cdot p'}}. With {{tex:p\cdot P = mc\,E/c}} and {{tex:p\cdot p' = EE'(1-\cos\theta)/c^2}}, this rearranges to the Compton formula.

A surprise: this does not prove photons

In 1969 Willis Lamb and Marlan Scully showed that you can explain the photoelectric effect with light as an ordinary classical wave, provided the electrons in the metal are treated with quantum mechanics. The threshold, the linear dependence on frequency and even the absence of a delay all come out. So the photoelectric effect proves that something is quantized, but not necessarily the light. Established

What does prove it: one photon, one click

Send light at a half-silvered mirror with a detector on each output. A classical wave splits, and both detectors can click at the same moment. A single photon cannot split: it is detected at one output or the other, never both. Light from a source that emits one photon at a time should give no coincidences.

In 1986 Philippe Grangier, Gérard Roger and Alain Aspect did this with single photons from calcium atoms and found coincidences far below what any classical wave allows. Earlier, in 1977, Jeff Kimble, Mario Dagenais and Leonard Mandel had seen antibunching: photons from a single atom arriving more evenly spaced than random, impossible for a classical field. and compare the two detectors. Established

How many photons?

A green laser pointer of 5 milliwatts at 532 nm emits {{tex:P\lambda/hc \approx 1.3\times10^{16}}} photons every second. Lumps of energy are real, but in everyday light there are so many that the graininess is invisible.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why does brighter red light not free electrons from sodium?tap
Each photon has energy hf below the work function, and an electron absorbs one photon at a time.
Why doesn’t the photoelectric effect alone prove photons exist?tap
A semiclassical model, with classical light and quantized electrons, reproduces it (Lamb and Scully, 1969).
Which experiment shows light itself is quantized?tap
Single-photon experiments: no coincidences at a beam splitter (Grangier, Roger and Aspect, 1986) and antibunching (Kimble, Dagenais and Mandel, 1977).
