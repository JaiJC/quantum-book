The Schrödinger equation is perfectly reversible: run it backwards and every state returns to where it started. Yet an excited hydrogen atom in its 2p state decays to the ground state with a steady, irreversible half-life of about a nanosecond, like a radioactive nucleus. Where does a constant rate of decay come from, in a theory with no arrow of time? This chapter derives the answer, Fermi’s golden rule, and then shows where it breaks.

On the plate: the probability that an excited state is still excited, falling away as time passes.

Small pushes over time

Let a weak, possibly time-dependent term {{tex:V(t)}} act on a system whose states {{tex:|n\rangle}} you know. Write the state as {{tex:\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle}}, so that the known phases are taken out and the coefficients change only because of {{tex:V}}. This is the interaction picture, halfway between Schrödinger’s and Heisenberg’s (Chapter 31). To first order, starting in state {{tex:i}}:

{{texd:c_f(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t \langle f|V(t')|i\rangle\, e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt'}}

with {{tex:\omega_{fi} = (E_f - E_i)/\hbar}}. Iterating gives an infinite series, the Dyson series, which in Part Five becomes the expansion behind every Feynman diagram.

The sinc-squared peak

Switch on a constant coupling {{tex:V}} at {{tex:t = 0}}. The integral is elementary, and the probability of having jumped to {{tex:f}} is

{{texd:P_{i\to f}(t) = \frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{(\Delta/2)^2}, \qquad \Delta = \omega_{fi}}}

As a function of the energy mismatch {{tex:\Delta}}, this is a peak. Let time run to 10. The peak gets taller as {{tex:t^2}} and narrower as {{tex:1/t}}, so the area under it grows in proportion to {{tex:t}}. At short times, energy need not be conserved; at long times, only transitions to states of nearly the same energy survive.

Fermi’s golden rule

Now let the final states form a continuum, with {{tex:\rho(E)}} states per unit energy: the many directions and energies a photon could fly off with, for instance. Summing the peak over them, its growing area times the density of states gives a probability that grows steadily, at a constant rate:

{{texd:\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)}}

Dirac derived it in 1927; Fermi called it a golden rule. The plate tests it honestly. One level is coupled with equal strength to 101 evenly spaced levels, and the full Schrödinger equation is solved exactly. The survival probability, amber, follows {{tex:e^{-\Gamma t}}}, white, with {{tex:\Gamma}} from the golden rule. Then, at {{tex:t = 2\pi\hbar/\delta}}, where {{tex:\delta}} is the level spacing, the probability comes back. The decay was reversible all along; it only looks permanent when the levels are so dense that the return time is longer than the universe’s age. That is the answer to the opening question. Established

Einstein’s three processes

In 1916 and 1917, before quantum mechanics existed, Einstein showed that atoms in thermal radiation must do three things: absorb light, emit when light stimulates them, and emit spontaneously. Requiring Planck’s law (Chapter 22) fixed the ratio of the spontaneous rate {{tex:A}} to the stimulated coefficient {{tex:B}}, {{tex:A/B = \hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3}}. Stimulated emission, which copies the incoming photon exactly, is the principle of the laser.

The golden rule gives {{tex:A}} directly once light is quantized (Part Five): {{tex:A = \frac{4\alpha\omega^3}{3c^2}|\langle f|\mathbf r|i\rangle|^2}}. For hydrogen’s 2p state this is {{tex:6.27\times10^8}} per second, a lifetime of 1.60 ns, which matches measurement.

Which jumps are allowed

Light couples to an atom mainly through its electric dipole, {{tex:e\mathbf r}}. The matrix element {{tex:\langle f|\mathbf r|i\rangle}} vanishes unless the states have opposite parity and {{tex:\Delta\ell = \pm1}}, {{tex:\Delta m = 0, \pm1}}: the photon carries away one unit of angular momentum. These are the selection rules.

The plate shows hydrogen’s allowed jumps between the first three shells. The 2s state has nowhere to go: 1s has the same parity. So 2s lives about 0.12 seconds, a hundred million times longer than 2p, until it decays by emitting two photons at once. States trapped like this are called metastable, and the hydrogen 1s–2s transition, driven with two photons, is now one of the most precisely measured frequencies in physics.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why does the transition probability grow linearly in time?tap
The sinc² peak’s height grows as t² and its width shrinks as 1/t, so its area, summed over a continuum, grows as t.
Is spontaneous decay truly irreversible?tap
Not fundamentally; with discrete levels the probability revives after 2πħ/δ. With a true continuum the return never comes.
Why is hydrogen’s 2s state long-lived?tap
A single-photon dipole jump to 1s is forbidden (no parity change, Δℓ = 0), so it must emit two photons.
