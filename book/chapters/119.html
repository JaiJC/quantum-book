Grover’s search, Hamiltonian simulation, matrix inversion and phase estimation were discovered separately, over twenty years. In 2019 András Gilyén, Yuan Su, Guang Hao Low and Nathan Wiebe showed they are all one algorithm with different settings: choose a polynomial, and a sequence of simple phase rotations applies that polynomial to the singular values of a matrix. The framework, the quantum singular value transformation, has become the common language of quantum algorithms.

Quantum signal processing

Take a single-qubit “signal” rotation that encodes a number {{tex:x\in[-1, 1]}}:

{{texd:W(x) = \begin{pmatrix}x & i\sqrt{1-x^2}\\ i\sqrt{1-x^2} & x\end{pmatrix}}}

Interleave it with phase rotations {{tex:e^{i\phi_kZ}}}: {{tex:e^{i\phi_0Z}\prod_{k=1}^dW(x)e^{i\phi_kZ}}}. The top-left entry is a polynomial {{tex:P(x)}} of degree {{tex:d}}. Low and Chuang proved in 2016–17 that, with appropriate phases, {{tex:P}} can be essentially any polynomial of definite parity with {{tex:|P| \le 1}} on {{tex:[-1, 1]}}. All phases zero give the Chebyshev polynomial {{tex:T_d(x) = \cos(d\arccos x)}}. Established

Block encodings and QSVT

To act on a matrix {{tex:A}} rather than a number, embed it in the top-left block of a larger unitary, a block encoding, {{tex:U = \begin{pmatrix}A/\alpha & \cdot\\ \cdot & \cdot\end{pmatrix}}}, built for instance by linear combinations of unitaries (Chapter 118). Alternating {{tex:U}}, {{tex:U^\dagger}} and phase rotations on the ancilla then applies {{tex:P}} to every singular value of {{tex:A/\alpha}} at once. That is the QSVT. Its special cases: {{tex:P}} close to a step function gives search and amplitude amplification; {{tex:P(x) \approx e^{-ixt}}} gives Hamiltonian simulation with optimal cost; {{tex:P(x) \approx 1/x}} on the relevant range gives matrix inversion (Chapter 120); and phase estimation can be phrased the same way. John Martyn and colleagues called it a “grand unification” in 2021.
