Every operation you can perform on a single qubit, every gate in every quantum algorithm acting on one qubit at a time, is a rotation of the Bloch sphere. Nothing more. The Pauli X gate is a half turn about the x-axis; the Hadamard is a half turn about the diagonal between x and z; the T gate is an eighth of a turn about z. Learn to see gates as rotations and single-qubit circuits become geometry you can do in your head.

The Pauli matrices of Chapter 40 are gates: X flips {{tex:|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle}}, Z flips the sign of {{tex:|1\rangle}}, Y does both with a factor {{tex:i}}. The Hadamard {{tex:H = (X + Z)/\sqrt2}} turns {{tex:|0\rangle}} into {{tex:|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt2}}. The phase gates {{tex:S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)}} and {{tex:T = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})}} turn about z by 90° and 45°.

Every gate is a rotation

Any 2 × 2 unitary is, up to a global phase, a rotation by angle {{tex:\theta}} about some axis {{tex:\hat n}}:

{{texd:R_{\hat n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,\mathbb 1 - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}}

The half-angle is the spinor sign of Chapter 39: a {{tex:2\pi}} rotation gives {{tex:-\mathbb 1}}, invisible as a global phase but visible when the gate is controlled by another qubit. Any rotation can be built from rotations about just z and y, the ZYZ decomposition: {{tex:U = e^{i\alpha}R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)}}. The Hadamard, for example, is {{tex:R_y(\pi/2)R_z(\pi)}} up to phase.

Why these gates

H, S and the two-qubit CNOT generate the Clifford gates, which turn out to be easy to simulate classically (Chapter 108). Adding T, a rotation by an angle that is not a multiple of 90°, makes the set universal: any rotation can be approximated arbitrarily well by sequences of H and T (Chapter 100). In fault-tolerant machines, T gates are the expensive ones, needing “magic states” (Chapter 140), so algorithms are often costed by their T count.
