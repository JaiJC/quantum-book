No engine, however clever, can turn all of its heat into work. A power station running between steam at 550 °C and a river at 27 °C can never be more than about 64% efficient, even with frictionless parts and perfect insulation. That limit is a law of nature, and it was found by a young French engineer, Sadi Carnot, in 1824, before anyone knew what heat was.

Thermodynamics is the physics of energy when you cannot track every atom. Its laws also run every living cell, which survives by spending free energy. And quantum physics began as a thermodynamics puzzle.

Heat is energy

In the 1840s James Joule let falling weights turn a paddle wheel in an insulated tub of water and measured the water warming. A fixed amount of mechanical work always produced the same amount of heat: about 4.15 joules per calorie in his 1850 paper, against today’s 4.184. Established

So heat is a form of energy, and energy is conserved. That is the first law: the change in a system’s internal energy equals heat added minus work done, {{tex:\Delta U = Q - W}}. and watch the thermometer.

The direction of time

The first law allows heat to flow from cold to hot, as long as the energy adds up. It never happens. The second law forbids it: heat flows spontaneously only from hot to cold. Rudolf Clausius made this quantitative by defining entropy. When heat {{tex:Q}} enters a system reversibly at temperature {{tex:T}}, its entropy rises by {{tex:\Delta S = Q/T}}, and the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

Carnot’s engine

An engine takes heat {{tex:Q_h}} from a hot source, does work, and dumps heat {{tex:Q_c}} into a cold sink. Carnot imagined the best possible engine: a gas in a piston taken round a cycle of two isotherms and two adiabats, all done reversibly. The plate draws the cycle on a graph of pressure against volume; the enclosed area is the work per cycle.

Set the hot side to 800 K and the cold side to 300 K. The efficiency is

{{texd:\eta = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}}}

which is 62.5%. No engine working between these temperatures can do better, because a better one could be paired with a Carnot engine run backwards to move heat from cold to hot for free, breaking the second law.

DeeperAround a reversible cycle the entropy of the gas returns to its starting value, so the entropy taken from the hot source, {{tex:Q_h/T_h}}, equals the entropy given to the cold sink, {{tex:Q_c/T_c}}. Then {{tex:\eta = W/Q_h = 1 - Q_c/Q_h = 1 - T_c/T_h}}.

Free energy: what is actually available

Most chemistry and all of biology happen at fixed temperature and pressure, open to the surroundings. There the useful quantity is the Gibbs free energy, {{tex:G = H - TS}}. A process can happen on its own only if it lowers {{tex:G}}, and the drop in {{tex:G}} is the most work that can be extracted.

Your cells run on one such process: splitting ATP into ADP and phosphate. Under standard conditions it releases about 30.5 kJ per mole, roughly 0.32 electronvolts per molecule; inside a living cell, where ATP is kept far from equilibrium, the figure is closer to 50 kJ per mole. Muscles, nerve signals and the copying of DNA are paid for in this currency. Established

Relations for free

Because {{tex:U}}, {{tex:H}}, {{tex:F}} and {{tex:G}} are functions of the state, their mixed second derivatives must agree. That gives the Maxwell relations, which connect quantities that look unrelated. One of them, {{tex:(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V}}, lets you work out how entropy changes on expansion, which you cannot measure directly, from how pressure changes on heating, which you can.

DeeperFrom {{tex:dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV}}: {{tex:S = -\partial F/\partial T}} and {{tex:P = -\partial F/\partial V}}. Since {{tex:\partial^2F/\partial V\partial T = \partial^2F/\partial T\partial V}}, it follows that {{tex:(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V}}.
