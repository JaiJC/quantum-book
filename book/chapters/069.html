In the late 1930s, physicists computed the first correction to the electron’s properties from QED and got infinity. Not a large number: infinity. For a decade it looked as if quantum field theory was broken. It wasn’t. The infinities turned out to sit only in quantities that can never be measured on their own, and to cancel in every prediction that can. Learning to see that was the hardest step in twentieth-century physics, and it starts with one loop.

On the plate: two predictions that each depend on an arbitrary cutoff. Move the cutoff. Their difference does not move.

Where infinity comes from

A closed loop in a Feynman diagram carries a momentum {{tex:k}} that nothing fixes, so it is integrated over all values (Chapter 64). At large {{tex:k}}, propagators fall like {{tex:1/k^2}} or {{tex:1/k}}, while the integral {{tex:\int d^4k}} grows like {{tex:k^4}}. If the powers don’t win, the integral diverges at high momentum: an ultraviolet divergence, from arbitrarily short distances.

Count powers and you get the superficial degree of divergence {{tex:D}}. In QED, {{tex:D = 4 - E_\gamma - \tfrac32E_e}}, where {{tex:E_\gamma}} and {{tex:E_e}} count external photons and electrons. Only diagrams with few external lines can diverge: the photon’s self-energy ({{tex:D = 2}}), the electron’s ({{tex:D = 1}}), and the vertex ({{tex:D = 0}}). Three kinds of infinity, no more, however many loops you draw. Established

Taming the integrals

To handle a divergent integral, first make it finite, then see what survives. One way is a cutoff {{tex:\Lambda}}: stop at momenta above it. The modern way, introduced by Gerard ’t Hooft and Martinus Veltman in 1972, is dimensional regularization: compute in {{tex:d = 4 - 2\epsilon}} dimensions, where the integral converges, and watch the answer as {{tex:\epsilon \to 0}}. Divergences appear as poles, from the Gamma function’s {{tex:\Gamma(\epsilon) \approx 1/\epsilon - \gamma}}. It respects gauge symmetry, which a crude cutoff does not.

Two tools do most of the work. Feynman parameters combine denominators, {{tex:\frac{1}{AB} = \int_0^1\frac{dx}{[xA + (1-x)B]^2}}}, and a shift of {{tex:k}} then makes the integral spherically symmetric, and doable.

The vacuum polarization loop

A photon can briefly become an electron–positron pair and back. That loop, {{tex:\Pi(q^2)}}, diverges logarithmically. With a cutoff it reads, roughly, {{tex:\Pi(q^2) \approx \frac{\alpha}{3\pi}\ln\frac{\Lambda^2}{m^2}}} plus a finite function of {{tex:q^2}}. Move the cutoff to 10 (log₁₀ of {{tex:\Lambda/m}}).

The plate shows {{tex:\Pi}} at two momenta, {{tex:Q = 10m}} and {{tex:Q = 1000m}}. Both slide with the cutoff. Their difference stays fixed, at {{tex:\frac{\alpha}{3\pi}\ln(10^4) = 0.0071}} for large {{tex:Q}}. Measurable things, like how the strength of the force changes between two distances, are differences of this kind. The infinity is the same at every momentum, so it never enters a comparison.

The clue in the infinity

The divergent part of the photon loop has exactly the form of a term already in the Lagrangian, {{tex:F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}}}. So it can be absorbed into a redefinition of the electron’s charge. The divergent parts of the electron loops absorb into its mass and the normalization of its field. Nothing new is needed. The charge you write in the Lagrangian, the “bare” charge, is not the charge you measure, and only the measured one appears in predictions. That is renormalization, the next chapter. The infinity was telling us that the bare parameters were never observable.

What you now have

Ultraviolet divergences from loops, the superficial degree of divergence and QED’s three divergent functions, cutoffs and dimensional regularization, Feynman parameters, and the vacuum polarization loop with its cutoff-independent differences.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why do loop integrals diverge?tap
The loop momentum is unconstrained, and at large k the integrand does not fall fast enough to beat d⁴k.
Why is dimensional regularization preferred?tap
It keeps gauge symmetry, which simple cutoffs break.
Why doesn’t the cutoff affect predictions?tap
It enters only as a constant that is the same at every momentum, absorbed into the measured charge, mass and field normalization.
