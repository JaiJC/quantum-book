For two thousand years astronomers insisted that the heavens moved in perfect circles. Then Johannes Kepler spent years on Tycho Brahe’s measurements of Mars and found that its orbit was an ellipse, with the Sun not at the centre but at one focus. Why an ellipse, of all the shapes there are?

Isaac Newton answered with three laws of motion, one law of gravity and two conservation laws. This chapter rebuilds that answer, because quantum mechanics keeps the conservation laws even where it throws out the rest.

Three laws

A body keeps moving in a straight line at steady speed unless a force acts. A force changes its momentum at a rate equal to the force, {{tex:\mathbf F = d\mathbf p/dt}}. And forces come in pairs: if A pushes B, B pushes A equally hard the other way.

Gravity between two masses pulls with {{tex:F = GMm/r^2}}. Henry Cavendish measured the constant {{tex:G}} in 1798 with a torsion balance in a shed, and so, in effect, weighed the Earth: he got a density 5.45 times that of water, close to today’s 5.51. Established

Launch a planet

The plate is a small solar system. A planet starts at the right, moving straight up, with a speed of 1.00 times the speed for a circular orbit. .

At exactly 1.00 you get a circle. Slower, an ellipse with the launch point farthest out. Faster, an ellipse stretching away. Beyond {{tex:\sqrt2 \approx 1.41}} times circular speed the planet never comes back: it has reached escape speed, and the path opens into a hyperbola.

What stays the same

Watch the bars under the orbit. The planet speeds up near the Sun and slows far away, trading kinetic energy for gravitational energy, yet the total energy {{tex:E = \tfrac12 mv^2 - GMm/r}} never changes.

The second bar is the angular momentum, {{tex:L = mr\,v_\perp}}. Gravity pulls straight towards the Sun, so it cannot twist the orbit, and {{tex:L}} is fixed too. That single fact is Kepler’s second law: the line from Sun to planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The shaded wedges on the plate each take the same time.

Why an ellipse

With energy and angular momentum both fixed, the distance {{tex:r}} can be found as a function of the angle. The result is the equation of a conic section:

{{texd:r(\theta) = \frac{\ell}{1 + e\cos\theta}, \qquad \ell = \frac{L^2}{GMm^2}}}

The eccentricity {{tex:e}} is set by the energy: {{tex:e = 0}} is a circle, {{tex:e < 1}} an ellipse, {{tex:e = 1}} a parabola, {{tex:e > 1}} a hyperbola. An ellipse is what you get from an inverse-square force and nothing else. A force falling as {{tex:1/r^3}}, for instance, would make most orbits spiral in or fly away.

DeeperSubstitute {{tex:u = 1/r}}. Using {{tex:L = mr^2\dot\theta}} to replace time by angle, Newton’s radial equation becomes {{tex:d^2u/d\theta^2 + u = GMm^2/L^2}}: a simple oscillator equation in {{tex:\theta}}, whose solution is {{tex:u = (1 + e\cos\theta)/\ell}}.

Kepler’s third law

For any orbit, the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the average distance:

{{texd:T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,a^3}}

Put in the Sun’s mass and Earth’s distance and you get 365.25 days. Mars, 1.52 times further out, takes {{tex:1.52^{3/2} = 1.88}} years. Set an orbit size of 1.5 AU and its year is 1.84 years.

Collisions and rockets

When two bodies collide, the forces between them are equal and opposite, so the total momentum cannot change. If no energy is lost to heat, kinetic energy is conserved as well, and those two rules fix the outcome completely. Send a ball of mass 1.0 times the target’s into it. With equal masses the moving ball stops dead and the other leaves with all its speed, the trick of Newton’s cradle.

A rocket is a collision run backwards: throwing mass out the back pushes the rest forward. Momentum gives the rocket equation, {{tex:\Delta v = v_e\ln(m_0/m_1)}}. With exhaust at 4.4 km/s and a rocket that is 90% fuel, {{tex:\Delta v \approx 10}} km/s, barely enough for orbit. The logarithm is why rockets are mostly fuel.

Seeing the Earth turn

Newton’s laws hold in a frame that is not accelerating. The Earth’s surface is rotating, and in 1851 Léon Foucault showed it with a heavy pendulum hung from the dome of the Panthéon in Paris. The pendulum keeps swinging in one plane while the floor turns beneath it, so the plane appears to rotate, once every 32 hours at Paris’s latitude. Established

What you now have

Newton’s laws, gravity, and three conserved quantities: momentum, energy and angular momentum. In Chapter 12 you will see where those conservation laws really come from.

Three questions to carry with you.

Why is angular momentum conserved in a planetary orbit?tap
Gravity points straight at the Sun, so it exerts no twisting force (torque) about the Sun.
What decides whether an orbit is an ellipse or a hyperbola?tap
The total energy: negative means bound (ellipse or circle); positive means unbound (hyperbola).
Why are rockets mostly fuel?tap
The speed gained grows only with the logarithm of the mass ratio, so large gains need enormous ratios.
