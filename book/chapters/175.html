General anaesthesia has been used for 180 years, and how it works is still not fully understood. Xenon is a striking case: a noble gas that forms no chemical bonds, yet at high enough concentration it produces anaesthesia. Its isotopes are chemically identical. In 2018 a study found that mice needed more of the isotopes with nuclear spin than of the spin-zero ones to lose their righting reflex. If it holds up, chemistry alone cannot explain it, and a spin-dependent process must be involved. One study is not enough, and the chapter shows why.

Lipids or proteins?

Around 1900 Hans Meyer and Charles Overton independently found that anaesthetic potency tracks solubility in olive oil across a huge range of compounds: the Meyer–Overton rule, suggesting anaesthetics act by dissolving in membrane lipids. In 1984 Nicholas Franks and William Lieb showed that anaesthetics inhibit a lipid-free protein, firefly luciferase, with potencies following the same rule. The modern consensus is that anaesthetics act on specific proteins, notably GABA_A receptors (enhanced by most agents) and NMDA receptors (blocked by xenon and ketamine), by binding in hydrophobic pockets. That explains a lot, but not everything. Established

The isotope experiment

Na Li and colleagues exposed mice to xenon isotopes and measured the concentration at which half lost their righting reflex, the ED₅₀. Spin-zero isotopes (¹³²Xe, ¹³⁴Xe) gave an ED₅₀ near 15%; spin-active ones (¹²⁹Xe with spin ½, ¹³¹Xe with spin 3/2) near 22%. Choose or and a xenon fraction of 18.0%. Turin and colleagues had earlier reported in 2014 that anaesthetics change electron-spin signals in fruit flies. Jordan Smith, Christoph Simon and colleagues proposed in 2021 a radical-pair model in which the xenon nucleus’s hyperfine field alters spin dynamics in a reaction involving superoxide, reproducing the direction of the effect. What would make it convincing

The study used about 80 mice. Behavioural endpoints are noisy, isotopes come from different suppliers with possibly different trace contaminants, and a single laboratory’s result can reflect unrecognized procedural differences. Independent replication, with blinded isotope identity, purity analysis, a dose-response design powered to detect a 1.5-fold difference, and ideally a direct measurement of the proposed radical pairs, would be needed before the result reshapes anaesthesia theory. The radical-pair model also needs evidence that the relevant radicals form in neurons at sufficient rates. Speculative; it needs independent replication.

What you now have

The Meyer–Overton rule and the protein-target view, xenon’s NMDA action, the 2018 isotope study and its ED₅₀ values, the radical-pair model of xenon anaesthesia, and the standards for replication.
