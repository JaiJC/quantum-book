Is consciousness a quantum-gravity effect? Roger Penrose, who shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on black holes, argued in 1989 that human understanding is not computable, and so cannot come from a classical brain. With the anaesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff he proposed a mechanism: quantum superpositions in microtubules, protein tubes inside neurons, that collapse through gravity. The theory is called Orch-OR, orchestrated objective reduction. Most physicists and neuroscientists consider it very unlikely. This chapter states the claims precisely and shows how to judge them.

On the plate: the timescales at stake.

The claims

Orch-OR has separable parts. (1) Tubulin proteins in microtubules can exist in quantum superpositions of conformations, coherent across many tubulins and neurons. (2) Such superpositions collapse by a gravity-related process with time {{tex:\tau\approx\hbar/E_G}}, where {{tex:E_G}} is the gravitational self-energy of the difference between the superposed mass distributions (the Diósi–Penrose proposal, Chapter 54). (3) Each collapse is a moment of conscious experience, and the sequence is “orchestrated” by cell biology, at around 40 Hz. (4) This process is non-computable. Each part needs separate evidence. Decoherence against neural time

In 2000 Max Tegmark estimated decoherence times in the brain: about {{tex:10^{-13}}} to {{tex:10^{-20}}} seconds for superpositions of neuron firing states and of microtubule conformations, owing to collisions with ions and water. Neural processes take {{tex:10^{-3}}} to {{tex:10^{-1}}} seconds. The gap is ten orders of magnitude or more. Scott Hagan, Hameroff and Jack Tuszynski replied in 2002 that Tegmark’s parameters were wrong, for instance that ordered water and Debye screening could shield microtubules, and obtained {{tex:10^{-5}}} to {{tex:10^{-4}}} s. Even granting their assumptions, that is still shorter than the 25 ms Orch-OR needs. For the collapse time to be 25 ms, {{tex:E_G = \hbar/\tau}}: set {{tex:\tau =}} 10^-1.6 s. Speculative

New experiments, and what they show

In 2021 Sandro Donadi, Catalina Curceanu and colleagues searched for the faint radiation that Diósi–Penrose collapse would produce, deep under the Gran Sasso mountain, and ruled out the parameter-free version of the model; versions with an adjustable parameter survive. In 2024 Nathan Babcock, Philip Kurian and colleagues reported superradiance, collective enhanced emission, from networks of tryptophan residues in microtubules, a genuine collective quantum optical effect in a protein at room temperature, though not evidence of the coherent conformational superpositions Orch-OR needs. Also in 2024, Sana Khan, Michael Wiest and colleagues found that rats given epothilone B, a drug that stabilizes microtubules, took about 69 seconds longer to lose consciousness under isoflurane: interesting for anaesthesia mechanisms, but not a test of quantum effects. Separately, “quantum cognition” uses the mathematics of quantum probability to model human decisions, with no claim that the brain is quantum; do not confuse the two. Speculative for Orch-OR.
