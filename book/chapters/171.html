DNA’s bases pair through hydrogen bonds: guanine with cytosine, adenine with thymine. In 1963 Per-Olov Löwdin suggested that the protons in those bonds could tunnel across to the partner base, briefly turning each base into a rare tautomer that pairs with the wrong partner. If a replicating polymerase arrived at that moment, it could copy the mismatch: a spontaneous mutation, from quantum tunnelling. It is an arresting link between quantum physics, cancer and evolution. Sixty years on, the tautomers are known to exist; how much tunnelling contributes, and whether it matters for real mutation rates, is unsettled.

On the plate: a proton’s double well in a base pair.

Tautomers and mispairs

In the G–C pair, two protons sit in hydrogen bonds between the bases. If both move across together, a double proton transfer, the result is G*–C*, both in their rare tautomeric forms. Normally the canonical form is lower in energy by a few tenths of an eV, so the tautomer is rare and short-lived. If the strands separate while the tautomer persists, G* can pair with thymine instead of cytosine, a transition mutation after the next round of copying. In 2015 Isaac Kimsey, Hashim Al-Hashimi and colleagues used NMR relaxation dispersion to detect rare, transient Watson–Crick-like mispairs in DNA, formed by tautomerization or ionization, at populations around 0.1–1% and lifetimes of milliseconds or less. Established that such mispairs occur.

Two quantities matter: the equilibrium tautomer fraction, {{tex:\approx e^{-\Delta E/k_BT}}}, which tunnelling does not change; and how quickly that equilibrium is reached, which it does. In 2022 Louie Slocombe, Marco Sacchi and Jim Al-Khalili, modelling the G–C pair as an open quantum system, found that tunnelling makes the tautomer form within picoseconds and populate it far more than classical estimates. Debated

Does it cause mutations?

For a tautomer to become a mutation it must survive until a polymerase reads it, and the tautomer is also short-lived: if it decays back in picoseconds while strand separation takes much longer, the polymerase meets the canonical form. Some modelling suggests tautomers can persist through strand separation in some cases; others find they revert first. Polymerases also check base-pair geometry, and repair enzymes fix many mismatches. Spontaneous mutation rates, about {{tex:10^{-10}}} per base per replication in humans after proofreading and repair, have many contributing causes. Whether proton tunnelling contributes a measurable share is not known. Speculative for a role in real mutation rates.

What you now have

Löwdin’s tautomer hypothesis, double proton transfer in G–C, the experimental detection of transient mispairs, an open-system double-well model separating equilibrium populations from rates, and the gap between tautomer formation and mutation.
