So far particles were put into physics by hand: here is an electron, there a photon. Quantum field theory does something different. Take a field, the mattress of springs from Chapter 58, and quantize it the way Chapter 36 quantized a single oscillator. Each wavelength of the field is an oscillator; each rung of its ladder is one more quantum. Those quanta have definite energy and momentum, obey {{tex:E^2 = p^2 + m^2}}, and are absolutely identical. They are particles. Nobody put them in.

On the plate: a field made of many oscillator modes, one per wavenumber. Raise one by one rung and you have made one particle.

Fields as oscillators

Take the Klein–Gordon field and expand it in plane waves. Each Fourier component {{tex:\phi_{\mathbf k}}} obeys {{tex:\ddot\phi_{\mathbf k} = -(k^2 + m^2)\phi_{\mathbf k}}}: a harmonic oscillator with frequency {{tex:\omega_{\mathbf k} = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2}}}. A field is an infinite set of independent oscillators, one per wavenumber. Quantize each one with its own ladder operators (Chapter 36):

{{texd:\phi(\mathbf x) = \int\!\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf k}}}\left(a_{\mathbf k}\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} + a_{\mathbf k}^\dagger\,e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\right)}}

with {{tex:[a_{\mathbf k}, a_{\mathbf k'}^\dagger] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')}}, which follows from the canonical rule {{tex:[\phi(\mathbf x), \pi(\mathbf y)] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)}}, where {{tex:\pi = \dot\phi}} is the field’s momentum. Established

One rung is one particle

The energy becomes {{tex:H = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\omega_{\mathbf k}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf k}a_{\mathbf k}}} plus a constant. The state {{tex:a^\dagger_{\mathbf k}|0\rangle}} has energy {{tex:\omega_{\mathbf k} = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2}}} and momentum {{tex:\mathbf k}}: exactly a relativistic particle of mass {{tex:m}}. Raising the same mode twice makes two identical particles; raising different modes makes particles with different momenta.

An infinite constant

Each oscillator keeps its zero-point energy {{tex:\tfrac12\omega_{\mathbf k}}} even with no particles present, and there are infinitely many. Summed, the vacuum has infinite energy. For most of physics this is harmless: only energy differences matter, so we drop it by normal ordering, writing every {{tex:a^\dagger}} to the left of every {{tex:a}}.

Gravity, though, feels absolute energy. Cut the sum off at the Planck scale and the vacuum energy density comes out about {{tex:10^{120}}} times larger than the dark energy astronomers measure (Chapter 91). This is the cosmological constant problem, the worst mismatch between estimate and observation in physics, and it is unsolved. Debated

Microcausality

Chapter 55 found that a single relativistic particle leaks outside the light cone. In field theory, the quantity that decides whether a measurement here can affect one there is the commutator of the field at the two points. Compute it, and the particle and antiparticle contributions cancel exactly outside the light cone:

{{texd:[\phi(x), \phi(y)] = 0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad (x - y)^2 < 0}}

The plate shows this commutator in one space dimension at a fixed time 4.0: a Bessel function inside the cone, and exactly zero outside. This is the precise sense in which quantum field theory respects relativity, and why entanglement cannot signal (Chapter 50).

What you now have

A field as infinitely many oscillators, canonical commutators and mode expansions, particles as quanta with {{tex:E = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2}}}, wave packets, normal ordering and the cosmological constant problem, and microcausality.
