No instrument has ever displayed a wavefunction. You cannot photograph {{tex:\psi}}, or touch it, or measure it in a single shot. Yet every detector count in every quantum experiment, from the double slit of Chapter 25 to the largest particle collider, is predicted by it, and predicted correctly.

This chapter says exactly how. The wavefunction is a complex number at every point in space. Its size sets the odds of finding the particle there; its phase, which no single measurement sees, decides how waves add and cancel.

The Born rule

In 1926 Max Born proposed the rule that turns {{tex:\psi}} into predictions. The probability of finding the particle between {{tex:x}} and {{tex:x + dx}} is

{{texd:P(x)\,dx = |\psi(x)|^2\,dx}}

Because the particle must be somewhere, the total probability must be one: {{tex:\int|\psi|^2dx = 1}}. A wavefunction scaled to satisfy this is normalized. Established

Averages

With a probability distribution you can compute averages, called expectation values. The average position is {{tex:\langle x\rangle = \int x\,|\psi|^2\,dx}}, and the spread is {{tex:\Delta x = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2}}}, exactly as in Chapter 10.

For the lowest state of a particle in a box of width {{tex:L}}, {{tex:\psi = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(\pi x/L)}}, symmetry gives {{tex:\langle x\rangle = L/2}}, and integration gives {{tex:\langle x^2\rangle = L^2\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{2\pi^2}\right) \approx 0.283\,L^2}}. Expectation values are what experiments measure when they average over many identically prepared particles.

Phase you cannot see

Multiply the whole wavefunction by the same phase factor {{tex:e^{i\theta}}}. Turn it by 0 °. Every arrow on the plate turns, but {{tex:|\psi|^2}} does not change at all, and neither does any prediction. A global phase is unobservable; {{tex:\psi}} and {{tex:e^{i\theta}\psi}} are the same physical state.

Phase you can see

Now build a wavefunction from two overlapping bumps, and change only the phase of one relative to the other: 0 °. Where they overlap, the probability swells at 0° and collapses at 180°. The relative phase between parts of a wavefunction is physical, and it is what made the stripes in the double slit.

{{texd:|\psi_1 + e^{i\phi}\psi_2|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(e^{i\phi}\,\psi_1^*\psi_2\big)}}

Probability flows

Probability is conserved locally, like water: it cannot vanish here and reappear there; it has to flow in between. Schrödinger’s equation, which you will meet in Chapter 31, implies a continuity equation, {{tex:\partial_t|\psi|^2 + \partial_x j = 0}}, with a probability current

{{texd:j = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\psi^*\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)}}

For a plane wave {{tex:\psi = Ae^{ikx}}}, the current is {{tex:j = (\hbar k/m)|A|^2}}: density times velocity, with velocity {{tex:\hbar k/m = p/m}}. The phase gradient is the flow. A real wavefunction, with no phase variation, carries no current at all.

What you now have

The Born rule, normalization, expectation values, the difference between global and relative phase, and the probability current.

Why can a global phase never be measured?tap
It multiplies every amplitude by the same factor of size one, so every probability |…|² is unchanged.

The variation of the phase in space; a real wavefunction has zero current.
