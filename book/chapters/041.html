Two electrons, each spin up or down: four combinations. But nature does not treat them as four equal states. It sorts them into a group of three and a single one left over, and the energy difference between the three and the one decides whether iron is a magnet, whether two hydrogen atoms bond, and how an oxygen molecule sticks to haemoglobin. This chapter shows how angular momenta add, and where the three and the one come from.

On the plate: four two-spin states, regrouping into three plus one.

Two ways to label the same states

Combine angular momenta {{tex:j_1}} and {{tex:j_2}}. You can label the joint states by each part’s own {{tex:m_1, m_2}}, the uncoupled basis, or by the total {{tex:J}} and its component {{tex:M = m_1 + m_2}}, the coupled basis. The total runs in whole steps:

{{texd:J = |j_1 - j_2|,\ |j_1 - j_2| + 1,\ \dots,\ j_1 + j_2}}

Choose {{tex:j_1 =}} 0.5 and {{tex:j_2 =}} 0.5. The plate lays out the uncoupled states as a grid; states on the same diagonal share {{tex:M}}. Counting the diagonals shows why the totals come out as they do, and the numbers always match: {{tex:(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1) = \sum_J (2J+1)}}. Established

Building the coupled states

The recipe needs only the lowering operator of Chapter 39. The top state is unambiguous: {{tex:|J{=}j_1{+}j_2, M{=}J\rangle = |j_1, j_1\rangle|j_2, j_2\rangle}}. Apply {{tex:J_- = J_{1-} + J_{2-}}} to step down the {{tex:J = j_1 + j_2}} ladder. At each {{tex:M}}, the state orthogonal to it starts the next ladder down, {{tex:J = j_1 + j_2 - 1}}, and so on. The numbers that appear are the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, {{tex:\langle j_1 m_1; j_2 m_2|J M\rangle}}.

The plate shows the whole table for your {{tex:j_1}} and {{tex:j_2}}: rows are coupled states, columns uncoupled ones, amber positive and red negative. Most entries are zero, because {{tex:M = m_1 + m_2}}. The table is an orthogonal matrix, a change of basis (Chapter 8) and nothing more.

Three and one

For two spin-½ particles, the recipe gives

{{texd:\begin{gathered}|1,1\rangle = |{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle,\quad |1,0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle + |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\big),\quad |1,-1\rangle = |{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle \\ |0,0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\big)\end{gathered}}}

The triplet is symmetric when the particles are swapped; the singlet is antisymmetric. The singlet has total spin zero in every direction, so if you measure the two spins along the same axis, any axis, they always disagree. Measure them along axes 60° apart, and they agree with probability {{tex:\sin^2(\theta/2)}}. Neither spin has a direction of its own, yet the pair is perfectly correlated. That is entanglement, and Part Two builds Bell’s theorem on exactly this state.

Spin meets orbit

An electron moving through the nucleus’s electric field sees, in its own frame, a magnetic field, which acts on its spin. The energy is proportional to {{tex:\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S}}, which does not commute with {{tex:L_z}} or {{tex:S_z}} but does commute with {{tex:J^2}}. So the coupled basis is the right one, and the trick is

{{texd:\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = \tfrac12\left(J^2 - L^2 - S^2\right)}}

A p electron ({{tex:\ell = 1}}) can have {{tex:j = \tfrac32}} or {{tex:\tfrac12}}, at slightly different energies. In sodium this splits the famous yellow line into two, at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, about 17 cm⁻¹ apart. The plate shows the doublet. Established

The Landé g-factor

Orbital and spin angular momentum make magnetic moments with different strengths: the spin counts about twice as much ({{tex:g_s \approx 2}}). So the total moment is not parallel to {{tex:\mathbf J}}. In the vector model, {{tex:\mathbf L}} and {{tex:\mathbf S}} precess around {{tex:\mathbf J}}, and only the moment’s projection on {{tex:\mathbf J}} survives on average:

{{texd:g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}}}

Three questions to carry with you.

Why do two spin-½ particles make J = 1 and J = 0?tap
J runs from |½ − ½| = 0 to ½ + ½ = 1, and 2 × 2 = 3 + 1 states.
What makes the singlet special?tap
It has zero total spin along every axis and is antisymmetric under exchange; the two spins always disagree along a common axis.
Why is the coupled basis right for spin–orbit coupling?tap
L·S commutes with J², L² and S², not with L_z or S_z separately.
