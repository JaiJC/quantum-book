What you just saw

Electrons travel as waves and arrive as dots

In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has a wavelength. The idea sounds strange until you look at the screen you just filled. A dot is where one electron was detected. The stripes are where electrons are more and less likely to land, and that pattern is the fingerprint of a wave going through both slits and overlapping with itself.

Change the energy and the stripes squeeze together or spread apart. Faster electrons have shorter waves, and shorter waves make tighter stripes.

The rule de Broglie proposed links wavelength to momentum:

{{texd:\lambda = \frac{h}{p}}}

Here {{tex:h}} is Planck’s constant, {{tex:6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}}}, and {{tex:p}} is the momentum. For an electron with kinetic energy {{tex:E}} well below its rest energy, {{tex:p = \sqrt{2 m_e E}}}, so

{{texd:\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e E}} \approx \frac{1.226\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{E / \text{eV}}}}}

Grey dots are atoms about 0.2 nm apart, roughly the spacing in a metal crystal. Waves this short fit between atoms, which is why crystals can diffract electrons.

The stripe spacing on the screen is {{tex:\Delta y = \lambda L / d}}, where {{tex:L}} is the distance to the screen and {{tex:d}} is the gap between the slits. Double the energy and {{tex:\lambda}} shrinks by {{tex:\sqrt 2}}, so the stripes move closer by the same factor.

Deep · where the stripe spacing comes from

Take a point on the screen at height {{tex:y}}, far away ({{tex:L \gg d}}). The paths from the two slits differ in length by {{tex:d \sin\theta}}, with {{tex:\tan\theta = y/L}}. The two waves add up in step when that difference is a whole number of wavelengths:

{{texd:d \sin\theta_m = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots}}

For small angles {{tex:\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = y/L}}, so the bright bands sit at {{tex:y_m = m\lambda L/d}} and are spaced {{tex:\lambda L/d}} apart. Dark bands fall halfway between, where the path difference is {{tex:(m+\tfrac12)\lambda}}. The full intensity is

{{texd:I(y) \propto \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d y}{\lambda L}\right)\,\operatorname{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\pi a y}{\lambda L}\right)}}

where {{tex:a}} is the width of each slit and {{tex:\operatorname{sinc} x = \sin x / x}}. With the detector on, the cross term that makes the {{tex:\cos^2}} factor disappears, and only the single-slit envelope is left. The simulator above draws its dots from exactly this formula.

This is not a thought experiment. Tonomura and colleagues at Hitachi recorded the build-up one electron at a time in 1989, using an electron biprism that splits each electron’s wave in two, the way two slits do. Molecules of about 2,000 atoms have since shown the same stripes. Established

Looking ahead to qubits

Set the odds, then roll

A qubit is the simplest quantum system: it reads 0 or 1. Its state is an arrow on a sphere. Point it up and it always reads 0. Point it down and it always reads 1. Anywhere in between, each reading is random, but the odds are fixed by the angle.
