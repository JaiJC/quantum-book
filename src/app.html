One Electron at a Time

Shoot a single electron at a wall with two narrow slits in it, and see where it lands on a screen behind. That is the whole experiment. People have argued about what it means for a hundred years, and by the end of this chapter you will have run it yourself.

Look at the plate. On the left is the gun, which fires electrons one at a time. In the middle is the wall, with its two slits. On the right is the screen, which glows wherever an electron strikes it. The tall panel beyond is the same screen turned to face you, so you can watch the hits pile up.

This is the double-slit experiment. Feynman thought it held the central mystery of quantum mechanics, and that once you understood it, the rest would follow.

The strange part

I have closed the top slit. Tap to open or close it, and compare.

With one slit, the stripes vanish into a single smooth band. Now think about a spot where a dark stripe used to be. With one slit open, electrons land there. Open the second slit and fewer land there.

More ways through, fewer hits. Bullets cannot do that. Waves can.

The wave

Both slits are open again, and the plate now shows the wave. Tap to hide it or bring it back.

Each electron travels as a wave, and the wave goes through both slits. Behind the wall the two halves overlap. Where crest meets crest the wave is strong, and you get a bright stripe. Where crest meets trough the two cancel, and you get a dark one. Follow the bright rays: they end on the bright stripes.

The wave does not say where the electron is. It sets the odds of where it will land. When it reaches the screen, it still makes one dot.

NotePhysicists call this wave the wavefunction. The chance of a hit at a spot is the square of the wave’s size there. Established

Peeking

Now cheat. Put a detector beside each slit, so that every electron flashes the one it passes. are on; tap to switch them off and on.

With the detectors on, the stripes are gone, and you get the bullet answer after all: two blurred bands, overlapping. Knowing the path destroys the stripes. You can have one or the other, never both.

DeeperNo one has to look at the detector. Any physical record of the path entangles the electron with its surroundings, and the two halves of its wave can no longer interfere. This is decoherence. Established

Your turn

Faster electrons have shorter waves, and shorter waves make tighter stripes. The small marks beside the screen show where the dark stripes fall at one particular energy.

Drag this number sideways. The electrons now carry 110 eV, which gives them a wavelength of 0.117 nm. Find the energy where the dark lanes of the wave meet the marks, then to check.

The dark lanes are too close together. Lower the energy.

That is it: 54 electronvolts, and a wavelength of 0.167 nanometres. It is the energy Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer used in 1927, when they first caught electrons behaving as waves. Established

The rule behind the dial is de Broglie’s: a particle with momentum {{tex:p}} has wavelength {{tex:\lambda = h/p}}, where {{tex:h}} is Planck’s constant. More energy, more momentum, shorter wave.

Deeper

For an electron with kinetic energy {{tex:E}} far below its rest energy, {{tex:p = \sqrt{2 m_e E}}}, so

{{texd:\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e E}} \approx \frac{1.226\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{E/\text{eV}}}}}

The stripe spacing on the screen is {{tex:\Delta y \approx \lambda L / d}}, with {{tex:L}} the distance to the screen and {{tex:d}} the gap between the slits.

What you found

An electron travels as a wave, through both slits at once, and arrives as a single dot. The wave sets the odds. Watching which way it went wipes the stripes out.
