Step 1 of 10 · The setup

A gun, a wall, a screen

On the left, a gun shoots electrons, one at a time. In the middle, a wall with two narrow slits. On the right, a screen that glows wherever an electron hits it.

The tall panel at the far right is that same screen seen face on, so you can watch the hits pile up.

This is the double-slit experiment. Richard Feynman said it holds the whole mystery of quantum mechanics.

Find the gun, the two slits and the screen, then tap Next.

Step 2 of 10 · One electron

Fire a single electron

Tap “Fire one” and watch where it goes.

It hit the screen in one spot and left one dot. Whatever an electron is, it arrives like a tiny particle.

Step 3 of 10 · A few more

Fire ten

Keep tapping “Fire one” until there are 10 dots.

Each one lands somewhere different, and you can’t tell in advance where the next will go. So far it looks random.

Step 4 of 10 · Predict

What will 2,000 electrons draw?

If electrons are tiny bullets, each one goes through the top slit or the bottom slit. Make your guess before you look.

Locked in. Now let’s find out.

Step 5 of 10 · Find out

Fire 2,000 electrons

Press and hold “Hold to fire”, or tap “Fire 500” a few times. Watch the face-on screen.

You guessed two bands, the bullet answer. The screen shows stripes instead: bright, dark, bright, dark.

You guessed stripes, and stripes it is. Most people guess two bands.

You guessed one glow. The screen shows stripes instead: bright, dark, bright, dark.

Every electron still landed as a single dot. The stripes only appear when thousands are added up.

Step 6 of 10 · The strange part

Close one slit

Tap “Close top slit”, then hold to fire.

With one slit, the stripes are gone: one smooth band.

Now think about a dark stripe from before. With one slit open, electrons land there. Open a second slit and fewer land there. More ways through, fewer hits. Bullets can’t do that. Waves can.

Step 7 of 10 · Why

See the wave

Both slits are open again.

Tap “Show the wave”.

Each electron travels as a wave that passes through both slits. Behind the wall the two parts overlap.

Where crest meets crest, the wave is strong: a bright stripe. Where crest meets trough, they cancel: a dark stripe. The bright rays land on the bright stripes.

The wave only sets the odds of where the electron lands. When it reaches the screen, it still makes one dot.

Physicists call this wave the wavefunction. The chance of a hit at a spot is the square of the wave’s size there. ESTABLISHED

Step 8 of 10 · Peek

Watch which slit it uses

A detector at each slit flashes when an electron passes, so you know which way it went.

Tap “Watch the slits”, then hold to fire.

Each electron now flashes at one slit, and the stripes are gone. You get the bullet answer: two blurred bands, overlapping.

Knowing the path destroys the stripes. You can have one or the other, never both.

No human needs to look. Any physical record of the path entangles the electron with the detector, and the two parts of its wave can no longer interfere. This is called decoherence. ESTABLISHED

Step 9 of 10 · Your turn

Tune the wavelength

Faster electrons have shorter waves, and shorter waves make tighter stripes. The green marks on the screen show where the dark stripes fall at one special energy.

Change the energy until the waves’ dark lanes hit the green marks. Then fire to check.

90 eVλ = 0.129 nm

Spot on: 54 eV, a wavelength of 0.167 nm. That is the energy Davisson and Germer used in 1927, when they first saw electrons behave as waves. ESTABLISHED

De Broglie’s rule links wavelength to momentum: {{tex:\lambda = h/p}}. More energy means more momentum, so a shorter wave.

For an electron with kinetic energy {{tex:E}} far below its rest energy, {{tex:p = \sqrt{2 m_e E}}}, so

{{texd:\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e E}} \approx \frac{1.226\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{E/\text{eV}}}}}

The stripe spacing is {{tex:\Delta y \approx \lambda L / d}}, where {{tex:L}} is the distance to the screen and {{tex:d}} the gap between the slits.

Step 10 of 10 · What you found

Waves going in, dots coming out

An electron travels as a wave through both slits and lands as a single dot. The wave sets the odds. Watching the path wipes out the stripes.
